## ODEs, SDEs, and Brownian motion

An ordinary differential equation prescribes a deterministic local velocity,

$$
dx_t=f(x_t,t)\,dt.
$$

Integrating over a small interval gives

$$
x_{t+\Delta t}-x_t=\int_t^{t+\Delta t}f(x_s,s)\,ds.
$$

Once the initial state is fixed, the vector field determines the trajectory.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-6.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Ordinary differential equation and its integral form." caption="An ODE expresses a trajectory increment as an ordinary time integral of its drift. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 6." %}

A stochastic differential equation adds a noise increment,

$$
dx_t=\mu(x_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(x_t,t)\,dW_t.
$$

The drift $$\mu$$ moves the mean locally. The diffusion coefficient $$\sigma$$ scales a Wiener-process increment, so the same initial state generates a distribution over trajectories.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Stochastic differential equation and integral form." caption="An SDE combines a deterministic drift integral with a stochastic Wiener integral. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 7." %}

A Wiener process starts at zero, has independent increments, and satisfies

$$
W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal N(0,t-s)
\qquad (s<t).
$$

Its discrete approximation uses increments $$\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_k$$ with $$\epsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$$. The square-root scaling is the defining contrast with an ordinary time increment.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Wiener process properties discrete approximation and sample paths." caption="Independent Gaussian increments with variance equal to elapsed time converge to Brownian paths. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 8." %}

It is tempting to write $$dW_t=N_tdt$$, but Brownian motion is almost surely nowhere differentiable. Over $$\Delta t$$, the difference quotient has variance $$1/\Delta t$$, which diverges as the interval shrinks. A first-order linear approximation cannot describe the path.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-9.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Brownian motion as nondifferentiable white noise integral." caption="Brownian increments scale as the square root of time, so an ordinary derivative does not exist. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 9." %}

## The Itô integral depends on where the integrand is sampled

Consider the diffusion-only SDE

$$
dX_t=\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t,
\qquad
X_T-X_0=\int_0^T\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t.
$$

Ordinary Riemann or Lebesgue integration does not define this object because the integrator is not differentiable and has nonzero quadratic variation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="SDE integral and several stochastic integration conventions." caption="A stochastic integral requires a convention for evaluating the integrand on every random increment. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 11." %}

The Stieltjes-style left sum over a partition $$0=t_0<\cdots<t_n=T$$ is

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{\tau_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}),
\qquad
\tau_i\in[t_i,t_{i+1}].
$$

For Brownian integrators, different choices of $$\tau_i$$ need not converge to the same limit.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Stieltjes sum for a Brownian stochastic integral." caption="Unlike a smooth integrator, Brownian motion makes the sampling point inside each interval mathematically consequential. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 12." %}

The Itô integral chooses the left endpoint:

$$
\int_0^TY_t\,dW_t
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{t_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}).
$$

This makes the integrand adapted: the coefficient multiplying the next noise increment depends only on information already observed.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Itô left endpoint sum compared with a general Stieltjes sum." caption="The left-endpoint convention gives the Itô integral its non-anticipating interpretation. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 13." %}

The choice changes the value. For $$Y_t=W_t$$, the left sum satisfies

$$
\sum_iW_{t_i}\Delta W_i
=\frac12W_T^2-rac12\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2
\longrightarrow \frac12W_T^2-\frac12T.
$$

The correction survives because Brownian quadratic variation converges to $$T$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Itô integral of Brownian motion derived from left sums." caption="The quadratic-variation term creates the minus one-half-time correction in the Itô integral of Brownian motion. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 14." %}

Using the right endpoint gives the backward Itô integral. The same calculation changes the sign of the correction:

$$
\int_0^TW_t\,d^-W_t=\frac12W_T^2+\frac12T.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Backward Itô integral using right endpoint sums." caption="Right-endpoint evaluation adds rather than subtracts the Brownian quadratic-variation correction. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 15." %}

The Stratonovich integral uses midpoint values. Its ordinary chain-rule behavior is often convenient, while Itô's adapted convention is convenient for probabilistic calculations. The three symbols encode distinct limiting sums.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-16.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Itô backward Itô and Stratonovich endpoint conventions." caption="Left, right, and midpoint sampling define Itô, backward Itô, and Stratonovich integrals. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 16." %}

## Itô's formula

The Brownian example gives

$$
d\!\left(\frac12W_t^2\right)=W_t\,dW_t+\frac12dt.
$$

The second term is the stochastic counterpart of the second-order Taylor remainder.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-17.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Itô formula motivated by the square of Brownian motion." caption="Brownian quadratic variation turns a nominally second-order term into an order-dt correction. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 17." %}

For a smooth function $$\phi(X_t,t)$$,

$$
d\phi
=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}dt
+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}dX_t
+\frac12\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}(dX_t)^2.
$$

Since $$dW_t=O(\sqrt{dt})$$, one keeps $$(dW_t)^2=dt$$ while higher-order products vanish.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-18.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="General one dimensional Itô formula and scaling rules." caption="The second derivative survives because a squared Brownian increment has order dt. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 18." %}

Substituting $$dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t$$ gives

$$
d\phi(X_t,t)
=\left(\partial_t\phi+\mu\partial_x\phi
+\frac12\sigma^2\partial_{xx}\phi\right)dt
+\sigma\partial_x\phi\,dW_t.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Substitution of an SDE into Itô formula." caption="Drift contributes the first derivative, while diffusion variance contributes the second derivative. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 19." %}

For $$dX_t=dW_t$$ and $$\phi(x)=x^2/2$$, this reduces to the earlier identity,

$$
d\phi=W_t\,dW_t+\frac12dt.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Itô formula applied to one half Brownian motion squared." caption="The general formula recovers the explicit Brownian-square calculation. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 20." %}

The source summary places the Itô integral and Itô formula side by side: one defines how stochastic increments accumulate; the other propagates a smooth observable through those increments.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-21.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Summary of Itô integral and Itô formula." caption="The integral convention and stochastic chain rule are the two ingredients needed for density evolution. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 21." %}

## From trajectories to probability densities

Let $$p(x,t)$$ denote the marginal density of the SDE state. The Fokker--Planck--Kolmogorov equation is

$$
\partial_t p(x,t)
=-\partial_x\!\left(\mu(x,t)p(x,t)\right)
+\frac12\partial_{xx}\!\left(\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)\right).
$$

The drift transports probability, while diffusion spreads it.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Fokker Planck equation for a one dimensional SDE." caption="The trajectory-level drift and diffusion determine a deterministic density-level PDE. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 23." %}

The drift term is a probability current: the local velocity $$\mu$$ moves mass into or out of an interval. The diffusion term compares probability flow across nearby boundaries.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Drift probability current across an interval." caption="A spatial derivative of drift times density records the imbalance of deterministic probability flow. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 24." %}

The second derivative expresses the net diffusive exchange between neighboring intervals: diffusion exports mass from locally dense regions and imports it from adjacent regions.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-25.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Diffusive probability exchange between neighboring intervals." caption="A second spatial derivative represents the difference of two neighboring probability currents. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 25." %}

## The infinitesimal generator

The infinitesimal generator describes how the expectation of an observable changes over an infinitesimal step:

$$
\mathcal L\phi(x)
=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}
\frac{\mathbb E[\phi(X_{t+\Delta t})\mid X_t=x]-\phi(x)}{\Delta t}.
$$

Itô's formula computes this limit.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Definition of the infinitesimal generator." caption="The generator is the instantaneous rate of change of conditional expected observables. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 26." %}

The stochastic integral has zero conditional mean under the Itô convention. Therefore

$$
\mathcal L\phi
=\mu\,\partial_x\phi
+\frac12\sigma^2\,\partial_{xx}\phi.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Infinitesimal generator derived from Itô formula." caption="Taking conditional expectation removes the martingale increment and leaves the drift and diffusion differential operator. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 27." %}

The generator acts on observables, while the density evolves under its adjoint. The two descriptions are equivalent but live on different objects.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-28.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Generator intuition and its relation to Fokker Planck." caption="The generator describes infinitesimal observables; its adjoint describes infinitesimal probability density. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 28." %}

## Weak-form proof of the Fokker--Planck equation

Directly differentiating a density can be awkward. The proof instead tests the density against an arbitrary smooth function $$\phi$$ and shows that both sides of the proposed PDE have the same integral against every such function.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-29.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Fokker Planck equation and weak proof strategy." caption="The proof switches from pointwise density calculus to expected test functions. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 29." %}

Because $$\mathbb E[\phi(X_t)]=\int\phi(x)p(x,t)\,dx$$,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int\phi(x)p(x,t)\,dx
=\int\phi(x)\partial_t p(x,t)\,dx.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-30.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Expected test function written as an integral against density." caption="The left side of the density PDE becomes the time derivative of an expected observable. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 30." %}

On the other hand, the difference quotient of expected observables can be conditioned on the current state. The tower property converts a transition-level expression into an integral over $$p(x,t)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-31.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Conditional expectation step in the weak Fokker Planck proof." caption="Conditioning on the present state links the density calculation to the infinitesimal generator. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 31." %}

Taking the infinitesimal limit yields

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\phi(X_t)]
=\int(\mathcal L\phi)(x)p(x,t)\,dx.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-32.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Infinitesimal limit yielding the generator in the weak proof." caption="The conditional difference quotient converges to the generator acting on the test function. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 32." %}

Substituting the generator gives

$$
\int\left(\mu\,\phi'+\frac12\sigma^2\phi''\right)p\,dx.
$$

The remaining task is to move the derivatives from $$\phi$$ onto the coefficient-weighted density.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-33.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Generator substituted into weak Fokker Planck identity." caption="The weak form initially places spatial derivatives on the observable rather than the density. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 33." %}

Assuming boundary terms vanish, integration by parts gives

$$
\int\mu p\,\phi'\,dx
=-\int\phi\,\partial_x(\mu p)\,dx,
$$

and applying it twice gives

$$
\frac12\int\sigma^2p\,\phi''\,dx
=\frac12\int\phi\,\partial_{xx}(\sigma^2p)\,dx.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-34.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Integration by parts assumption in the Fokker Planck proof." caption="Vanishing boundary terms let the generator derivatives transfer to the density factors. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 34." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-35.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="First and second integration by parts in the weak proof." caption="One integration moves the drift derivative; two integrations move the diffusion derivative. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 35." %}

Thus

$$
\int\phi\,\partial_t p\,dx
=\int\phi\left[-\partial_x(\mu p)
+\frac12\partial_{xx}(\sigma^2p)\right]dx.
$$

Since the equality holds for every suitable $$\phi$$, the two integrands agree, proving the Fokker--Planck equation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Completion of the weak Fokker Planck proof." caption="Equality against every test function identifies the density's drift--diffusion evolution equation. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 36." %}

## What the one-dimensional derivation leaves open

The multidimensional generalization replaces scalar derivatives with gradients, divergences, Hessians, and a diffusion matrix. The source also points to Girsanov's theorem for changing SDE measures, Feynman--Kac for PDE representations, and Doob's $$h$$-transform for conditioned dynamics.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-13/slide-37.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Multidimensional extension and related stochastic calculus concepts." caption="The closing slide locates the one-dimensional derivation within the wider stochastic-calculus toolkit. Cropped from the 2025 GDL stochastic-calculus lecture, slide 37." %}

The source's chain is now complete. Brownian scaling makes ordinary integration insufficient. The Itô convention fixes the stochastic integral. Itô's formula identifies the generator. The generator controls expected observables. Its adjoint, revealed by integration by parts, controls the probability density. Diffusion and flow models use different pieces of this chain, but the stochastic calculus establishes how their trajectory and density descriptions meet.
