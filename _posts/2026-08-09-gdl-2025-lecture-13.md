An SDE and its Fokker--Planck equation describe the same process at two different levels. The SDE describes random sample paths. The Fokker--Planck equation describes the deterministic evolution of their probability density. The bridge between them is the infinitesimal generator: Itô's formula tells us how the generator acts on smooth observables, and integration by parts transfers that action to the density.

That sentence compresses nearly all of stochastic calculus needed for diffusion and flow models. The details matter because Brownian paths are not differentiable, their squared increments do not vanish, and the value of a stochastic integral depends on where its integrand is sampled. We will build the bridge in its mathematical order: first trajectories, then stochastic integration, then observables, and finally densities.

Keep the levels separate from the beginning. A realization $$t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$$ is one random curve indexed by an outcome $$\omega$$. The marginal density $$p(x,t)$$ discards the identity of that curve and records only the distribution of its position at a fixed time. An observable $$\phi$$ converts a state into a scalar, and $$\mathbb E[\phi(X_t)]$$ averages that scalar over all realizations. The generator tells us how this last quantity changes locally. None of these objects is interchangeable with another, even when they are written using the same variables $$x$$ and $$t$$.

This distinction also explains the direction of the derivation. We cannot differentiate the Brownian sample path and then average the result. We first define integration along the path, use that definition to obtain a valid chain rule for smooth observables, and only then take expectations. The density equation appears at the end as the unique evolution consistent with all of those expected observables.

## ODEs, SDEs, and Brownian motion

### An ODE determines one trajectory

An ordinary differential equation prescribes the local velocity of a state $$x_t\in\mathbb R$$:

$$
dx_t=f(x_t,t)\,dt.
$$

The differential notation is shorthand for an integral equation. Over the interval from $$t$$ to $$t+\Delta t$$,

$$
x_{t+\Delta t}-x_t
=\int_t^{t+\Delta t} f(x_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

Once the initial value and vector field are fixed, this equation determines a trajectory under the usual existence and uniqueness conditions. A numerical solver approximates the integral. For example, the forward Euler step replaces the changing velocity inside the interval by its value at the left endpoint:

$$
x_{t+\Delta t}\approx x_t+f(x_t,t)\Delta t.
$$

The approximation error shrinks as the time step is refined because an ordinary differentiable trajectory has an increment of order $$\Delta t$$. This ordinary scaling will fail as soon as Brownian noise enters.

The integral form also identifies what the differential equation asserts locally. Dividing by $$\Delta t$$ gives

$$
\frac{x_{t+\Delta t}-x_t}{\Delta t}
=\frac{1}{\Delta t}
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}f(x_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

If the integrand is continuous, the right side converges to $$f(x_t,t)$$. Ordinary differential notation is therefore backed by an ordinary derivative. This fact is so familiar that it is easy to assume the same interpretation for an SDE. Brownian scaling will show exactly where that assumption breaks.

### An SDE determines a distribution over trajectories

A one-dimensional stochastic differential equation adds a random increment:

$$
dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t.
$$

The drift coefficient $$\mu(X_t,t)\in\mathbb R$$ supplies deterministic motion. The diffusion coefficient $$\sigma(X_t,t)\in\mathbb R$$ scales the random motion. The Wiener process $$W_t$$, also called Brownian motion, supplies the noise. Capitalizing $$X_t$$ emphasizes that the state is now random.

The SDE is again an integral equation:

$$
X_{t+\Delta t}-X_t
=\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\mu(X_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau
+\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\sigma(X_\tau,\tau)\,dW_\tau.
$$

The first integral is ordinary. The second is not. We still need to say what it means to integrate against $$dW_\tau$$. That definition is the main reason for introducing Itô calculus rather than treating an SDE as an ODE with a noisy derivative.

Even before defining the integral, a small-step approximation reveals the two scales:

$$
X_{t+\Delta t}-X_t
\approx \mu(X_t,t)\Delta t
+\sigma(X_t,t)\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon,
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1).
$$

The drift increment is order $$\Delta t$$, whereas the random increment is order $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$. Over one very small step, the random increment is therefore larger in magnitude. Its sign fluctuates, however, so accumulated drift and accumulated noise both remain visible over a finite time interval.

Conditioned on the current state, the approximation has moments

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[X_{t+\Delta t}-X_t\mid X_t]
&\approx \mu(X_t,t)\Delta t,\\
\operatorname{Var}(X_{t+\Delta t}-X_t\mid X_t)
&\approx \sigma^2(X_t,t)\Delta t.
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficients therefore have distinct statistical meanings. The drift controls the first conditional moment per unit time. The squared diffusion coefficient controls the second conditional moment per unit time. Those same two coefficients will reappear as the first- and second-derivative terms of both the generator and the Fokker--Planck equation.

### Brownian motion is a continuous random-walk limit

A standard Wiener process satisfies three defining properties:

1. It starts at the origin: $$W_0=0$$.
2. Its increment over an interval is Gaussian:

   $$
   W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal N(0,t-s),\qquad 0\le s<t.
   $$

3. Increments over non-overlapping time intervals are independent.

The properties determine more than isolated increments. For $$0\le s\le t$$,

$$
W_t=W_s+(W_t-W_s),
$$

and the two terms on the right are independent. Therefore

$$
\mathbb E[W_t]=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(W_t)=t,
\qquad
\mathbb E[W_sW_t]=s=\min(s,t).
$$

The covariance remembers the shared portion of the two paths up to time $$s$$. After time $$s$$, the new increment is independent. This combination of temporal dependence and independent future increments is what makes Brownian motion a useful driving process for a Markovian SDE.

The variance equals elapsed time, so the standard deviation over an interval of length $$\Delta t$$ is $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$. A discrete approximation on the grid $$t_k=k/n$$ is therefore

$$
W_{t_k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_i,
\qquad
\epsilon_i\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,1).
$$

Equivalently, each step is

$$
W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}=\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_k,
\qquad \Delta t=\frac{1}{n}.
$$

The factor $$1/\sqrt n$$ is essential. Without it, the variance after $$n$$ steps would grow like $$n$$. Dividing each step by $$\sqrt n$$ keeps the variance at time one equal to one while the grid becomes finer. The continuous limit gives Brownian motion.

Indeed, at time $$t_k=k/n$$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(W_{t_k})
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_i)
=\frac{k}{n}=t_k.
$$

The same calculation gives variance $$t_j-t_i$$ for the increment between two grid points. Disjoint increments depend on disjoint sets of $$\epsilon_i$$ and are independent. The discrete construction therefore already contains the three Wiener properties; the limit supplies a continuous path.

Brownian motion is continuous but almost surely nowhere differentiable. To see why, inspect the difference quotient:

$$
\frac{W_{t+\Delta t}-W_t}{\Delta t}
\sim \mathcal N\!\left(0,\frac{1}{\Delta t}\right).
$$

Its variance diverges as $$\Delta t\to0$$. The path does not settle onto a finite tangent. Writing $$dW_t=N_tdt$$ would require a conventional derivative $$N_t=dW_t/dt$$, but no such ordinary random variable exists.

The same scaling explains why local linearization fails. A differentiable path with velocity $$w(t)$$ has increment $$w(t)\Delta t$$, whose magnitude is order $$\Delta t$$. A Brownian increment has magnitude order $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$. The ratio $$\sqrt{\Delta t}/\Delta t=1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$$ diverges. Brownian motion is not a smooth curve perturbed by a small first-order error; its roughness dominates at the smallest scales.

There is a useful contrast between signed and squared increments. Over a partition, the signed increments telescope:

$$
\sum_i\Delta W_i=W_T-W_0.
$$

Their absolute values grow without stabilizing as the partition is refined, which is another symptom of infinite path variation. Their squares, however, approach a finite deterministic limit. It is this quadratic variation—not an ordinary derivative—that supplies the second-order bookkeeping of Itô calculus.

## The stochastic integral depends on the sampling point

### Ordinary Stieltjes intuition is insufficient

Consider the diffusion-only SDE

$$
dX_t=Y_t\,dW_t,
\qquad
X_T-X_0=\int_0^T Y_t\,dW_t.
$$

In an SDE, $$Y_t$$ is often $$\sigma(X_t,t)$$, so the coefficient can depend on the random state. Start with a partition

$$
0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=T
$$

and choose one point $$t_i^*\in[t_i,t_{i+1}]$$ in every interval. A Stieltjes-style sum is

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{t_i^*}
\bigl(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\bigr).
$$

For a smooth integrator, changing $$t_i^*$$ only changes a higher-order error, and all reasonable choices converge to the same integral. Brownian motion behaves differently. The increment is order $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$, and its correlation with the chosen value of $$Y$$ can leave an order-one contribution after summing over all intervals. The sampling convention is therefore part of the definition.

The point is not that stochastic integrals never converge. It is that the symbol $$\int Y_t\,dW_t$$ is incomplete until we specify which limiting sum we mean.

The failure can be seen from a single interval. Moving the evaluation point from the left endpoint to the right changes the product by

$$
\bigl(Y_{t_{i+1}}-Y_{t_i}\bigr)\Delta W_i.
$$

When $$Y_t$$ itself depends on Brownian motion, its increment is generally order $$\sqrt{\Delta t_i}$$. The product is then order $$\Delta t_i$$. Summing roughly $$T/\Delta t$$ such products can produce an order-$$T$$ difference. For ordinary smooth functions, the change in $$Y$$ would instead be order $$\Delta t_i$$, the product would be of higher order, and the choice would disappear in the limit.

The stochastic integral thus records a temporal convention. Left-endpoint evaluation says that the coefficient is chosen using the present and past before the next random shock arrives. Right-endpoint evaluation lets the coefficient depend on that shock. Midpoint evaluation symmetrizes the two. Their differences are invisible for finite-variation integrators but measurable for Brownian motion.

### Itô, backward Itô, and Stratonovich conventions

The Itô integral evaluates the integrand before observing the next Brownian increment:

$$
\int_0^T Y_t\,dW_t
:=\lim_{\lVert\Pi\rVert\to0}
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{t_i}
\bigl(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\bigr).
$$

Here $$\lVert\Pi\rVert$$ is the largest interval length in the partition. If $$Y_{t_i}$$ depends only on information available up to time $$t_i$$, then it is independent of the future increment conditional on the present. This adapted, non-anticipating structure gives

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[
Y_{t_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i})
\mid \mathcal F_{t_i}
\right]=0,
$$

and under the standard square-integrability conditions,

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\int_0^T Y_t\,dW_t\right]=0.
$$

The zero-mean property will later remove the stochastic term when we derive the infinitesimal generator.

To make the conditioning explicit, let $$\mathcal F_{t_i}$$ contain all information observed up to $$t_i$$. Adaptedness means that $$Y_{t_i}$$ is $$\mathcal F_{t_i}$$-measurable. Independent Brownian increments then give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[Y_{t_i}\Delta W_i]
&=\mathbb E\!\left[
\mathbb E[Y_{t_i}\Delta W_i\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}]
\right]\\
&=\mathbb E\!\left[
Y_{t_i}\mathbb E[\Delta W_i\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}]
\right]=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every finite Itô sum has zero mean. Passing to the stochastic-integral limit preserves that property under the usual integrability assumptions. This calculation is the operational benefit of choosing the left endpoint: the coefficient can be random without anticipating the increment it multiplies.

The backward Itô integral evaluates at the right endpoint:

$$
\int_0^T Y_t\,d^-W_t
:=\lim_{\lVert\Pi\rVert\to0}
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{t_{i+1}}
\bigl(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\bigr).
$$

The Stratonovich integral uses the midpoint average:

$$
\int_0^T Y_t\circ dW_t
:=\lim_{\lVert\Pi\rVert\to0}
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
\frac{Y_{t_i}+Y_{t_{i+1}}}{2}
\bigl(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\bigr).
$$

These are different integrals, not three numerical approximations to one hidden value. The Itô convention is natural for conditional expectation and martingale calculations. Stratonovich integration obeys the ordinary chain rule and often appears when smooth noise is idealized as rapidly fluctuating noise. Backward Itô integration becomes useful when a stochastic process is described in reverse time.

More generally, choosing the convex evaluation point

$$
Y_{t_i}^{(\alpha)}
=(1-\alpha)Y_{t_i}+\alpha Y_{t_{i+1}}
$$

interpolates between Itô at $$\alpha=0$$, Stratonovich at $$\alpha=1/2$$, and backward Itô at $$\alpha=1$$. For a Brownian-driven integrand, changing $$\alpha$$ changes the drift correction. A stochastic differential equation therefore needs both its coefficients and its integration convention. The same written symbols can describe different processes if that convention is left implicit.

### The integral of Brownian motion exposes the difference

Set $$Y_t=W_t$$. For the Itô convention, write $$\Delta W_i=W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}$$ and use

$$
W_{t_i}\Delta W_i
=\frac12\left(W_{t_{i+1}}^2-W_{t_i}^2\right)
-\frac12(\Delta W_i)^2.
$$

Summing gives

$$
\sum_i W_{t_i}\Delta W_i
=\frac12\left(W_T^2-W_0^2\right)
-\frac12\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2.
$$

The first sum telescopes. The second is the quadratic variation of Brownian motion. Since independent increments satisfy

$$
\mathbb E[(\Delta W_i)^2]=\Delta t_i,
$$

their accumulated squares converge to the interval length:

$$
\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2\longrightarrow T.
$$

For a uniform partition with step $$\Delta t=T/n$$, this convergence can be checked directly. Each increment is $$\mathcal N(0,\Delta t)$$, so

$$
\mathbb E[(\Delta W_i)^2]=\Delta t,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}((\Delta W_i)^2)=2(\Delta t)^2.
$$

Independence gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2\right]&=n\Delta t=T,\\
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2\right)
&=2n(\Delta t)^2=\frac{2T^2}{n}\longrightarrow0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the sum converges to $$T$$ in mean square. The randomness of individual squared increments averages away, while their total remains finite.

Therefore

$$
\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t
=\frac12W_T^2-\frac12W_0^2-\frac12T.
$$

For standard Brownian motion $$W_0=0$$, but retaining the initial value makes the telescoping structure explicit.

The backward Itô sum uses the other algebraic identity,

$$
W_{t_{i+1}}\Delta W_i
=\frac12\left(W_{t_{i+1}}^2-W_{t_i}^2\right)
+\frac12(\Delta W_i)^2,
$$

and hence

$$
\int_0^T W_t\,d^-W_t
=\frac12W_T^2-\frac12W_0^2+\frac12T.
$$

The two answers differ by $$T$$, exactly the quadratic variation. Midpoint averaging cancels the two corrections:

$$
\int_0^T W_t\circ dW_t
=\frac12W_T^2-\frac12W_0^2.
$$

This is why Stratonovich calculus recovers the familiar chain-rule answer for this example. The Itô correction is not a finite-grid artifact. It survives the continuous limit because there are order $$1/\Delta t$$ increments, each with a square of order $$\Delta t$$.

The example also explains why replacing $$dW_t$$ by a formal white-noise derivative is dangerous. An ordinary calculus manipulation would predict

$$
\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t
=\frac12(W_T^2-W_0^2)
$$

without specifying a convention. That value is Stratonovich, not Itô. The missing terms are exactly $$-T/2$$ for the left-endpoint convention and $$+T/2$$ for the right-endpoint convention. Stochastic calculus does not merely attach rigor to an intuitive integral; it distinguishes limits that ordinary notation collapses.

## Itô's formula is a second-order chain rule

### Why a second-order term survives

Rearranging the Itô result yields

$$
d\!\left(\frac12W_t^2\right)
=W_t\,dW_t+\frac12dt.
$$

Ordinary calculus would contain only the first term. The extra $$dt/2$$ comes from the second-order Taylor remainder. Brownian motion forces us to retain that remainder because $$dW_t$$ is order $$\sqrt{dt}$$ and hence $$(dW_t)^2$$ is order $$dt$$.

For a smooth scalar function $$\phi(X_t,t)$$, expand to second order:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\phi(X_t,t)
={}&\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\,dt
+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\,dX_t
+\frac12\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}\,(dX_t)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The displayed expansion suppresses terms that are too small to survive. The full second-order Taylor expansion contains

$$
\frac12\partial_{tt}\phi\,(dt)^2
+\partial_{tx}\phi\,dt\,dX_t
+\frac12\partial_{xx}\phi\,(dX_t)^2.
$$

Since $$dX_t$$ contains an order-$$\sqrt{dt}$$ Brownian part, the three terms scale as $$dt^2$$, $$dt^{3/2}$$, and $$dt$$, respectively. Only the last remains at first order in elapsed time. This is why Itô's formula is second order in space but only first order in time.

The stochastic multiplication rules keep precisely the terms of order $$dt$$:

$$
(dW_t)^2=dt,
\qquad
dt\,dW_t=dW_t\,dt=0,
\qquad
(dt)^2=0.
$$

These symbols summarize limiting quadratic-variation statements rather than ordinary algebra on infinitesimals. For example, $$(dW_t)^2=dt$$ means that accumulated squared Brownian increments converge to elapsed time. A cross-product such as $$dt\,dW_t$$ has order $$dt^{3/2}$$ and disappears after summation in the limit.

### Substituting the SDE

Now let

$$
dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t.
$$

Its square is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(dX_t)^2
={}&\mu^2(dt)^2
+2\mu\sigma\,dt\,dW_t
+\sigma^2(dW_t)^2\\
={}&\sigma^2(X_t,t)\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$

Writing every term makes the scale separation clear:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu^2(dt)^2&=o(dt),\\
2\mu\sigma\,dt\,dW_t&=o(dt),\\
\sigma^2(dW_t)^2&=\sigma^2dt.
\end{aligned}
$$

The drift does not contribute to quadratic variation because a bounded-variation increment is too small after squaring. The diffusion contribution survives because its increment is already of square-root order. This gives a quick way to anticipate the generator: drift will produce a first derivative, and diffusion covariance will produce a second derivative.

Substituting into the Taylor expansion gives Itô's formula:

> **Itô's formula.** For a sufficiently smooth function $$\phi(x,t)$$,
>
> $$
> d\phi(X_t,t)
> =\left(
> \partial_t\phi
> +\mu\,\partial_x\phi
> +\frac12\sigma^2\,\partial_{xx}\phi
> \right)dt
> +\sigma\,\partial_x\phi\,dW_t.
> $$
>
> The coefficients and derivatives on the right are evaluated at $$(X_t,t)$$.
{: .block-lemma }

The drift couples to the first spatial derivative because it produces an order-$$dt$$ displacement. The diffusion variance couples to the second derivative because Brownian increments have a non-vanishing quadratic variation. The coefficient is $$\sigma^2$$, not $$\sigma$$, because variance—not signed displacement—survives in the second-order term.

The earlier example is recovered by taking $$X_t=W_t$$, so $$\mu=0$$ and $$\sigma=1$$, and choosing $$\phi(x)=x^2/2$$. Since $$\partial_x\phi=x$$ and $$\partial_{xx}\phi=1$$,

$$
d\phi(W_t)=W_t\,dW_t+\frac12dt.
$$

Integrating from zero to $$T$$ gives

$$
\frac12W_T^2-\frac12W_0^2
=\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t+\frac12T,
$$

or

$$
\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t
=\frac12W_T^2-\frac12W_0^2-\frac12T.
$$

The general formula therefore reproduces the endpoint-sum calculation exactly. The first calculation starts from the definition of the stochastic integral and discovers the correction. The second starts from the stochastic chain rule and recovers the same integral. Their agreement is a useful consistency check.

For a time-dependent observable, $$\partial_t\phi$$ accounts for explicit change even if the state does not move. The terms $$\mu\partial_x\phi$$ and $$(\sigma^2/2)\partial_{xx}\phi$$ account for change induced by the state dynamics. The remaining term $$\sigma\partial_x\phi\,dW_t$$ retains the pathwise randomness. Taking expectations will remove this martingale term but retain the three deterministic rates.

The integral and the formula play complementary roles. The Itô integral defines how a stochastic coefficient accumulates along a Brownian path. Itô's formula propagates a smooth observable through that stochastic path. The formula is what lets us stop asking for the nonexistent derivative of $$X_t$$ and instead ask how well-behaved functions of $$X_t$$ change.

## From trajectory dynamics to probability flow

### The Fokker--Planck equation

Let $$p(x,t)$$ denote the density of $$X_t$$. For the SDE

$$
dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t,
$$

the density evolves according to the Fokker--Planck--Kolmogorov equation

$$
\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\left[\mu(x,t)p(x,t)\right]
+\frac12\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}
\left[\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)\right].
$$

This is a deterministic PDE even though each realization of the SDE is random. The first term transports probability according to the drift. The second term redistributes probability through local random motion.

Two limiting cases isolate the roles. If $$\sigma=0$$, the SDE reduces to an ODE and the density obeys

$$
\partial_t p=-\partial_x(\mu p),
$$

the one-dimensional continuity equation. Probability moves but does not spread randomly. If $$\mu=0$$ and $$\sigma$$ is constant, then

$$
\partial_t p=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\partial_{xx}p,
$$

the heat equation. An initially concentrated density is smoothed by Brownian motion. The full Fokker--Planck equation superposes these two mechanisms while allowing both coefficients to depend on position and time.

The equation can be written as a conservation law,

$$
\partial_t p(x,t)=-\partial_x J(x,t),
$$

with the conventional rightward probability current

$$
J(x,t)
=\mu(x,t)p(x,t)
-\frac12\partial_x\!\left[\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)\right].
$$

The signs depend on whether $$J$$ is defined as rightward outgoing current or leftward incoming current. The conservation statement does not: density in a small interval $${[x,x+dx]}$$ changes by current entering at the left boundary minus current leaving at the right boundary,

$$
\partial_t p(x,t)\,dx
\approx J(x,t)-J(x+dx,t)
\approx-\partial_xJ(x,t)\,dx.
$$

Dividing by $$dx$$ and taking the spatial limit produces $$\partial_t p=-\partial_xJ$$. Integrating the conservation law over the whole real line gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb R}p(x,t)\,dx
=-J(+\infty,t)+J(-\infty,t).
$$

When the boundary current vanishes, total probability remains one. The boundary assumption that preserves mass here will reappear as the condition that removes boundary terms during integration by parts.

The drift contribution $$J_{\mathrm{drift}}=\mu p$$ is density times velocity. If $$\mu>0$$, probability moves to the right; if $$\mu<0$$, it moves to the left. Spatial variation in this current creates local accumulation or depletion.

The diffusion current

$$
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
=-\frac12\partial_x(\sigma^2p)
$$

points down the gradient of $$\sigma^2p$$. For constant $$\sigma$$, it carries mass away from high-density regions and toward low-density regions. One useful picture is a pair of neighboring repulsive drifts: every local packet sends probability in both directions, but unequal neighboring packets leave a net flow from the denser side. Taking the difference of these local flows produces a second spatial derivative in the density equation.

When $$\sigma$$ varies with position, diffusion is controlled by the product $$\sigma^2p$$ rather than by density alone. A region can export a large diffusive current because it contains many particles, because each particle receives larger random kicks, or both. This is why the Fokker--Planck term is

$$
\frac12\partial_{xx}(\sigma^2p)
$$

rather than $$(\sigma^2/2)\partial_{xx}p$$ in general. Pulling $$\sigma^2$$ outside the derivative is valid only for spatially constant diffusion.

The current representation also resolves the sign. A positive rightward drift produces positive $$J_{\mathrm{drift}}$$. If the current is larger at the right boundary than at the left, the interval loses probability, hence the minus sign in $$-\partial_xJ$$. One may instead define an incoming current with the opposite sign, but both the current definition and the conservation law must change together.

This current picture explains the PDE, but it does not yet derive it from the SDE. For that we need an operator that converts path-level increments into rates of change of observables.

## The infinitesimal generator acts on observables

### Definition through conditional expectation

Pathwise differentiation is ill-defined for an SDE, but the conditional expectation of a smooth observable has a well-defined infinitesimal rate. For a smooth function $$\phi$$, define

$$
(\mathcal L_t\phi)(x)
=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow0}
\frac{
\mathbb E[\phi(X_{t+\Delta t})\mid X_t=x]-\phi(x)
}{\Delta t}.
$$

The infinitesimal generator $$\mathcal L_t$$ acts on functions, not on sample paths. It asks: if the process is currently at $$x$$, at what instantaneous rate does the conditional expectation of $$\phi(X_t)$$ change?

For a deterministic ODE, the same definition yields

$$
(\mathcal L_t\phi)(x)=f(x,t)\phi'(x).
$$

The generator differentiates $$\phi$$ along the vector field. An SDE adds the curvature term generated by the conditional variance. Seen this way, the stochastic generator is not an unrelated object: it extends the ordinary directional derivative by the one second-order term forced by Brownian quadratic variation.

Different choices of $$\phi$$ probe different statistics. Taking $$\phi(x)=x$$ probes the local mean. Taking $$\phi(x)=x^2$$ probes the second moment. A smooth approximation to an indicator of a set probes how probability moves into or out of that set. An actual indicator $$\mathbf 1_A(x)$$ would be especially direct, because

$$
\mathbb E[\mathbf 1_A(X_t)]=\mathbb P(X_t\in A).
$$

But the indicator is discontinuous, so the derivatives required by Itô's formula do not exist at the boundary of $$A$$. Smooth test functions retain the probability-probing idea while making the calculation legal.

### Itô's formula computes the generator

For a time-independent observable $$\phi(x)$$, Itô's formula gives

$$
d\phi(X_t)
=\left(
\mu(X_t,t)\phi'(X_t)
+\frac12\sigma^2(X_t,t)\phi''(X_t)
\right)dt
+\sigma(X_t,t)\phi'(X_t)\,dW_t.
$$

Integrate over a short interval:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(X_{t+\Delta t})-\phi(X_t)
={}&\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\left(\mu\phi'+\frac12\sigma^2\phi''\right)(X_s,s)\,ds\\
&+\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\sigma(X_s,s)\phi'(X_s)\,dW_s.
\end{aligned}
$$

Conditioning on $$X_t=x$$ kills the mean of the second integral. Divide by $$\Delta t$$. Under continuity, the average of the first integrand over the shrinking interval converges to its value at $$(x,t)$$. This gives the generator formula rather than merely suggesting it through symbolic division by $$dt$$.

Condition on $$X_t=x$$ and take expectations. The Itô stochastic integral has zero conditional mean because its coefficient is evaluated before the future Brownian increment. Dividing by $$dt$$ leaves

$$
(\mathcal L_t\phi)(x)
=\mu(x,t)\frac{\partial\phi(x)}{\partial x}
+\frac12\sigma^2(x,t)
\frac{\partial^2\phi(x)}{\partial x^2}.
$$

The generator packages the drift and diffusion into a differential operator. It moves between three objects that should not be conflated:

- The coefficients $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma$$ parameterize the path-level SDE.
- The operator $$\mathcal L_t$$ acts on smooth observables $$\phi$$.
- The adjoint operator $$\mathcal L_t^*$$ acts on densities $$p$$.

The Fokker--Planck equation will be $$\partial_t p=\mathcal L_t^*p$$. The remaining work is to justify the adjoint relation and compute $$\mathcal L_t^*$$.

Simple observables verify the interpretation. For $$\phi(x)=x$$,

$$
(\mathcal L_t\phi)(x)=\mu(x,t),
$$

so the generator returns the instantaneous conditional mean increment. For $$\phi(x)=x^2$$,

$$
(\mathcal L_t\phi)(x)=2x\mu(x,t)+\sigma^2(x,t).
$$

The first term changes the squared state through deterministic motion; the second increases it through noise. These are exactly the first two local moment rates identified from the small-step SDE. The operator summarizes those rates for every smooth observable at once.

## The weak-form proof of the Fokker--Planck equation

### Test the unknown density against a smooth function

Suppose $$X_t$$ has density $$p(x,t)$$. For an arbitrary smooth test function $$\phi$$,

$$
\mathbb E[\phi(X_t)]
=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x)p(x,t)\,dx.
$$

Differentiating the density representation gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\phi(X_t)]
=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x)
\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}\,dx.
$$

This step is formal if written without assumptions. The weak formulation avoids demanding a pointwise derivative of $$p$$ at the start. We choose $$\phi$$ smooth and compactly supported, interpret the time derivative through the integrated quantity, and derive an identity that remains meaningful even when the density has limited regularity. If a classical smooth density exists, the same identity reduces to the familiar pointwise PDE.

The use of arbitrary $$\phi$$ replaces the failed indicator-function idea from the generator section. An indicator would measure the probability of one set exactly, but its discontinuity prevents the required differentiation. Smooth test functions measure softened regions. Allowing every such function provides enough probes to determine the density evolution without differentiating a sharp set boundary.

The generator gives a second expression for the same derivative:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\phi(X_t)]
=\mathbb E[(\mathcal L_t\phi)(X_t)]
=\int_{\mathbb R}(\mathcal L_t\phi)(x)p(x,t)\,dx.
$$

This identity can be derived directly from the transition density, which makes the difference between a fixed integration variable $$x$$ and the random state $$X_t$$ explicit. Let $$q(x_2,t_2\mid x_1,t_1)$$ be the transition density. Over a short interval $$t_1<t_2$$,

First marginalize the later state:

$$
p(x_2,t_2)
=\int_{\mathbb R}
q(x_2,t_2\mid x_1,t_1)p(x_1,t_1)\,dx_1.
$$

Multiplying by $$\phi(x_2)$$ and integrating over $$x_2$$ gives

$$
\mathbb E[\phi(X_{t_2})]
=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}
\phi(x_2)q(x_2,t_2\mid x_1,t_1)
p(x_1,t_1)\,dx_2\,dx_1.
$$

The inner integral is $$\mathbb E[\phi(X_{t_2})\mid X_{t_1}=x_1]$$. The outer integral averages that conditional expectation over the current state. Subtracting the corresponding expression at $$t_1$$ produces

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{
\mathbb E[\phi(X_{t_2})]-\mathbb E[\phi(X_{t_1})]
}{t_2-t_1}\\
&=\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}
\int_{\mathbb R}
\left[
\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x_2)
q(x_2,t_2\mid x_1,t_1)\,dx_2
-\phi(x_1)
\right]
p(x_1,t_1)\,dx_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

For each fixed starting point $$x_1$$, the expression in brackets is the conditional expected change of $$\phi$$. Dividing by the elapsed time and sending $$t_2\downarrow t_1$$ turns it into $$(\mathcal L_{t_1}\phi)(x_1)$$. Thus

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x)\partial_t p(x,t)\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R}(\mathcal L_t\phi)(x)p(x,t)\,dx.
$$

This is the weak evolution equation. It tracks the density through every smooth observable rather than differentiating the density pointwise from the outset.

The fixed symbols and random symbols now have precise roles. The variables $$x_1$$ and $$x_2$$ are integration coordinates. The random variables $$X_{t_1}$$ and $$X_{t_2}$$ select positions according to the process. The transition density connects the two coordinate systems, while the tower property connects conditional and marginal expectations. Mixing $$x$$ with $$X_t$$ obscures this step and makes the generator look as though it acts directly on the density. It does not; the adjoint will perform that action.

### Move the generator from the observable to the density

Insert the generator:

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}(\mathcal L_t\phi)p\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R}\mu p\,\phi'\,dx
+\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}\sigma^2p\,\phi''\,dx.
$$

Assume the test function has compact support, or that the products decay sufficiently quickly at the boundary. One integration by parts transfers the derivative in the drift term:

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\mu p\,\phi'\,dx
=-\int_{\mathbb R}
\phi\,\partial_x(\mu p)\,dx.
$$

The boundary contribution $$[\mu p\phi]_{-\infty}^{\infty}$$ vanishes under the stated assumption.

The diffusion term requires two integrations by parts. First,

$$
\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}\sigma^2p\,\phi''\,dx
=-\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}
\partial_x(\sigma^2p)\,\phi'\,dx,
$$

and then

$$
-\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}
\partial_x(\sigma^2p)\,\phi'\,dx
=\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}
\phi\,\partial_{xx}(\sigma^2p)\,dx.
$$

The first derivative changes sign once. The second derivative changes sign twice and therefore appears with a plus sign. Combining the two terms gives

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}(\mathcal L_t\phi)p\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x)
\left[
-\partial_x(\mu p)
+\frac12\partial_{xx}(\sigma^2p)
\right]dx.
$$

The bracket defines the adjoint action

$$
(\mathcal L_t^*p)(x)
=-\partial_x\!\left[\mu(x,t)p(x,t)\right]
+\frac12\partial_{xx}\!\left[\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)\right].
$$

The word *adjoint* refers to the integral pairing

$$
\langle \mathcal L_t\phi,p\rangle
=\langle\phi,\mathcal L_t^*p\rangle,
\qquad
\langle u,v\rangle
:=\int_{\mathbb R}u(x)v(x)\,dx.
$$

It does not mean that $$\mathcal L_t$$ and $$\mathcal L_t^*$$ have the same pointwise formula. The generator differentiates the test function. Its adjoint differentiates coefficient-weighted density. Integration by parts is the operation that changes one formula into the other.

We have therefore shown

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x)\partial_t p(x,t)\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x)(\mathcal L_t^*p)(x,t)\,dx
$$

for every suitable test function $$\phi$$. Equality against every test function implies equality in the weak sense:

$$
\boxed{
\partial_t p(x,t)
=-\partial_x\!\left[\mu(x,t)p(x,t)\right]
+\frac12\partial_{xx}\!\left[\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)\right]
}.
$$

To see why the last implication is justified, define the residual

$$
r(x,t)=\partial_t p(x,t)-(\mathcal L_t^*p)(x,t).
$$

The derivation shows $$\int\phi(x)r(x,t)\,dx=0$$ for every smooth compactly supported $$\phi$$. If the residual were positive or negative on a region of nonzero measure, one could choose a nonnegative test function supported inside that region and obtain a nonzero integral. Hence $$r=0$$ as a distribution. When all terms are continuous, this is also pointwise equality.

The proof has moved through the objects in the order required by the stochastic process. Brownian increments define the Itô integral. The integral supports Itô's formula. Itô's formula gives the generator on observables. Conditional expectation connects those observables to the marginal density. Integration by parts produces the adjoint generator, which is the Fokker--Planck operator.

## Beyond the one-dimensional derivation

For a process $$\mathbf X_t\in\mathbb R^d$$,

$$
d\mathbf X_t
=\boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf X_t,t)\,dt
+\boldsymbol\Sigma(\mathbf X_t,t)\,d\mathbf W_t,
$$

define the diffusion matrix

$$
\mathbf a(\mathbf x,t)
=\boldsymbol\Sigma(\mathbf x,t)
\boldsymbol\Sigma(\mathbf x,t)^\top.
$$

The generator becomes

$$
(\mathcal L_t\phi)(\mathbf x)
=\sum_i\mu_i(\mathbf x,t)\partial_i\phi(\mathbf x)
+\frac12\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}(\mathbf x,t)
\partial_i\partial_j\phi(\mathbf x),
$$

and its adjoint gives

$$
\partial_t p(\mathbf x,t)
=-\sum_i\partial_i\!\left[\mu_i(\mathbf x,t)p(\mathbf x,t)\right]
+\frac12\sum_{i,j}
\partial_i\partial_j\!\left[a_{ij}(\mathbf x,t)p(\mathbf x,t)\right].
$$

The conceptual sequence is unchanged: first derivatives encode drift, the covariance $$\mathbf a$$ weights second derivatives, and the adjoint transfers derivatives from observables to densities.

The matrix $$\mathbf a$$ carries the multidimensional content. Its diagonal entries give the instantaneous variance rates of individual coordinates. Its off-diagonal entries give instantaneous covariance rates, so correlated noise creates mixed derivatives $$\partial_i\partial_j(a_{ij}p)$$. If $$\boldsymbol\Sigma=g(t)\mathbf I$$ is isotropic and independent of position, then $$\mathbf a=g^2(t)\mathbf I$$ and the diffusion term reduces to

$$
\frac{g^2(t)}{2}\sum_i\partial_i^2p
=\frac{g^2(t)}{2}\Delta p.
$$

This is the form commonly used in diffusion models. The simpler Laplacian is a special case of the covariance-weighted second-order operator, not the general multidimensional rule.

Three further tools sit naturally beside this derivation. **Girsanov's theorem** compares path measures of SDEs with different drifts. The **Feynman--Kac formula** represents solutions of certain PDEs as expectations over stochastic paths. **Doob's $$h$$-transform** modifies a process to express conditioning on future events. They change measures, connect stochastic paths to PDE solutions, and construct conditioned dynamics, respectively. Each builds on the distinction established here between paths, observables, operators, and densities.

The chain developed here tells us which object each extension changes. Girsanov changes the law on complete paths while retaining a compatible diffusion structure. Feynman--Kac converts a PDE solution into an expected path functional, reversing the direction used in the Fokker--Planck derivation. Doob's transform changes the generator so that conditioned trajectories become ordinary trajectories of a new process. Their formulas require additional assumptions, but their placement after the generator and density equation is deliberate.
