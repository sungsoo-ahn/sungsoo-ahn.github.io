## Why geometric flow matching?

Protein structure makes the geometry concrete. A backbone can be represented by one rigid frame per residue, an element of $$\mathrm{SE}(3)^N$$, while side-chain conformations are described by periodic torsion angles in products of $$\mathrm{SO}(2)$$. Treating every coordinate as an unconstrained vector ignores both the rigid-body and periodic state spaces.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-5.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Protein backbone frames and side chain torsion angles as geometric variables." caption="Protein structures combine rigid residue frames with periodic side-chain angles. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 5." %}

Flow-based protein models therefore work on product manifolds rather than only in Cartesian coordinates. FoldFlow and FrameFlow are direct examples; later models such as Proteus, MultiFlow, PepFlow, and PPFlow extend the construction or combine continuous structure with discrete molecular variables. Their shared problem is to define a valid path and velocity on each component of the state.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-6.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="AlphaFold style protein structure models and flow based extensions." caption="Flow-based protein models inherit structured frames and torsions from the representation used by modern folding systems. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 6." %}

## Geometry needed for Riemannian flows

A manifold $$\mathcal M$$ is locally Euclidean. Each chart $$\phi_i:U_i\to V_i\subset\mathbb R^d$$ gives coordinates for one patch, while an atlas of charts covers the full space.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Manifold covered by local Euclidean coordinate charts." caption="Charts describe a globally curved space through locally Euclidean patches. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 7." %}

Smoothness is a compatibility condition. On overlapping patches, the transition map $$\phi_j\circ\phi_i^{-1}$$ must be infinitely differentiable. This lets a derivative computed in one chart transform consistently into another.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Smooth transition maps between overlapping manifold charts." caption="Smooth coordinate changes make differentiation independent of a particular chart. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 8." %}

At $$p\in\mathcal M$$, valid instantaneous directions form the tangent space $$T_p\mathcal M$$. A tangent vector can be defined as the directional-derivative operator induced by a curve through $$p$$, then represented in the coordinate basis of a chart. The tangent space is a vector space, but it is not a subset of the manifold itself.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-9.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent space, directional derivative, and coordinate basis." caption="Tangent vectors are intrinsic directions represented through a chart basis. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 9." %}

A Riemannian metric assigns an inner product to every tangent space,

$$
g_p(u,v)=u^\top G(p)v,
$$

with $$G(p)$$ positive definite and smoothly varying. It turns tangent coordinates into geometric lengths and angles.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-10.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Riemannian metric on the tangent plane of a sphere." caption="The metric equips every tangent space with its local inner product. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 10." %}

The pair $$(\mathcal M,g)$$ is a Riemannian manifold. Its shortest metric-length curves are geodesics, the geometric replacements for Euclidean straight lines.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Geodesic minimizing curve length on a Riemannian manifold." caption="A geodesic minimizes the metric length among local curves joining its endpoints. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 11." %}

For $$v\in T_p\mathcal M$$, the exponential map follows the geodesic initialized at $$p$$ with velocity $$v$$ and returns its point at unit time:

$$
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=\gamma_v(1).
$$

In Euclidean space it becomes ordinary addition, $$p+v$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential map from a tangent vector to a manifold point." caption="Exp moves a tangent displacement back onto the manifold along a geodesic. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 12." %}

The logarithmic map locally reverses this operation. $$\operatorname{Log}_p(q)$$ is the tangent at $$p$$ whose exponential reaches $$q$$. It is well-defined only where the exponential map is locally invertible; a distant point may be connected by several geodesics.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Logarithmic map from a manifold point to the tangent space." caption="Log expresses a nearby endpoint as a tangent displacement from its base point. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 13." %}

## From Euclidean to Riemannian flow matching

In ordinary flow matching, a velocity field $$u_t$$ generates a one-to-one flow $$\psi_t$$ through

$$
\frac{dX_t}{dt}=u_t(X_t),
\qquad X_t=\psi_t(X_0).
$$

The flow pushes the base law at time zero toward the data law at time one.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow matching ODE and one to one flow map." caption="A Euclidean velocity field defines the probability transport through its ODE flow. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 14." %}

The practical recipe has three steps. Choose tractable conditional paths and velocities indexed by $$z$$. Marginalize them to obtain the target probability path and posterior-averaged marginal velocity. Then learn that velocity with the conditional regression loss, whose gradient matches the intractable marginal objective.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three step flow matching construction using conditional paths and loss." caption="Conditional paths, posterior marginalization, and conditional regression are the reusable flow-matching template. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 15." %}

The condition $$z$$ is flexible. It may be the source, the endpoint, or a source--endpoint pair, provided the conditional path satisfies the required marginal boundary conditions. Pairing both endpoints even permits a coupling whose source prior is difficult to evaluate but easy to sample.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-16.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Alternative conditioning variables for flow matching boundary paths." caption="Only the marginalized endpoint conditions constrain the choice of conditional index. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 16." %}

The marginalization identity is the central bridge:

$$
u_t(x)=\mathbb E\left[u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x\right].
$$

It is the same conditional-to-marginal relation used for denoising scores.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-17.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow marginalization identity and connection to related conditional expectation identities." caption="A posterior expectation turns tractable conditional velocities into the marginal field. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 17." %}

Riemannian flow matching now asks for three replacements: an ODE whose velocity is valid on $$\mathcal M$$, a conditional velocity ending at the desired point, and a loss comparing tangent vectors with the Riemannian metric.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three ingredients to generalize for Riemannian flow matching." caption="ODE, conditional velocity, and regression norm must all respect the manifold. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 19." %}

The notation $$dx_t=u_t(x_t)dt$$ abbreviates a curve equation: find $$\gamma(t)\in\mathcal M$$ such that

$$
\dot\gamma(t)=u_t(\gamma(t)),
\qquad u_t(\gamma(t))\in T_{\gamma(t)}\mathcal M.
$$

There is no intrinsic quotient $$dx_t/dt$$ until the derivative is interpreted as this tangent to a curve.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Manifold ODE interpreted as a curve with tangent velocity." caption="A manifold ODE asks for a curve whose derivative equals a tangent vector field. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 20." %}

Numerical integration uses the exponential map:

$$
x_{t+\Delta t}\approx
\operatorname{Exp}_{x_t}\!\left(\Delta t\,u_t(x_t)\right).
$$

For a constant geodesic velocity, the exact path is $$x_t=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}(t u_0(x_0))$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-21.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential map integration step and geodesic path." caption="Exp replaces vector addition in both a local solver step and a geodesic conditional path. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 21." %}

Reversing a short exponential-map step suggests the endpoint-conditioned target

$$
u_t(x_t\mid x_1)
=\frac{1}{1-t}\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_1).
$$

It points along the remaining geodesic toward $$x_1$$ with the speed needed to arrive at time one.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-22.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Conditional manifold velocity derived from logarithmic map to endpoint." caption="The remaining endpoint displacement is measured by Log and scaled by the remaining time. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 22." %}

The associated conditional flow is the geodesic interpolation

$$
\psi_t(x_0\mid x_1)
=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}
\left(t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(x_1)\right).
$$

Unlike the Euclidean Gaussian path, its density may be difficult to write explicitly. Flow matching does not need that density: sampling the endpoint pair and evaluating the path and tangent target is sufficient.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Geodesic conditional flow and unnecessary explicit Riemannian path density." caption="A tractable geometric path can support conditional regression even when its density is unavailable. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 23." %}

Finally, compare model and target in the tangent metric:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid Z)\right\rVert_{g,X_t}^2
\right].
$$

Its gradient equals that of the intractable marginal Riemannian flow-matching loss for the same reason as in Euclidean space: the conditional target averages to the marginal field at fixed $$X_t$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Riemannian conditional flow matching loss and gradient equivalence." caption="The conditional target is tangent-valued, and the metric supplies its squared error. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 24." %}

## Discrete states as continuous-time Markov chains

Some geometric systems mix continuous coordinates with discrete atom, residue, or nucleotide identities. A continuous-time Markov chain provides a continuous time variable for the discrete component, making it possible to coordinate both parts of a joint generative process.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Simultaneous discrete molecular identities and continuous three dimensional coordinates." caption="CTMCs put discrete molecular variables on the same continuous clock as geometric coordinates. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 26." %}

Let the vocabulary be $$\mathcal T$$ and the length-$$d$$ state space be $$\mathcal S=\mathcal T^d$$. A state $$x=(x^1,\ldots,x^d)$$ is a tuple of tokens. A three-character word example makes the combinatorial growth explicit: each coordinate takes an alphabet value, while the full state is one point in the product vocabulary.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Length three discrete words as points in a product vocabulary." caption="A high-dimensional discrete state is a tuple of coordinate-level symbols. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 27." %}

A CTMC is specified infinitesimally by rates $$u_t(y,x)$$:

$$
\Pr(X_{t+h}=y\mid X_t=x)
=\delta_{y,x}+h\,u_t(y,x)+o(h).
$$

Off-diagonal rates must be nonnegative, and each source column must sum to zero. The diagonal entry is therefore the negative total rate of leaving the state.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-28.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="CTMC transition kernel and rate conditions on a discrete state space." caption="A rate matrix is the discrete continuous-time analogue of a velocity field. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 28." %}

The probability path follows the Kolmogorov forward equation,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y)=\sum_x u_t(y,x)p_t(x).
$$

Using the zero column sum separates incoming and outgoing flux:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y)
=\sum_{x\ne y}u_t(y,x)p_t(x)
-\sum_{x\ne y}u_t(x,y)p_t(y).
$$

This is the discrete counterpart of a continuity equation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-29.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Kolmogorov forward equation decomposed into incoming and outgoing probability flux." caption="CTMC probability changes by incoming jump flux minus outgoing jump flux. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 29." %}

## Conditional discrete flow matching

The continuous recipe now transfers almost verbatim. Define a conditional rate field $$u_t(y,x\mid z)$$ and its conditional path; marginalize the rates under the posterior over $$z$$; then replace regression to the intractable marginal rate by a conditional loss.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-30.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three step program for discrete flow matching." caption="Conditional construction, posterior marginalization, and conditional learning reappear for CTMC rates. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 30." %}

Suppose $$u_t(\cdot,\cdot\mid z)$$ generates $$p_t(x\mid z)$$. Then the rate field generating the mixture $$p_t(x)=\sum_z p_t(x\mid z)p(z)$$ is

$$
u_t(y,x)
=\sum_z u_t(y,x\mid z)p(z\mid X_t=x).
$$

Substituting this expression into the Kolmogorov equation and applying Bayes' rule recovers the derivative of the marginal path.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-31.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Proof of the discrete flow matching marginalization trick." caption="Posterior averaging of conditional jump rates generates the marginalized CTMC path. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 31." %}

An ideal discrete flow loss compares the full outgoing-rate vector at the sampled state,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert u_t(\cdot,X_t)-u_t^\theta(\cdot,X_t)\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

Because the marginal rate is intractable, training uses $$u_t(\cdot,X_t\mid Z)$$ instead. The proof of gradient equivalence follows the same conditional-expectation calculation as continuous flow matching.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-32.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Marginal and conditional discrete flow matching losses." caption="Conditional rate supervision replaces regression to an intractable posterior-averaged rate vector. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 32." %}

## Factorized paths and posterior parameterization

The full rate vector has one component for every state in $$\mathcal S$$. For 100 binary variables, that is $$2^{100}$$ destinations, so a direct output is impossible.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-33.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential output dimension of a full discrete transition rate vector." caption="A full-state CTMC rate output grows exponentially with the number of discrete coordinates. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 33." %}

Factorization permits one coordinate to change at a time:

$$
u_t(y,x)=\sum_{i=1}^d
\delta(y^{\setminus i},x^{\setminus i})
u_t^i(y^i,x).
$$

Each head outputs rates over one coordinate's vocabulary while conditioning on the full current state.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-34.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Full discrete rate space compared with coordinate factorized transition rates." caption="Coordinate factorization restricts infinitesimal jumps to one component at a time. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 34." %}

For a small time step, the coordinate-wise transition factors approximate the joint kernel. The conditional probability path likewise factorizes, and the training loss becomes a sum over coordinate-level rate-vector errors.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-35.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Coordinate wise Euler kernel and factorized discrete flow matching loss." caption="Factorized paths turn the full-state objective into coordinate-level conditional rate regression. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 35." %}

A practical conditional path is built from a coupling $$(X_0,X_1)$$ and a schedule $$\kappa_t$$. Independently for each coordinate,

$$
p_t^i(x^i\mid x_0,x_1)
=\kappa_t\delta_{x^i,x_1^i}
+(1-\kappa_t)\delta_{x^i,x_0^i}.
$$

The token equals its target value with probability $$\kappa_t$$ and its source value otherwise. The compatible conditional rate is

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x^i\mid x_0,x_1)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\left(\delta_{y^i,x_1^i}-\delta_{y^i,x^i}\right).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/slide-36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Endpoint coupled factorized path and its conditional jump rate." caption="Each coordinate switches from its coupled source token toward its target token according to the schedule. Cropped from the 2025 GDL Riemannian-and-discrete-flow lecture, slide 36." %}

Marginalization shows that the model need not output a separate rate for every endpoint pair. It is enough to predict the endpoint posterior $$p_\theta(x_1\mid x_t)$$ and average the conditional rate:

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\left[p(x_1^i=y^i\mid x)-\delta_{y^i,x^i}\right].
$$

The network therefore resembles an ordinary denoiser over coordinate vocabularies, while the schedule converts its posterior prediction into a valid CTMC rate. This is the discrete counterpart of predicting a denoised endpoint and analytically converting it into a continuous velocity.
