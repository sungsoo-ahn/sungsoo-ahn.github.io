Flow matching is easy to state in Euclidean space. Choose a probability path, write a velocity field that moves samples along it, and regress a neural field onto tractable conditional velocities. Molecular data immediately stress every part of that sentence. A protein backbone is not an arbitrary vector, a torsion angle is periodic, and an amino-acid identity is not continuous at all.

The useful question is therefore not whether flow matching can be applied to geometric data. It is what must replace a Euclidean velocity when the state lies on a manifold or in a discrete vocabulary. The answer is different in the two cases. A manifold velocity is a tangent vector. A discrete velocity is an infinitesimal matrix of jump rates. The conditional-to-marginal identity behind training survives both changes.

This chapter follows the three-part order of the lecture. We first recap the geometric and flow-matching objects that will be replaced. We then derive Riemannian flow matching by substituting geodesics, exponential maps, logarithmic maps, and metric norms for Euclidean operations. Finally, we make the same construction on a discrete state space using a continuous-time Markov chain.

The lecture draws primarily on the [NeurIPS 2024 Flow Matching tutorial](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/tutorial/99531), with the manifold construction following [Flow Matching on General Geometries](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03660) and the discrete construction following [Generative Flows on Discrete State-Spaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04997).

## Why geometric flow matching starts with representation

A protein structure already mixes several geometries. For a protein of $$N$$ residues, one common backbone representation assigns a rigid frame to every residue. Each frame consists of a three-dimensional rotation and translation, so the complete backbone lies in a product space written schematically as

$$
\mathrm{SE}(3)^N.
$$

The rotation specifies the local orientation around a backbone atom such as $$C_\alpha$$, while the translation specifies the frame origin. A side chain is naturally described by torsion angles. Each angle is periodic and therefore lives on a copy of $$\mathrm{SO}(2)$$; up to seven torsions per residue give a product such as

$$
\mathrm{SO}(2)^{N\times 7}.
$$

The exact number of active torsions depends on the amino-acid type. The important point is that neither rigid frames nor angles behave like unconstrained coordinates in $$\mathbb R^d$$. Adding two rotation matrices does not produce a rotation matrix, and the angles $$0$$ and $$2\pi$$ denote the same conformation.

Backbone frames and side-chain torsions therefore occupy different geometric state spaces. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

Structure generation models inherit this representation problem from structure prediction. [AlphaFold2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03819-2) established a frame-based structure module with iterative refinement. Many later protein generators use a related structural parameterization while replacing prediction with a stochastic or deterministic transport from noise to a protein.

The intermediate structures make geometric denoising visible." %}

From <a href='https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03819-2'>Jumper et al. (2021)</a>." %}

The lecture names FoldFlow and FrameFlow as direct flow-based models, then Proteus, MultiFlow, PepFlow, and PPFlow as extensions. The models do not all generate the same object, but they share a dependency: the transport must stay inside the legal state space. A Euclidean update can leave $$\mathrm{SO}(3)$$ or ignore angular periodicity. Geometric flow matching changes the differential equation rather than repairing invalid states afterward.

## The geometry required by a manifold-valued flow

### Charts make a curved space locally differentiable

A manifold $$\mathcal M$$ is a topological space that locally resembles Euclidean space. For each patch $$U_i\subset\mathcal M$$, a chart

$$
\phi_i:U_i\longrightarrow V_i\subset\mathbb R^d
$$

assigns Euclidean coordinates. The collection of patches covers the manifold. No single chart has to describe the entire object: longitude and latitude work locally on a sphere but become singular at the poles and identify several coordinate values along a seam.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s07-manifold-atlas.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A curved manifold covered by two patches, each mapped to a Euclidean coordinate plane." caption="An atlas represents a curved space through overlapping local coordinate patches. Each chart maps one patch into Euclidean coordinates without claiming that the manifold is globally flat. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

A smooth manifold requires compatible charts. If $$U_i$$ and $$U_j$$ overlap, the coordinate-change map

$$
\phi_j\circ\phi_i^{-1}:
\phi_i(U_i\cap U_j)\longrightarrow \phi_j(U_i\cap U_j)
$$

must be infinitely differentiable. This condition is what allows a derivative computed in one chart to be translated into another. Without it, “differentiate a function on $$\mathcal M$$” would depend on an arbitrary coordinate choice.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s08-smooth-chart-transition.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two overlapping manifold patches and their smooth transition map between coordinate images." caption="On an overlap, the transition map carries coordinates from one chart to another. Smooth compatibility makes calculus independent of which local coordinate system is used. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

### A velocity belongs to the tangent space, not to the manifold

At a point $$p\in\mathcal M$$, all valid instantaneous directions form the tangent space $$T_p\mathcal M$$. This is a vector space attached to $$p$$. It is not a subset of the manifold: a tangent arrow can point away from a sphere even though the curve that realizes the arrow remains on the sphere to first order.

One formal definition treats a tangent vector as a directional-derivative operator. Let $$\gamma:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to\mathcal M$$ be a smooth curve with $$\gamma(0)=p$$. For a smooth test function $$f:\mathcal M\to\mathbb R$$, the tangent induced by $$\gamma$$ acts as

$$
X_{\gamma,p}[f]
=\left.\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))\right|_{t=0}.
$$

The operator definition is intrinsic: two curves define the same tangent if they differentiate every smooth test function in the same way. A chart gives a numerical representation. Writing $$x^i$$ for its coordinates,

$$
X_{\gamma,p}[f]
=\sum_{i=1}^{d}
\left.\frac{d}{dt}(\phi\circ\gamma)^i(t)\right|_{t=0}
\frac{\partial(f\circ\phi^{-1})}{\partial x^i}(\phi(p)).
$$

The coordinate velocity supplies coefficients, while the partial derivatives supply a basis of $$T_p\mathcal M$$. A change of chart transforms both pieces so that the action on $$f$$ stays fixed.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s09-sphere-tangent-space.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A tangent plane touching a sphere at point p with two tangent directions." caption="The tangent plane contains the instantaneous directions available at a point on the sphere. Its vectors form a linear space even though the sphere itself does not. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

A flow field on $$\mathcal M$$ is therefore not one vector in a common ambient space. It is a rule

$$
u_t(p)\in T_p\mathcal M
$$

that returns a tangent vector in the particular tangent space attached to each current state $$p$$.

### The metric turns tangent coordinates into geometry

A Riemannian metric assigns an inner product to every tangent space:

$$
g_p:T_p\mathcal M\times T_p\mathcal M\longrightarrow\mathbb R.
$$

In local coordinates it can be written

$$
g_p(u,v)=u^\top G(p)v,
$$

where $$G(p)$$ is positive definite and changes smoothly with $$p$$. The coordinates of a tangent vector depend on the chart, but the inner product does not. The metric defines the local squared norm

$$
\lVert v\rVert_{g,p}^{2}=g_p(v,v),
$$

which will later replace the ordinary squared error in the flow-matching loss.

The pair $$(\mathcal M,g)$$ is a Riemannian manifold. For a smooth curve $$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal M$$, its metric length is

$$
L(\gamma)=\int_0^1
\sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}\!\left(\dot\gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t)\right)}\,dt.
$$

A geodesic locally minimizes this length. It plays the role of a straight line, but that role is local. Two distant points can have several geodesics between them, and a shortest geodesic can cease to be unique at the cut locus.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s11-geodesic-on-sphere.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A curve leaving a tangent plane and following the surface of a sphere." caption="A tangent vector at (p) initializes a surface curve (gamma). On a Riemannian manifold, the geodesic is the local replacement for a straight Euclidean trajectory. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

### Exponential and logarithmic maps replace addition and subtraction

Given $$v\in T_p\mathcal M$$, let $$\gamma_v$$ be the geodesic satisfying

$$
\gamma_v(0)=p,
\qquad
\dot\gamma_v(0)=v.
$$

The exponential map follows that geodesic for unit time:

$$
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=\gamma_v(1).
$$

Its input is a tangent displacement and its output is a point on the manifold. In Euclidean space, the geodesic is $$\gamma_v(t)=p+tv$$, so $$\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=p+v$$. This is why the exponential map replaces addition in a manifold integrator.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s12-exponential-map.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A tangent vector at p followed by a geodesic to its endpoint on a sphere." caption="The exponential map sends a tangent displacement at (p) to the endpoint reached along its geodesic. In flat space, the same operation is ordinary vector addition. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The logarithmic map reverses this construction locally:

$$
\operatorname{Log}_p(q)\in T_p\mathcal M,
\qquad
\operatorname{Exp}_p(\operatorname{Log}_p(q))=q.
$$

It expresses the endpoint $$q$$ as a tangent displacement based at $$p$$. The qualifier “locally” matters. The map is uniquely defined only in a neighborhood where $$\operatorname{Exp}_p$$ is a diffeomorphism. On a sphere, the antipode of $$p$$ can be reached along infinitely many great-circle directions, so there is no unique logarithm there.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s13-logarithmic-map.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A logarithmic map from a sphere point q back to a tangent vector at p." caption="The logarithmic map expresses a nearby endpoint (q) as a tangent displacement based at (p). Its uniqueness is restricted to the region before geodesics become ambiguous. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

These objects give the replacement dictionary needed later: tangent vectors replace free Euclidean vectors, the metric replaces the dot product, geodesics replace straight lines, $$\operatorname{Exp}$$ replaces addition, and $$\operatorname{Log}$$ replaces subtraction.

## The conditional-to-marginal argument in Euclidean space

Before changing the geometry, the lecture reconstructs ordinary flow matching. Let $$p_0$$ be a source distribution and $$p_1=q$$ the data distribution. A time-dependent velocity field $$u_t$$ defines the ODE

$$
\frac{dX_t}{dt}=u_t(X_t),
\qquad t\in[0,1].
$$

Solving the ODE from an initial state defines a flow map $$\psi_t$$:

$$
X_t=\psi_t(X_0).
$$

Under the usual regularity conditions, $$\psi_t$$ is one-to-one and pushes $$p_0$$ to a probability path $$p_t$$ ending at $$p_1$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s14-euclidean-flow-path.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A compact source density transported into a multimodal target density." caption="A flow map transports samples from a simple source law (p) to a target law (q) while defining intermediate distributions. From the <a href='https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/tutorial/99531'>NeurIPS 2024 Flow Matching tutorial</a>." %}

The marginal velocity $$u_t(x)$$ that generates a chosen marginal path is generally unavailable. Flow matching avoids computing it directly by introducing a conditioning variable $$Z$$. For each value $$z$$, choose a tractable conditional probability path $$p_t(x\mid z)$$ and a conditional velocity $$u_t(x\mid z)$$ that generates that path.

The conditional paths mix into the desired marginal path:

$$
p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz.
$$

The corresponding marginal velocity is the posterior average

$$
u_t(x)
=\int u_t(x\mid z)p(z\mid x,t)\,dz
=\mathbb E\!\left[u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x\right].
$$

The expectation is conditioned on the current state. An unconditional average over $$Z$$ would combine velocities intended for different locations and would not, in general, generate $$p_t$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s15-conditional-marginal-fields.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Four panels comparing a conditional probability path, marginal path, conditional velocity, and marginal velocity." caption="Conditional paths and fields are easy to construct for a fixed endpoint; posterior averaging produces the marginal path and field. From the <a href='https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/tutorial/99531'>NeurIPS 2024 Flow Matching tutorial</a>." %}

The conditioning variable is deliberately flexible. It can be the source sample $$X_0$$, the target sample $$X_1$$, or a coupled pair $$(X_0,X_1)$$. What matters is that the mixture obeys the endpoint conditions

$$
\int p_0(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz=p_0(x),
\qquad
\int p_1(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz=p_1(x).
$$

Conditioning on a pair is useful because it only requires samples from a coupling $$\pi_{0,1}(X_0,X_1)$$. Its source marginal may be intractable as a density while remaining easy to sample. Flow matching uses the samples and conditional target, not an evaluation of the source density.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s16-paired-intractable-prior.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two source-target distribution pairs using cat and dog images to illustrate paired conditioning." caption="Pair conditioning only needs a coupling with the correct endpoint marginals. The source law may be hard to evaluate even when paired samples are available. From the <a href='https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/tutorial/99531'>NeurIPS 2024 Flow Matching tutorial</a>." %}

Let $$u_t^\theta$$ be the learned field. The ideal marginal objective is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,X_t\sim p_t}
\left[\left\lVert u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t)\right\rVert^2\right].
$$

Because $$u_t$$ is intractable, training uses conditional targets:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,Z,X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid Z)}
\left[\left\lVert u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid Z)\right\rVert^2\right].
$$

The two losses are not numerically identical; the conditional loss contains an extra conditional variance that does not depend on $$\theta$$. Their gradients are identical. Expanding the conditional square and conditioning on $$X_t$$ replaces $$u_t(X_t\mid Z)$$ in the cross term by its posterior mean $$u_t(X_t)$$. That is the learning argument that will survive both geometric generalizations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s17-flow-matching-identity-map.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A diagram connecting conditional and marginal flow maps, velocities, probability paths, ODE solving, differentiation, and marginalization." caption="The conditional and marginal constructions are connected by differentiation, ODE integration, continuity, posterior expectation, and marginalization. The learned regression target uses the lower conditional route to recover the upper marginal velocity. From the <a href='https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/tutorial/99531'>NeurIPS 2024 Flow Matching tutorial</a>." %}

## Riemannian flow matching

Riemannian flow matching asks for an ODE on $$(\mathcal M,g)$$ whose time-one distribution is the data law. The Euclidean construction contains three objects that must be changed together:

1. the ODE must produce a curve on $$\mathcal M$$;
2. the conditional target must be a tangent vector that reaches the endpoint;
3. the regression loss must compare tangent vectors with the Riemannian metric.

Changing only the path is insufficient. A network can draw a curve on a sphere while predicting an ambient vector with a radial component, and an ordinary coordinate norm can assign different errors after a change of chart.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s19-geodesic-conditional-flow.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A geodesic conditional flow between x0 and x1 with an exponential-logarithmic formula and schedule." caption="Riemannian flow matching replaces straight conditional paths by geodesics. On manifolds with closed-form exponential and logarithmic maps, the path and target velocity can be evaluated without simulating a stochastic bridge. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03660'>Chen and Lipman (2023)</a>." %}

### A manifold ODE is a curve equation

The familiar notation

$$
dx_t=u_t(x_t)\,dt
$$

is an abbreviation on a manifold. Subtraction and division of manifold points are not intrinsically defined, so $$dx_t/dt$$ cannot mean a coordinate-free quotient. The actual problem is to find a curve $$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal M$$ satisfying

$$
\gamma(t)=x_t,
\qquad
\dot\gamma(t)=u_t(\gamma(t)),
\qquad
u_t(x_t)\in T_{x_t}\mathcal M.
$$

Every velocity must belong to the tangent space at the current state. For an embedded manifold this can be enforced by projecting an ambient prediction onto $$T_{x_t}\mathcal M$$. For a Lie group or another space with a tailored parameterization, the network can predict tangent coordinates directly.

A first-order numerical step replaces Euclidean addition by the exponential map:

$$
x_{t+\Delta t}
\approx
\operatorname{Exp}_{x_t}\!\left(\Delta t\,u_t(x_t)\right).
$$

The operation has the same local meaning as Euler's method. It takes the current tangent velocity, scales it by the step size, and returns a legal manifold point. Repeating the update integrates a general time-varying field.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s21-manifold-euler-update.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A vector field on a curved surface with local tangent approximations connected across coordinate patches." caption="A manifold integrator advances through local tangent updates while the state remains on the curved space. The same intrinsic trajectory can be represented in several charts. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03660'>Chen and Lipman (2023)</a>." %}

A geodesic is the special case in which that local linearization is exact in the manifold sense. Starting at $$x_0$$ with initial velocity $$u_0(x_0)$$,

$$
x_t=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}\!\left(tu_0(x_0)\right).
$$

The tangent vector is parallel along the curve even though its ambient coordinates can change. Euclidean straight lines are recovered when $$\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}(v)=x_0+v$$.

### The conditional target points along the remaining geodesic

Now condition on an endpoint $$x_1$$. We want an ODE whose conditional trajectory ends at that point. Reversing a short exponential-map step gives

$$
u_t(x_t)
\approx
\frac{1}{\Delta t}
\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_{t+\Delta t}).
$$

For a constant-speed geodesic that reaches $$x_1$$ at time one, the remaining displacement is $$\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_1)$$ and the remaining time is $$1-t$$. The conditional velocity is therefore

$$
u_t(x_t\mid x_1)
=\frac{1}{1-t}
\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_1).
$$

The factor appears singular as $$t\to1$$, but the logarithmic displacement shrinks proportionally along the exact conditional path. Numerically, one still avoids evaluating exactly at the endpoint.

The resulting conditional flow is geodesic interpolation:

$$
\psi_t(x_0\mid x_1)
=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}\!\left(
t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(x_1)
\right),
\qquad t\in[0,1].
$$

More generally, a schedule $$\kappa(t)$$ with $$\kappa(0)=0$$ and $$\kappa(1)=1$$ gives

$$
\psi_t(x_0\mid x_1)
=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}\!\left(
\kappa(t)\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(x_1)
\right).
$$

On simple geometries—Euclidean space, spheres, tori, and several hyperbolic models—the geodesic and its tangent often have closed forms. Training is then simulation-free: sample $$(x_0,x_1)$$, sample $$t$$, evaluate $$x_t$$ and its conditional tangent, and regress.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s23-manifold-conditional-path.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two points on a curved manifold connected through x_t, with a tangent velocity toward x_1." caption="At the current point (x_t), the conditional field lies in (T_{x_t}mathcal M) and points along the remaining geodesic to (x_1). A different path around the manifold would imply a different conditional field. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The conditional density $$p_t(x\mid x_1)$$ can be difficult to express. In Euclidean flow matching, one often writes a Gaussian centered at the interpolated point. On a manifold, a comparable density must account for curvature and the volume form, and the exponential map can be many-to-one away from its injectivity neighborhood.

Flow matching does not require evaluating that density. It requires sampling a conditional state and computing its target velocity. A deterministic geodesic coupling supplies both. One could instead solve the Riemannian continuity equation for a desired density path, but doing so would discard the main computational advantage of conditional flow matching.

### The metric supplies the conditional regression loss

The model and target now belong to the same tangent space $$T_{X_t}\mathcal M$$. Their discrepancy should be measured by the metric at the sampled state:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,Z,X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid Z)}
\left[
\left\lVert
u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid Z)
\right\rVert_{g,X_t}^{2}
\right].
$$

The intractable marginal loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,X_t\sim p_t}
\left[
\left\lVert
u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t)
\right\rVert_{g,X_t}^{2}
\right].
$$

At fixed $$t$$ and $$X_t=x$$, the metric tensor $$G(x)$$ is fixed. Expanding the conditional square produces a cross term

$$
-2\,g_x\!\left(
u_t^\theta(x),
\mathbb E[u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x]
\right),
$$

and the posterior expectation is the marginal field $$u_t(x)$$. The remaining conditional-target norm is independent of $$\theta$$. Hence

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta)
=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RFM}}(\theta).
$$

The theorem is the Euclidean conditional-flow-matching proof with one disciplined change: every comparison is made inside the current tangent space using $$g_{X_t}$$.

## Discrete states as continuous-time Markov chains

Protein, molecule, and DNA generation also contain discrete variables: residue identities, atom types, bond types, or nucleotides. A discrete-time Markov chain can generate those variables, but a mixed model then has one clock for continuous coordinates and another for discrete transitions. A continuous-time Markov chain gives both components a common $$t\in[0,1]$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s26-mixed-molecular-flow-models.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A molecule generated over continuous time with coordinate flow and discrete atom-type alternatives represented below." caption="A molecular generator may transport three-dimensional geometry and categorical atom identities on the same continuous clock. The geometric component can use a flow, while the discrete component uses simplex dynamics or a CTMC. From the <a href='https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/tutorial/99531'>NeurIPS 2024 Flow Matching tutorial</a>." %}

Let $$\mathcal T$$ be a finite vocabulary with $$K$$ tokens. A length-$$d$$ state belongs to the product space

$$
\mathcal S=\mathcal T^d,
\qquad
x=(x^1,\ldots,x^d).
$$

For fixed-length text with $$d=3$$, “Cat,” “Dog,” and “Car” are three states, while each character is a coordinate-level token. The number of complete states is $$K^d$$. That distinction between a token and a full state will become decisive when we parameterize rates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s27-discrete-word-space.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="The words Cat, Dog, and Car marked as separate points in a discrete product state space." caption="A discrete example is a point in a product vocabulary, not a coordinate in a continuous plane. Nearby placement in the diagram is illustrative; the CTMC is defined by transition rates rather than Euclidean distance. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

### Infinitesimal transition rates replace velocities

A time-inhomogeneous CTMC $$(X_t)_{0\le t\le1}$$ has a short-time transition kernel

$$
p_{t+h\mid t}(y\mid x)
:=\Pr(X_{t+h}=y\mid X_t=x)
=\delta_{y,x}+h\,u_t(y,x)+o(h).
$$

The coefficient $$u_t(y,x)$$ is the instantaneous rate of jumping from source state $$x$$ to destination $$y$$. It is not itself a probability. For $$y\ne x$$ it must satisfy

$$
u_t(y,x)\ge0.
$$

Every source column must sum to zero:

$$
\sum_y u_t(y,x)=0.
$$

Consequently,

$$
u_t(x,x)=-\sum_{y\ne x}u_t(y,x)\le0.
$$

The negative diagonal records the total rate of leaving $$x$$. These rate conditions ensure that the short-time kernel is normalized and nonnegative to first order in $$h$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-16/s28-ctmc-rate-field.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A discrete grid with arrows from current state x to possible destination states y and label u_t(y,x)." caption="A CTMC rate field assigns an outgoing jump rate from the current state (x) to every possible destination (y). The diagonal coefficient balances the total outward rate. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The process begins from a prior distribution $$p_0(x)=\Pr(X_0=x)$$. Its one-time marginal evolves according to the Kolmogorov forward equation:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y)
=\sum_x u_t(y,x)p_t(x).
$$

Separating the term $$x=y$$ and using the zero column sum gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y)
=\sum_{x\ne y}u_t(y,x)p_t(x)
-\sum_{x\ne y}u_t(x,y)p_t(y).
$$

The first sum is probability flux arriving at $$y$$ from other states. The second is flux leaving $$y$$. This is the discrete analogue of a continuity equation: the density at one location changes because probability moves across its boundary, except that the boundary is now a set of allowed jumps.

The analogy with a continuous velocity should not be pushed too far. A vector field says in which infinitesimal direction a sample moves. A CTMC rate vector says how frequently it jumps to each destination. Individual paths are discontinuous even though their distribution evolves continuously in time.

## Conditional discrete flow matching

The lecture now repeats the three-step flow-matching program without changing its order:

1. define a conditional rate field and conditional probability path;
2. derive the marginal rate by posterior averaging;
3. replace an intractable marginal objective by a conditional objective with the same gradient.

The repeated pattern is the point. Vector fields and rate matrices are different objects, but mixture paths obey the same conditional-expectation algebra.

### Posterior averaging gives the marginal rate

Let $$Z$$ index a family of conditional CTMCs. For each $$z$$, suppose $$u_t(y,x\mid z)$$ generates a conditional path $$p_t(x\mid z)$$. The mixture path is

$$
p_t(x)=\sum_z p_t(x\mid z)p(z).
$$

The rate field that generates this mixture is

$$
u_t(y,x)
=\sum_z u_t(y,x\mid z)p(z\mid X_t=x)
=\mathbb E\!\left[u_t(y,x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x\right].
$$

The proof proceeds through the probability path, just as in the lecture. Start from the Kolmogorov equation for the proposed marginal field:

$$
\sum_x u_t(y,x)p_t(x).
$$

Substitute the posterior average:

$$
\sum_x\sum_z
u_t(y,x\mid z)
p(z\mid X_t=x)p_t(x).
$$

Bayes' rule gives

$$
p(z\mid X_t=x)p_t(x)=p_t(x\mid z)p(z),
$$

so the expression becomes

$$
\sum_z p(z)
\left[
\sum_xu_t(y,x\mid z)p_t(x\mid z)
\right].
$$

The bracket is the conditional Kolmogorov equation, $$d p_t(y\mid z)/dt$$. Therefore

$$
\sum_xu_t(y,x)p_t(x)
=\sum_zp(z)\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y\mid z)
=\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y).
$$

Posterior averaging of conditional rates generates the marginalized path exactly.

### Conditional rate regression has the correct gradient

An ideal model $$u_t^\theta$$ would minimize error against the entire marginal outgoing-rate vector:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,X_t\sim p_t}
\left[
\left\lVert
u_t(\cdot,X_t)-u_t^\theta(\cdot,X_t)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

The dot denotes every possible destination. This objective is intractable because the marginal rate contains the posterior over $$Z$$. The conditional objective is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CDFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,Z,X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid Z)}
\left[
\left\lVert
u_t(\cdot,X_t\mid Z)-u_t^\theta(\cdot,X_t)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

Conditioning on $$X_t$$ in the cross term replaces the conditional rate by the marginal rate. As before, the conditional target contributes an additive variance independent of $$\theta$$. Thus the gradients of the conditional and marginal losses agree.

There is one constraint hidden inside this regression view: the predicted object must still be a legal generator. Off-diagonal outputs need to be nonnegative, and the diagonal must close the column sum. A convenient parameterization predicts nonnegative off-diagonal rates and defines the diagonal analytically rather than asking the optimizer to learn normalization.

## Factorized paths and velocities

### The full state-space output is exponential

The short-time kernel requires the vector

$$
p_{t+h\mid t}(\cdot\mid x)
=\delta_{\cdot,x}+h\,u_t(\cdot,x)+o(h).
$$

A direct model would output one rate for every state in $$\mathcal S=\mathcal T^d$$. For a binary vector of length 100,

$$
\lvert\mathcal S\rvert=2^{100},
$$

which rules out an explicit full-state rate vector. The vocabulary at each coordinate is small; the Cartesian product is not.

The standard restriction is to permit one coordinate to jump at a time. Let $$y^{\setminus i}$$ denote every coordinate of $$y$$ except coordinate $$i$$. A factorized generator has the form

$$
u_t(y,x)
=\sum_{i=1}^{d}
\delta_{y^{\setminus i},x^{\setminus i}}
u_t^i(y^i,x).
$$

The Kronecker delta forces the other $$d-1$$ coordinates to remain equal to the current state. The coordinate rate $$u_t^i(y^i,x)$$ may depend on the entire current state $$x$$, so the model can still express interactions between coordinates. Factorization restricts which infinitesimal jumps occur, not what information determines their rates.

For a small step $$h$$, the full transition kernel can be written to first order as a product of coordinate transitions:

$$
\Pr(X_{t+h}=y\mid X_t=x)
=\prod_{i=1}^{d}
\left[
\delta_{y^i,x^i}
+h\,u_t^i(y^i,x)
\right]
+o(h).
$$

Expanding the product keeps the no-jump term and all single-coordinate terms at order $$h$$. Simultaneous changes of two coordinates appear at order $$h^2$$ and vanish in the infinitesimal limit. This is why coordinate-wise Euler sampling matches the factorized CTMC generator.

The conditional probability path is factorized in the same coordinate system:

$$
p_t(x\mid z)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}p_t^i(x^i\mid z).
$$

The conditional loss becomes a sum of coordinate-level rate-vector errors:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CDFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E
\left[
\sum_{i=1}^{d}
\left\lVert
u_t^i(\cdot,X_t\mid Z)
-u_t^{\theta,i}(\cdot,X_t)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

Each network head has size $$K$$ rather than $$K^d$$. Its input can still contain all coordinates, so a residue's mutation rate can depend on the rest of a protein sequence or structure.

### A coupled endpoint path supplies a tractable conditional rate

In practice, take the conditioning variable to be a coupled endpoint pair $$Z=(X_0,X_1)$$ sampled from $$\pi_{0,1}$$. Choose a monotone schedule $$\kappa_t$$ with $$\kappa_0=0$$ and $$\kappa_1=1$$. For each coordinate,

$$
p_t^i(x^i\mid x_0,x_1)
=\kappa_t\delta_{x^i,x_1^i}
+(1-\kappa_t)\delta_{x^i,x_0^i}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
X_t^i=
\begin{cases}
X_1^i,&\text{with probability }\kappa_t,\\
X_0^i,&\text{with probability }1-\kappa_t.
\end{cases}
$$

The full conditional path is the product over coordinates. It begins at the coupled source state and ends at the coupled target state. Coordinates choose independently when viewed conditionally on the endpoints, although their predicted marginal behavior can remain dependent through the posterior over $$X_1$$.

The compatible conditional coordinate rate is

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x^i\mid x_0,x_1)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\left[
\delta_{y^i,x_1^i}-\delta_{y^i,x^i}
\right].
$$

If $$x^i\ne x_1^i$$, the only positive off-diagonal destination is the target token, with hazard $$\dot\kappa_t/(1-\kappa_t)$$. The negative current-state term sets the diagonal so that the rates sum to zero. If the coordinate already equals its target, the two delta terms cancel and it does not jump away under this conditional process.

The schedule has a simple survival interpretation. A coordinate remains at its source value with probability $$1-\kappa_t$$. Its instantaneous hazard of switching to the target is

$$
-\frac{d}{dt}\log(1-\kappa_t)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}.
$$

That is exactly the coefficient in the conditional rate. The construction is the discrete counterpart of dividing a remaining displacement by the remaining time.

Slides 36 and 37 deliberately repeat this endpoint-coupled path before the final parameterization. The repetition fixes the object being marginalized: not an arbitrary categorical velocity, but the rate induced by a particular source-target coupling and schedule.

### Predicting the target posterior is enough

The last step asks how to parameterize the marginal coordinate rate $$u_t^i(y^i,x)$$. Start from the marginalization identity over endpoint pairs:

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x)
=\sum_{x_0,x_1}
u_t^i(y^i,x^i\mid x_0,x_1)
p(x_0,x_1\mid X_t=x).
$$

Substituting the conditional rate gives

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\sum_{x_0,x_1}
\left[
\delta_{y^i,x_1^i}-\delta_{y^i,x^i}
\right]
p(x_0,x_1\mid X_t=x).
$$

The source endpoint $$x_0$$ does not appear inside the brackets, so it can be marginalized out:

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\sum_{x_1}
\left[
\delta_{y^i,x_1^i}-\delta_{y^i,x^i}
\right]
p(x_1\mid X_t=x).
$$

Equivalently,

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\left[
p(X_1^i=y^i\mid X_t=x)
-\delta_{y^i,x^i}
\right].
$$

The network only needs to predict the posterior distribution of the target token at each coordinate. The schedule converts that denoising posterior into a valid CTMC generator. This is the discrete analogue of predicting a clean endpoint and analytically converting it into a continuous velocity.

The three constructions now line up. Euclidean flow matching averages conditional vectors. Riemannian flow matching averages tangent vectors and measures their error with the local metric. Discrete flow matching averages rate vectors and evolves probabilities with the Kolmogorov equation. Geometry changes the object that moves; conditional regression remains the mechanism that makes the marginal dynamics learnable.

That common regression principle is the bridge across the three state spaces, while each space retains its own valid dynamics.
