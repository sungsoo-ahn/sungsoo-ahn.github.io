## From data geometry to equivariant networks

Geometric deep learning begins with a design question: given the geometry of a dataset, what is the recipe for a neural architecture?

Geometry studies measurements and relationships on a space. Euclidean, hyperbolic, and Riemannian geometries differ because they define different transformations and invariants.

The geometry of data is specified by transformations that preserve object identity. A cat remains the same cat after changes that the task declares irrelevant.

An equivariant neural network does not merely learn this relationship from augmented examples. Its operations are constructed to commute with the transformations.

## Symmetry of graphs and geometric graphs

An object is symmetric with respect to an operation when applying that operation preserves a specified property. The collection of property-preserving operations forms a group.

### Graph representation and permutation

A graph with $$n$$ nodes can be represented by an adjacency matrix $$A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$$ and node features $$X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$$. For a molecular graph, each node may carry an atom type, coordinates, or charge, while each edge records whether a bond exists and may carry a bond type or another pairwise feature.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s18-molecular-graph.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Molecule represented as atoms, bonds, node features, and edge features." caption="A molecular graph separates atom-wise and bond-wise information. The source lecture credits Xavier Bresson for this figure." %}

Relabeling vertices by a permutation matrix $$P$$ changes the stored tensors as

$$
X\mapsto PX,
\qquad
A\mapsto PAP^\top,
$$

without changing the graph.

The word permutation is incomplete until its action is specified. The same abstract relabeling acts as $$P$$ on node features and as $$A\mapsto PAP^\top$$ on pairwise features.

A graph-level property such as toxicity should be invariant:

$$
f(PAP^\top,PX)=f(A,X).
$$

A node-wise prediction should be equivariant:

$$
F(PAP^\top,PX)=PF(A,X).
$$

Solubility or toxicity is attached to the whole molecule and is therefore invariant to node labels. Atom-wise outputs, by contrast, must be permuted with the atoms. The two original PowerPoint visuals come from slide 21.

If the output is a distance matrix $$D$$, it transforms as $$D\mapsto PDP^\top$$. Equivariance always depends on both the input and output action.

Algebraic structures add operations. A monoid has a closed associative product and identity; a group additionally requires inverses. Rings, fields, and vector spaces add further operations or axioms.

A group $$(G,\cdot)$$ satisfies four axioms. For all $$g,h,i\in G$$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{closure:} &&g\cdot h\in G,\\
&\text{associativity:} &&(g\cdot h)\cdot i=g\cdot(h\cdot i),\\
&\text{identity:} &&e\cdot g=g\cdot e=g,\\
&\text{inverse:} &&g^{-1}\cdot g=g\cdot g^{-1}=e.
\end{aligned}
$$

The product need not commute. Abelian groups satisfy $$gh=hg$$; non-Abelian groups do not. Rotations in three dimensions provide a standard noncommutative example.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s29-noncommuting-rotations.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A board rotated around two axes in opposite orders, producing different final orientations." caption="Two-dimensional rotations commute, but three-dimensional rotations generally do not. Original PowerPoint image from slide 29." %}

### Matrix, permutation, orthogonal, and Euclidean groups

The general linear group $$GL(n,\mathbb R)$$ consists of invertible real $$n\times n$$ matrices under multiplication. For example,

$$
GL(2,\mathbb R)=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}
:a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R,\ ad-bc\neq 0
\right\}.
$$

Closure follows because a product of invertible matrices is invertible, associativity is inherited from matrix multiplication, the identity matrix supplies the identity element, and matrix invertibility supplies the inverse.

The symmetric group $$S_n$$ consists of all permutations of $$n$$ elements under composition. Permutation matrices embed $$S_n$$ as a subgroup of $$GL(n,\mathbb R)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s31-permutation-group.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Permutation matrices visualized by rearrangements of marked grid cells." caption="Permutation matrices realize the symmetric group inside the general linear group. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 31." %}

The orthogonal group

$$
O(n)=\{Q\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}:Q^\top Q=I\}
$$

contains rotations and reflections that preserve Euclidean distance while fixing the origin. Its special subgroup $$SO(n)$$ has determinant $$+1$$ and excludes reflections.

The Euclidean group $$E(n)$$ adds translations to orthogonal transformations. $$SE(n)$$ keeps only orientation-preserving rotations and translations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s33-euclidean-group.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A rigid object transformed by two successive rotation-and-translation operations." caption="Successive rigid motions compose into another rigid motion. Original PowerPoint diagram from slide 33." %}

Representing $$g\in SE(n)$$ as $$(R,t)$$ gives the semidirect product law

$$
(R,t)(R',t')=(RR',Rt'+t),
$$

and inverse $$(R,t)^{-1}=(R^{-1},-R^{-1}t)$$. The same element can be written as a homogeneous matrix,

$$
(R,t)\longleftrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}R&t\\0^\top&1\end{pmatrix},
$$

which turns the semidirect-product law into ordinary matrix multiplication.

Three-dimensional rotations can be represented by an axis and angle, three Euler rotations, a quaternion, or a rotation matrix. These are coordinate systems for the same group element, with different singularities and redundancies.

## Actions, homogeneous spaces, and quotients

A group becomes relevant to data through an action. A left action maps $$(g,x)$$ to $$g\cdot x$$ and satisfies compatibility $$(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$$ and identity $$e\cdot x=x$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s36-group-action.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A rotation carrying one point on a sphere to another." caption="A group action turns an abstract rotation into a transformation of a point in the domain. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 36." %}

The same rotation can act on a vector, a triangle, or a molecular coordinate array. The action law guarantees that composing rotations before or after applying them gives the same result.

For a triangle written as a coordinate array $$X=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\in\mathbb R^{3\times 2}$$ and a planar rotation $$R_\theta$$, the action is

$$
R_\theta\cdot X=(R_\theta x_1,R_\theta x_2,R_\theta x_3).
$$

An action is transitive when every point can be reached from every other point by some group element. A space with a transitive $$G$$-action is homogeneous.

Equivalently, the orbit

$$
Gx_0=\{g\cdot x_0:g\in G\}
$$

equals the entire space. The original examples from slide 38 contrast two transitive actions with one action that cannot cover its whole domain.

The stabilizer of $$x$$ is

$$
\operatorname{Stab}(x)=\{g\in G:g\cdot x=x\}.
$$

For a point on the sphere, rotations about its axis form a stabilizer; when the group acts on itself by left multiplication, only the identity fixes a point.

Given a subgroup $$H\le G$$, a left coset is $$gH=\{gh:h\in H\}$$. Cosets partition the group into equally sized pieces: begin with $$H$$, choose an element not yet covered, form its coset, and repeat until every group element belongs to one coset.

The quotient $$G/H$$ is the set of distinct cosets. A homogeneous space can often be identified with $$G/H$$: transformations differing by a stabilizer element reach the same point.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s41-quotient-sphere.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="The two-sphere identified with rotations modulo the stabilizer of a reference point." caption="The sphere is the homogeneous space \(S^2\cong SO(3)/SO(2)\): rotations differing only around the reference axis reach the same point. Original PowerPoint diagram from slide 41." %}

For a graph example, let $$H=\{(123),(231),(312)\}$$ be the cyclic subgroup of $$S_3$$. The quotient has two cosets,

$$
S_3/H
=\big\{
\{(123),(231),(312)\},
\{(321),(213),(132)\}
\big\},
$$

corresponding to the two orientations of a three-cycle.

## Representations, steerability, and equivariance

A group representation maps each group element to an invertible linear map $$\rho(g)$$ on a vector space, preserving multiplication:

$$
\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h),
\qquad
\rho(e)=I.
$$

Vectors transforming as $$v\mapsto\rho(g)v$$ are steerable features.

For graphs, node features use $$P$$, while vectorized adjacency features use the Kronecker-product representation $$P\otimes P$$:

$$
\operatorname{vec}(PAP^\top)
=(P\otimes P)\operatorname{vec}(A).
$$

These are distinct representations of the same permutation. A pairwise construction such as $$XX^\top$$ transforms in the same two-index way, and hypergraphs introduce still higher-order permutation representations.

### Circular and spherical harmonics

Circular harmonics provide steerable features for planar rotations. For each angular frequency $$k$$, the pair $$(\cos k\theta,\sin k\theta)$$ mixes through a two-dimensional rotation matrix when the input angle changes.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s45-circular-harmonics.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Cosine and sine circular harmonics with increasing angular frequency." caption="Circular harmonics of increasing degree oscillate more rapidly around the circle. The PowerPoint group is exported directly from slides 45–46." %}

The degree controls angular frequency and the corresponding feature transformation. For degree $$k$$,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos k(\theta_0+\theta)\\
\sin k(\theta_0+\theta)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos k\theta&-\sin k\theta\\
\sin k\theta&\cos k\theta
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos k\theta_0\\
\sin k\theta_0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Spherical harmonics $$Y_{\ell m}$$ form an orthonormal basis for functions on the sphere. Degree $$\ell$$ determines the frequency band, and order $$m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell$$ indexes its $$2\ell+1$$ components. In a common complex convention,

$$
Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)
=N_{\ell m}e^{im\phi}P_\ell^m(\cos\theta),
$$

where $$N_{\ell m}$$ is a normalization constant and $$P_\ell^m$$ is an associated Legendre polynomial. These functions also arise as angular solutions of Laplace's equation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s47-spherical-harmonics.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Real spherical harmonics arranged by degree and order." caption="Degree controls angular frequency, while order indexes the components within each degree. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 47." %}

Under a three-dimensional rotation, the degree-$$\ell$$ block transforms through the Wigner $$D^{(\ell)}(g)$$ matrix:

$$
Y^{(\ell)}(g\cdot \hat r)=D^{(\ell)}(g)Y^{(\ell)}(\hat r).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s48-wigner-steerability.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A spherical-harmonic coefficient vector transformed by a Wigner D matrix under rotation." caption="Rotating the argument of a degree-\(\ell\) spherical harmonic mixes its components through \(D^{(\ell)}(g)\). Original PowerPoint diagram from slide 48." %}

Steerable vectors therefore have types. Scalars, ordinary vectors, and higher-order tensor or harmonic features transform through different representations; tensor products can construct higher-order types from lower-order ones.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s49-steerable-types.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Scalar, vector, and matrix features flattened and combined into higher-order tensors." caption="Cartesian tensor products and Clebsch–Gordan tensor products build higher-order steerable types in different bases. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 49." %}

Finally, an operator $$\Phi:X\to Y$$ is equivariant when it commutes with the group actions:

$$
\Phi\big(\rho_X(g)x\big)=\rho_Y(g)\Phi(x).
$$

Invariance is the special case in which $$\rho_Y(g)=I$$. The input and output representations may differ, as they do for molecular coordinates, energies, forces, and tensor properties.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-02/s50-equivariant-operator.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A molecular operator applied before or after permutation and rotation, producing consistent outputs." caption="Equivariance is a commuting relation between the input action, the operator, and the output action. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 50." %}

This completes the source's progression. Graph relabeling first shows why one transformation needs several actions. Group theory then formalizes composition and inverse, actions connect transformations to data, stabilizers and quotients describe homogeneous spaces, and representations specify how features transform. Equivariant neural networks are the operators that preserve this entire structure.
