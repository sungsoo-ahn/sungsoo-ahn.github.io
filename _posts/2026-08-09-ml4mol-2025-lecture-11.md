## The quantum many-body problem

Quantum chemistry begins with the time-independent Schrödinger equation,

$$
\hat H\psi=E\psi.
$$

The equation is short because nearly all of the difficulty is hidden in its unknown. For a system with $$N_e$$ electrons and $$N_n$$ nuclei, the wavefunction accepts every particle coordinate at once,

$$
\psi:
\mathbb R^{3N_e}\times\mathbb R^{3N_n}\longrightarrow\mathbb C.
$$

The Hamiltonian $$\hat H$$ is an operator that maps one such function to another. An eigenfunction is special because the operator returns the same function multiplied by a real energy $$E$$. Once the particle numbers, nuclear charges, and masses are specified, the Hamiltonian is known; the eigenfunction and energy are the quantities to solve for.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s03-image1.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Atomic orbitals surrounding a nucleus, labeled 1s, 2s, 2p, and 3s." caption="Atomic orbitals are one-electron functions, whereas the exact molecular wavefunction depends jointly on every electron coordinate. The embedded graphic credits HowStuffWorks (2001); the exact page is unclear." %}

In atomic units, the nonrelativistic molecular Hamiltonian is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat H={}&
-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{N_e}\nabla_{r_i}^2
+\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{\lVert r_i-r_j\rVert}
-\sum_{i=1}^{N_e}\sum_{A=1}^{N_n}
\frac{Z_A}{\lVert r_i-R_A\rVert}\\
&-\sum_{A=1}^{N_n}\frac{1}{2M_A}\nabla_{R_A}^2
+\sum_{A<B}\frac{Z_AZ_B}{\lVert R_A-R_B\rVert}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The five terms are electronic kinetic energy, electron--electron repulsion, electron--nucleus attraction, nuclear kinetic energy, and nucleus--nucleus repulsion. The two-body repulsion couples electron coordinates. That coupling is what turns a collection of individually simple particles into a many-body problem.

### What the wavefunction contains

The Schrödinger equation has a family of solutions $$\psi_0,\psi_1,\ldots$$ with energies $$E_0,E_1,\ldots$$. The solution with the lowest energy $$E_0$$ is the ground state. The higher-energy solutions are excited states.

The wavefunction contains more than a three-dimensional spatial profile. Its squared magnitude gives a joint probability density over complete configurations, while its sign or phase controls interference and fermionic exchange. A one-electron density is obtained only after integrating out the remaining electron coordinates. For fixed nuclear geometry $$R$$,

$$
\rho(r)
=N_e\int
\left|\psi(r,r_2,\ldots,r_{N_e};R)\right|^2
dr_2\cdots dr_{N_e}.
$$

The factor $$N_e$$ makes $$\int\rho(r)dr=N_e$$. Distinct wavefunctions can have very different sign, phase, and correlation structure while producing similar low-dimensional marginals.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s05-image5.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Hydrogen 1s, 2s, and 2p wavefunctions and their corresponding electron densities." caption="A wavefunction can change sign across a node, but its electron density is nonnegative because it depends on the squared magnitude. Exact visual source unclear." %}

Exact analytic solutions exist only for one-body systems and a few special cases. Practical chemistry needs structured approximations. Wavefunction theory approximates $$\psi$$ directly. Density functional theory replaces the high-dimensional wavefunction with the three-dimensional density $$\rho$$ during the ground-state calculation.

Exact composite source unclear." %}

### Born--Oppenheimer separation

Nuclei are much heavier and move much more slowly than electrons. The Born--Oppenheimer approximation therefore freezes the nuclei while solving the electronic problem,

$$
\hat H_e(R)\psi(r;R)=E(R)\psi(r;R).
$$

The nuclear coordinates $$R$$ are parameters rather than quantum variables in this equation. The nuclear kinetic term is removed, and the nucleus--nucleus repulsion becomes a geometry-dependent constant. Repeating the electronic calculation over nuclear configurations produces the potential-energy surface $$E(R)$$ on which the nuclei move.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s07-image9.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A cartoon comparing a heavy nucleus to a light, rapidly moving electron." caption="The Born--Oppenheimer approximation exploits the mass and time-scale separation between nuclei and electrons. Exact visual source unclear." %}

This separation also explains ordinary ball-and-stick drawings. The nuclei can be displayed as identifiable points because the electronic problem is solved conditional on their positions. The drawing is not the electronic state; it is the framework on which the electronic energy landscape is evaluated.

The rest of the lecture introduces machine learning at three levels. Neural wavefunction methods parameterize $$\psi$$. Learned density-functional methods parameterize the unknown exchange-correlation contribution. Supervised models predict Hamiltonians or density fields produced by earlier electronic-structure calculations.

These choices should not be collapsed into a single task called “learning quantum chemistry.” A wavefunction model must preserve fermionic antisymmetry and is trained through expectation values over electron configurations. An exchange-correlation model must define an energy functional whose derivative remains usable inside a self-consistent field loop. A Hamiltonian predictor must respect the transformation rules of orbital-indexed matrix blocks. A density predictor must represent a scalar field and control its error throughout space. The labels may ultimately come from similar electronic-structure calculations, but the learned objects, constraints, and downstream computations are different.

There is also a difference between replacing an approximation and emulating its output. Neural wavefunctions and learned functionals intervene inside a first-principles variational calculation: changing the model changes the admissible quantum state or energy functional. The supervised methods at the end of the lecture instead learn Hamiltonians or densities already generated by a chosen DFT protocol. They can greatly reduce repeated computational cost, but their target inherits the approximations used to create it. Keeping this distinction visible will matter when interpreting every benchmark that follows.

## Machine learning for wavefunction theory

Wavefunction theory tries to approximate the high-dimensional state directly. Hartree--Fock supplies the baseline construction. Post-Hartree--Fock methods such as coupled-cluster theory and Møller--Plesset perturbation theory recover part of the correlation energy omitted by mean field, often at a steep computational cost.

### Hartree--Fock as a self-consistent mean field

Under the Born--Oppenheimer approximation, the electronic Hamiltonian at fixed geometry is

$$
\hat H_e
=-\frac12\sum_i\nabla_{r_i}^2
-\sum_{i,A}\frac{Z_A}{\lVert r_i-R_A\rVert}
+\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{\lVert r_i-r_j\rVert}
+E_{NN}(R),
$$

where $$E_{NN}(R)$$ is constant during the electronic solve. The last nonconstant term is a two-electron operator. Hartree--Fock replaces its explicit many-body action with one-electron Fock operators,

$$
\hat F_i\phi_i=\varepsilon_i\phi_i.
$$

A first factorized ansatz is the Hartree product,

$$
\psi(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e})
=\phi_1(r_1)\cdots\phi_{N_e}(r_{N_e}).
$$

For a closed-shell system, the resulting Fock operator is commonly written

$$
\hat F_i=\hat h_i+\sum_{j\in\mathrm{occ}}(2\hat J_j-\hat K_j).
$$

The one-electron core operator $$\hat h_i$$ contains kinetic energy and nuclear attraction. The Coulomb operator acts through the average density of orbital $$j$$,

$$
(\hat J_j\phi_i)(r_i)
=\left[
\int\frac{|\phi_j(r_j)|^2}{\lVert r_i-r_j\rVert}dr_j
\right]\phi_i(r_i).
$$

The exchange operator is nonlocal,

$$
(\hat K_j\phi_i)(r_i)
=\phi_j(r_i)
\int\frac{\phi_j^*(r_j)\phi_i(r_j)}{\lVert r_i-r_j\rVert}dr_j.
$$

Exchange has no classical electrostatic analogue. It appears because the electronic wavefunction must be antisymmetric.

The orbitals define $$\hat F$$, while $$\hat F$$ defines the orbitals. Hartree--Fock is therefore a fixed-point problem:

1. choose initial orbitals or a density matrix;
2. construct the Fock operator;
3. solve the one-electron eigenvalue equations;
4. update the orbitals and repeat until self-consistency.

In practice, both orbitals and operators are expanded in a finite basis. The differential equation then becomes a generalized matrix eigenproblem, but the circular dependence remains.

If the spatial orbitals are expanded as $$\phi_i(r)=\sum_\mu C_{\mu i}\chi_\mu(r)$$ in atomic basis functions $$\chi_\mu$$, the self-consistent equation takes the Roothaan--Hall form

$$
FC=SC\varepsilon,
$$

where $$S$$ is the basis-overlap matrix. The coefficients $$C$$ determine the density matrix; the density matrix determines the Coulomb and exchange contributions to $$F$$; diagonalizing the new $$F$$ produces new coefficients. A finite basis therefore discretizes the problem but does not remove self-consistency.

The Hartree--Fock energy is variational within the single-determinant family. Its difference from the exact nonrelativistic energy in the same basis is called the correlation energy. This difference is often small relative to the total electronic energy but decisive for energy differences between geometries, spin states, or reaction products. The challenge shown later in the lecture is therefore not simply to fit the large mean-field contribution. It is to recover a much smaller remainder consistently across configuration space without losing antisymmetry or numerical stability.

### Antisymmetry and the Slater determinant

The Hartree product is not a valid electronic state because electrons are fermions. Exchanging two electron coordinates must flip the sign,

$$
\psi(\ldots,r_i,\ldots,r_j,\ldots)
=-\psi(\ldots,r_j,\ldots,r_i,\ldots).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s14-image10.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated orbitals evaluated at several electron positions and assembled into a determinant wavefunction." caption="Evaluating orbitals at all electron positions produces a matrix whose determinant supplies the required fermionic sign change. Exact animation source unclear." %}

The Slater determinant enforces antisymmetry by construction,

$$
\psi(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e})
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_e!}}
\det\!\begin{bmatrix}
\phi_1(r_1)&\cdots&\phi_{N_e}(r_1)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_1(r_{N_e})&\cdots&\phi_{N_e}(r_{N_e})
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Swapping two electron coordinates swaps two rows of the matrix and changes the determinant sign. If two electrons of the same spin were assigned the same spin orbital, two columns would coincide and the determinant would vanish. The same algebra therefore produces the Pauli exclusion property.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s15-image15.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Allowed and forbidden orbital occupations under the Pauli exclusion principle." caption="Two electrons may share a spatial orbital only with opposite spins; duplicating the same spin orbital is forbidden. Exact visual source unclear." %}

A single determinant captures antisymmetry exactly but treats correlation only through the mean field and exchange. Post-Hartree--Fock expansions add configurations or perturbative corrections. Neural wavefunctions instead enlarge the determinant ingredients themselves.

### FermiNet: configuration-dependent neural orbitals

FermiNet retains determinants but replaces fixed one-electron orbitals with neural orbitals. An orbital associated with electron $$i$$ can depend on all electron positions,

$$
\phi_{i,\theta}
\left(r_i;r_1,\ldots,r_{i-1},r_{i+1},\ldots,r_{N_e},R\right),
$$

while remaining invariant to permutations among the non-$$i$$ electrons of the same spin. The orbital value can therefore adapt to the instantaneous configuration rather than only to an average density.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s16-image16.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated neural network mapping electron positions to a configuration-dependent orbital value." caption="FermiNet replaces fixed orbitals with neural functions of the complete electron configuration. Exact animation source unclear." %}

The final ansatz is a weighted determinant expansion,

$$
\psi_\theta(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e})
=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k
\det\Phi_\theta^{(k)}(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e}).
$$

Every component is antisymmetric, so their weighted sum remains antisymmetric. Increasing $$K$$ and using all-electron features enlarges the variational family. The architecture learns correlation through how each neural orbital changes with the other electron positions.

The dependence must be structured carefully. Electron labels are artificial, so relabeling the spin-up electrons among themselves, or the spin-down electrons among themselves, should permute intermediate electron features without changing the physical content. FermiNet uses permutation-equivariant electron streams built from electron--nucleus and electron--electron displacement features. The final determinant converts that equivariance into the required antisymmetry. Exponential envelopes tied to nuclei impose physically sensible decay far from the molecule, while the neural part learns the nodal and correlation structure that a fixed orbital basis struggles to express.

This construction separates two jobs. Determinants guarantee the sign change under exchange; neural configuration dependence supplies expressivity. Adding ordinary correlation features to a non-antisymmetric output would not repair fermionic statistics, while using a determinant of rigid one-electron orbitals would preserve statistics but retain the mean-field bottleneck. FermiNet combines the two constraints in one variational family.

### Variational Monte Carlo trains the wavefunction without labels

FermiNet does not require a dataset of exact wavefunctions. The variational principle provides the objective. For any admissible trial state,

$$
E_\theta
=\frac{
\int\psi_\theta^*(r)\hat H\psi_\theta(r)dr
}{
\int|\psi_\theta(r)|^2dr
}
\ge E_0.
$$

Define the normalized sampling law $$p_\theta(r)\propto|\psi_\theta(r)|^2$$ and the local energy

$$
E_L(r)=\frac{\hat H\psi_\theta(r)}{\psi_\theta(r)}.
$$

The variational energy becomes

$$
E_\theta=\mathbb E_{r\sim p_\theta}[E_L(r)].
$$

Monte Carlo sampling makes the high-dimensional integral tractable without placing the electrons on a full grid. For a real wavefunction, a score-function or REINFORCE-style derivative gives

$$
\nabla_\theta E_\theta
=2\,\mathbb E_{p_\theta}\!\left[
(E_L(r)-E_\theta)
\nabla_\theta\log|\psi_\theta(r)|
\right].
$$

Subtracting $$E_\theta$$ acts as a baseline and reduces variance. Training alternates between sampling electron configurations from the current state and updating the state with noisy energy gradients. The samples, local energies, and sampling distribution all change with $$\theta$$.

The variational upper bound gives the energy a special status: lowering an accurately estimated expectation value improves the trial state within the chosen ansatz, even when no reference wavefunction is available. The local energy also provides a diagnostic. For an exact eigenstate it is constant wherever the wavefunction is nonzero, because $$\hat H\psi/\psi=E$$. Variation of $$E_L(r)$$ across samples therefore reveals remaining wavefunction error, although finite Monte Carlo samples and optimization noise make that signal imperfect.

Sampling is not a side detail. Electron configurations are commonly advanced with Markov-chain Monte Carlo, so successive samples are correlated and may explore separated modes slowly. Near a node, dividing by a small $$\psi_\theta(r)$$ can create large local-energy fluctuations. The kinetic-energy term requires spatial derivatives of the network, making each sample substantially more expensive than an ordinary supervised example. VMC avoids a full grid in $$3N_e$$ dimensions, but it replaces grid cost with repeated sampling, network differentiation, and variance control.

FermiNet captures much of the correlation energy absent from mean-field theory across atoms and molecules.

The deck then returns to the higher solutions of the Schrödinger equation. Ground-state VMC minimizes one Rayleigh quotient. Excited-state computation must optimize several states without collapsing them all onto the ground state. The same FermiNet and PsiFormer ansatzes can then represent the individual states. The changed object is the training objective, not the antisymmetry machinery. Reported applications include vertical excitation energies and oscillator strengths for molecules large enough to exhibit double excitations and benzene-scale electronic structure.

Those observables test more than energy ordering. A vertical excitation holds the nuclear geometry fixed, whereas an adiabatic excitation compares states at their relaxed geometries. Oscillator strengths depend on transition matrix elements, not only eigenvalue differences. Conical intersections require several potential-energy surfaces to approach one another with the correct local structure. A useful excited-state method must therefore represent several states coherently enough to reproduce both their energies and the quantities coupling them.

## Learning the universal functional in density functional theory

Wavefunction theory keeps the full many-electron state. Density functional theory changes the central variable. The Hohenberg--Kohn existence theorem states that, for interacting particles in an external potential, the ground-state density uniquely determines that potential up to an additive constant. Ground-state observables are therefore functionals of $$\rho$$.

The variational theorem adds an optimization statement. There exists a universal functional $$F[\rho]$$ such that

$$
E_v[\rho]
=F[\rho]+\int v_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)\rho(r)dr,
$$

and the exact ground-state density minimizes $$E_v[\rho]$$ among admissible densities with the correct particle number. These theorems establish that a density-only formulation exists. They do not provide the universal functional in a computable form.

The word *universal* has a precise scope. The interacting kinetic and electron--electron terms collected in $$F[\rho]$$ do not depend on the particular external nuclear potential. Different molecules enter through $$v_{\mathrm{ext}}$$ and through the density that minimizes the total energy. In principle, one exact functional could therefore be reused across systems. In practice, the unknown map must act on a broad space of physically admissible densities, and an approximation can behave well near its training densities while failing when self-consistency moves it elsewhere.

### Kohn--Sham orbitals isolate the unknown remainder

Kohn and Sham introduce a fictitious noninteracting system whose ground-state density matches that of the interacting electrons. Its orbitals satisfy

$$
\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}[\rho]\phi_i(r)
=\varepsilon_i\phi_i(r),
\qquad
\rho(r)=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{occ}}|\phi_i(r)|^2.
$$

The orbitals are mathematical devices for constructing the density. They are not the interacting many-electron wavefunction.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s25-image31.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Kohn-Sham correspondence between an interacting electronic problem and a noninteracting effective-potential problem." caption="Kohn--Sham DFT replaces the interacting solve with noninteracting orbitals constrained to reproduce the same density. Exact visual source unclear." %}

The Kohn--Sham Hamiltonian separates known terms from one residual,

$$
\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}[\rho]
=-\frac12\nabla^2
+v_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)
+v_H[\rho](r)
+v_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho](r).
$$

The nuclear external potential and classical Hartree potential are

$$
v_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)
=-\sum_A\frac{Z_A}{\lVert r-R_A\rVert},
\qquad
v_H[\rho](r)
=\int\frac{\rho(r')}{\lVert r-r'\rVert}dr'.
$$

The exchange-correlation potential $$v_{\mathrm{xc}}$$ absorbs the interacting physics missing from the fictitious noninteracting construction, including exchange and correlation and the difference between the exact and noninteracting kinetic energies.

After solving the orbital equations, the total energy is written

$$
E[\rho]
=T_s[\rho]+E_H[\rho]+E_{\mathrm{ext}}[\rho]+E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho],
$$

with

$$
v_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho](r)
=\frac{\delta E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho]}{\delta\rho(r)}.
$$

Kohn--Sham DFT is exact if the exact exchange-correlation functional is known. Practical error enters through approximating that functional.

More explicitly, the universal functional is decomposed as

$$
F[\rho]=T_s[\rho]+E_H[\rho]+E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho].
$$

The noninteracting kinetic term $$T_s$$ is evaluated through the Kohn--Sham orbitals, and the classical Coulomb contribution $$E_H$$ is known explicitly from the density. Everything left over is assigned to $$E_{\mathrm{xc}}$$. Its name can be misleadingly narrow: it contains not only exchange and electron correlation, but also the difference between the true interacting kinetic energy and $$T_s$$. Learning this remainder is attractive because much of the physics has already been isolated, but the remainder must be differentiated with respect to a function and used repeatedly during SCF.

### Self-consistency returns

The density defines the Kohn--Sham Hamiltonian, the Hamiltonian defines the orbitals, and the occupied orbitals define a new density. A self-consistent field calculation iterates

$$
\rho^{(k)}
\longrightarrow
\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}[\rho^{(k)}]
\longrightarrow
\{\phi_i^{(k)}\}
\longrightarrow
\rho_{\mathrm{out}}^{(k)}(r)
=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{occ}}|\phi_i^{(k)}(r)|^2.
$$

The output density is mixed with the previous iterate to form $$\rho^{(k+1)}$$, and the process stops when the density, energy, or residual converges.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s28-group37.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Kohn-Sham self-consistent loop from a molecule and density guess through orbitals to an updated density." caption="A Kohn--Sham calculation is a fixed-point iteration because its density is both an input to and an output of the Hamiltonian. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

This loop changes how a learned functional should be judged. Evaluating a model once on reference densities measures only an off-line error. During self-consistency, a functional changes the potential and orbitals, which changes the next density presented to the same functional. Stable convergence and self-consistent observables are separate tests from pointwise accuracy on fixed inputs.

### Jacob's ladder organizes the functional inputs

Exchange-correlation approximations are often arranged on Jacob's ladder. Each rung exposes more information to the functional and usually increases cost.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s29-image53.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Jacob's ladder from Hartree-Fock theory through LDA, GGA, meta-GGA, hybrid GGA, and fully nonlocal functionals." caption="Jacob's ladder orders density-functional approximations by the information they use and the accuracy-cost regime they target. Exact visual source unclear." %}

| Family | Input features | Schematic form | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| LDA | $$\rho$$ | $$\int \epsilon_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\mathrm{LDA}}(\rho(r))\rho(r)dr$$ | LDA, LSDA |
| GGA | $$\rho,\nabla\rho$$ | $$\int \epsilon_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\mathrm{GGA}}(\rho,\nabla\rho)\rho(r)dr$$ | PBE, BLYP |
| meta-GGA | $$\rho,\nabla\rho,\tau$$ | $$\int \epsilon_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\mathrm{MGGA}}(\rho,\nabla\rho,\tau)\rho(r)dr$$ | SCAN, r2SCAN |
| hybrid | semilocal features plus exact exchange | $$\alpha E_x^{\mathrm{HF}}+(1-\alpha)E_x^{\mathrm{GGA}}+E_c^{\mathrm{GGA}}$$ | B3LYP, PBE0, HSE |

LDA evaluates a local density. GGA adds its gradient. A meta-GGA adds quantities such as kinetic-energy density $$\tau$$. Hybrid functionals mix in nonlocal Hartree--Fock exchange. The rows define different functional contracts, not merely neural networks of increasing size.

### Skala learns the exchange-correlation functional

Skala asks whether the unknown exchange-correlation contribution can be learned from accurate wavefunction calculations. Its inputs begin with density-derived features, and its output contributes an energy inside the Kohn--Sham calculation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s30-image55.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Skala pipeline from electron-density features through communicating grid points to exchange-correlation energy." caption="Skala learns an exchange-correlation contribution from density features, using wavefunction-theory energies as supervision. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14665'>Luise et al. (2025), Accurate and scalable exchange-correlation with deep learning</a>." %}

The parameterization shown in the lecture starts from a spin-resolved exchange-like form,

$$
E_{\mathrm{xc}}^\theta[\rho]
=-\frac34\left(\frac6\pi\right)^{1/3}
\int
\left[
\rho_\uparrow(r)^{4/3}+\rho_\downarrow(r)^{4/3}
\right]
f_\theta[x[\rho](r)]dr,
$$

with meta-GGA-style local features

$$

The architecture augments these local descriptors with communication among integration-grid points before producing and integrating an energy density.

The prefactor recovers an exchange-like density scaling, while $$f_\theta$$ learns a dimensionless enhancement from the local and nonlocal context. The spin-resolved $$\rho^{4/3}$$ terms retain a familiar density-functional scaffold rather than asking an unconstrained network to emit a total molecular energy. Grid weights then approximate the spatial integral. Communication between grid representations moves the model beyond an entirely pointwise semilocal rule, yet the prediction still enters the calculation as an exchange-correlation energy functional.

Training targets must be more accurate than the functional being learned. The lecture uses high-level wavefunction-theory references, including CCSD(T) near the complete-basis-set limit. The self-consistent procedure is part of training: run SCF with the current functional, expose the model to the densities it induces, and optimize against reference energies.

This creates two related training regimes. An initial fit on reference densities teaches the energy map in a controlled part of density space. Self-consistent fine-tuning then closes the loop: the current functional generates a potential, the potential changes the orbitals and density, and the resulting energy error updates the same functional. The latter is more expensive and harder to differentiate through, but it addresses distribution shift caused by the model itself. A functional accurate only on densities supplied by another method may be unstable or biased when asked to generate its own.

## Supervised Hamiltonians and electron-density fields

The final part of the lecture changes the learning problem. Instead of placing a model inside the definition of an exchange-correlation functional, it learns outputs generated by DFT calculations. A Hamiltonian matrix, a pointwise density query, and a complete density basis expansion are different targets. Each leaves a different downstream computation to perform.

### QHNet predicts an equivariant Hamiltonian

QHNet constructs datasets of DFT-computed Hamiltonian matrices and learns a map from molecular geometry to the matrix. The output is not a scalar invariant. Blocks of the Hamiltonian are indexed by atomic orbitals and must transform consistently when the molecule rotates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s37-image63.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="QHNet pipeline connecting molecular geometry, DFT Hamiltonians, a quantum tensor network, and downstream orbital properties." caption="QHNet predicts the structured Hamiltonian produced by a DFT calculation and evaluates matrix, eigenvalue, acceleration, and orbital-similarity errors. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04922'>Yu et al. (2023), Efficient and Equivariant Graph Networks for Predicting Quantum Hamiltonian</a>." %}

The predicted matrix can be diagonalized to obtain orbital energies and wavefunctions, or it can initialize a later SCF calculation. The symmetry of the network protects the coordinate transformation law of the matrix. It does not by itself guarantee that diagonalization or self-consistency will reproduce every observable.

This distinction explains the several error columns in the lecture figure. A small elementwise matrix error is useful but indirect. Eigenvalue error tests the spectrum after diagonalization; orbital similarity tests the eigenvectors, with the usual complications from phases and degenerate subspaces; SCF acceleration tests whether the prediction is a useful starting operator. Because eigendecomposition is nonlinear, similar matrix errors can yield different spectral or orbital errors. The downstream task should determine which metric matters.

### QHFlow learns a distribution over Hamiltonians

QHFlow replaces deterministic regression with equivariant flow matching conditioned on molecular geometry. The lecture shows two structured priors: a tensor-expansion-based invariant prior and a Gaussian orthogonal ensemble prior. A learned velocity transports the initial matrix $$H_0$$ to a target-like Hamiltonian $$H_1$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s38-image66.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="QHFlow transport from structured Hamiltonian priors to a target Hamiltonian, density, and frontier orbital energies." caption="QHFlow transports structured matrix priors toward geometry-conditioned Hamiltonians, then tests the result through density, orbital energies, and SCF acceleration. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18817'>Kim et al. (2025), High-order Equivariant Flow Matching for Density Functional Theory Hamiltonian Prediction</a>." %}

The predicted Hamiltonian defines a density and frontier orbital energies after eigendecomposition. QHFlow then feeds the matrix to an SCF solver. The acceleration claim is therefore operational: a useful prediction reduces SCF iterations or runtime without changing the converged solution quality.

Predicting a distribution is motivated by the possibility that one geometry may admit structured uncertainty rather than a single point estimate under finite data and model error. The prior must itself obey the matrix symmetries and orbital transformation structure; the equivariant flow then preserves the correct representation while transporting samples. Once a sample is passed into SCF, however, the physical solver remains the final arbiter. Flow matching changes how the initial Hamiltonian is proposed, not the fixed point that defines a converged calculation.

### DeepDFT queries density at a coordinate

DeepDFT predicts the electron density directly. A graph contains atom vertices and special probe vertices at requested coordinates. Atom vertices exchange messages with one another, while a probe receives messages from nearby atoms and returns $$\rho(r)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s39-image68.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="DeepDFT message passing between atom and probe nodes with density examples for a cathode material and liquid electrolyte." caption="DeepDFT treats density as a coordinate-query function: atom nodes exchange messages, while probe nodes receive local information and output charge density. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.03346'>Jørgensen & Bhowmik (2020), DeepDFT</a>." %}

This is an implicit neural representation conditioned on the molecular or material structure. It can be evaluated at arbitrary locations, but reconstructing a dense three-dimensional field requires many probe queries. The learned object is a function evaluator, not an explicit global field representation.

Probe nodes are deliberately one-way readers in the depicted architecture: atoms build a representation of the chemical environment, and a query location receives information needed to evaluate the field. Queries do not need to modify the atomic state or communicate with one another. This makes arbitrary-resolution evaluation natural and permits focusing samples near chemically important regions. It also means that a large regular grid repeats the probe computation many times, motivating the global-basis approaches that follow.

### GPWNO predicts a global basis expansion

GPWNO addresses repeated-query cost with a neural-operator representation. It combines plane waves, which describe smooth global and periodic structure, with Gaussian-type orbitals, which resolve localized high-frequency density near nuclei.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s40-image70.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Plane-wave and Gaussian-type-orbital bases combined with atomic and probe features for density prediction." caption="Plane waves represent global variation, while Gaussian-type orbitals concentrate resolution near nuclei. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04278'>Kim & Ahn (2024), Gaussian Plane-Wave Neural Operator for Electron Density Estimation</a>." %}

The plane-wave neural-operator layer alternates Fourier-domain filtering, inverse transforms, pointwise nonlinearities, and repeated operator blocks before producing a field coefficient representation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s40-image72.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="GPWNO layer with Fourier transforms, learned spectral filtering, pointwise nonlinearities, and repeated operator blocks." caption="GPWNO predicts a global field representation, so evaluating density does not require a fresh graph-network pass at every coordinate. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04278'>Kim & Ahn (2024)</a>." %}

The basis changes the computational interface. DeepDFT pays for flexible pointwise queries. GPWNO pays once to predict a global expansion and then evaluates that expansion cheaply. Approximation error can now arise from both the learned coefficients and the basis resolution.

The mixed basis addresses a characteristic imbalance in electron density. Valence and interstitial density can vary smoothly across a cell, which suits plane waves. Core-region density changes rapidly near nuclei, where a uniform Fourier representation would require many high-frequency modes. Localized Gaussian functions concentrate representational capacity at those singularly important regions. The model thus builds the expected spatial scales into the output representation instead of demanding that one homogeneous basis resolve them all equally.

### Gaussian orbitals at atoms and bonds

The closing method makes the density basis more explicitly chemical. It places spherical Gaussian fields at atomic centers and at virtual centers such as bond midpoints, learns expressive basis functions, and predicts their coefficients with a high-capacity equivariant network.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s41-image74.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Charge density assembled from Gaussian orbitals placed at atoms and virtual bond centers." caption="Atom-centered and virtual bond-centered Gaussian fields provide a compact basis for localized and bonding density. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19276'>Fu et al. (2024), A Recipe for Charge Density Prediction</a>." %}

Atom-centered functions capture core and local valence density. Bond-centered functions place capacity where shared electron density accumulates. The paper reports higher accuracy than earlier density predictors while running more than an order of magnitude faster, with a controllable accuracy-efficiency tradeoff through model and basis size.

The sequence of methods is a hierarchy of learned scientific objects. FermiNet and PsiFormer predict a state that still requires Monte Carlo expectation estimation. Skala predicts a functional whose derivative enters a self-consistent Kohn--Sham solve. QHNet and QHFlow predict an operator that must be diagonalized or refined by SCF. DeepDFT predicts pointwise values of a field. GPWNO and Gaussian-orbital models predict basis representations from which the field is reconstructed.

Machine learning does not remove the electronic-structure calculation in one uniform way. It chooses an object, preserves the constraints attached to that object, and hands the result to a different downstream computation. Antisymmetry, equivariance, self-consistency, basis resolution, and variational correctness belong to different interfaces; satisfying one does not silently guarantee the others.
