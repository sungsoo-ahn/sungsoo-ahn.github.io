## The quantum many-body problem

Quantum chemistry starts from a compact equation whose unknown is anything but compact:

$$
\hat H\psi = E\psi.
$$

For a system with electrons at positions $$r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e}$$ and nuclei at positions $$R_1,\ldots,R_{N_n}$$, the Hamiltonian contains five physical contributions:

$$
\hat H
=-
\sum_i \frac{1}{2}\nabla_{r_i}^2
+\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{\lVert r_i-r_j\rVert}
-\sum_{i,A}\frac{Z_A}{\lVert r_i-R_A\rVert}
-\sum_A\frac{1}{2M_A}\nabla_{R_A}^2
+\sum_{A<B}\frac{Z_AZ_B}{\lVert R_A-R_B\rVert}.
$$

The terms are, in order, electronic kinetic energy, electron--electron repulsion, electron--nucleus attraction, nuclear kinetic energy, and nucleus--nucleus repulsion. The Hamiltonian is known once the particles and charges are specified. The wavefunction $$\psi$$ and its energy $$E$$ are not.

### What the wavefunction contains

The wavefunction assigns a complex amplitude to every joint configuration of all particles. Its squared magnitude determines probabilities, and its sign or phase carries interference and fermionic information. The Schrödinger equation has many solutions $$\psi_0,\psi_1,\ldots$$ with corresponding energies $$E_0,E_1,\ldots$$. The lowest-energy solution is the ground state; higher solutions are excited states.

Even an ordinary three-dimensional electron density is already a marginal of this much larger object. Suppressing spin for readability,

$$
\rho(r)=N_e\int |\psi(r,r_2,\ldots,r_{N_e};R)|^2\,dr_2\cdots dr_{N_e}.
$$

This distinction drives the entire lecture. Wavefunction theory tries to approximate the high-dimensional object directly. Density functional theory replaces it, for ground-state calculations, with a three-dimensional density that must still encode the effects of the interacting electrons.

Exact analytic solutions are available only for very small one-body cases. Electron--electron repulsion couples the coordinates, so a naive representation grows exponentially with particle number. The practical "cures of dimensionality" are therefore structured approximations: WFT constrains the wavefunction family, whereas DFT shifts the central unknown to the electron density.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s06-image7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Molecular Hamiltonian decomposed into electronic and nuclear kinetic and interaction terms." caption="The molecular Hamiltonian makes the source of the many-body coupling explicit. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 6." %}

### Born--Oppenheimer separation

Nuclei are much heavier and move much more slowly than electrons. The Born--Oppenheimer approximation freezes the nuclear positions while solving the electronic problem:

$$
\hat H_{\mathrm e}(R)\psi(r;R)=E(R)\psi(r;R).
$$

For each fixed nuclear framework $$R$$, the electronic calculation returns an energy $$E(R)$$. Repeating that calculation over nuclear configurations produces the potential-energy surface on which the nuclei move. This is what makes the familiar ball-and-stick representation useful: the nuclei can be treated as identifiable points while electronic structure determines the landscape underneath them.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s07-image8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Potential-energy curves associated with different fixed nuclear arrangements." caption="Born--Oppenheimer separation turns repeated electronic calculations at fixed nuclei into a potential-energy landscape for nuclear motion. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 7." %}

Machine learning enters this picture in two distinct places. In WFT, a neural network parameterizes the wavefunction itself. In DFT and related supervised approaches, a network parameterizes an exchange-correlation functional, a Kohn--Sham Hamiltonian, or the electron density. The targets are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## Machine learning for wavefunction theory

Wavefunction methods approximate $$\psi$$ directly. Hartree--Fock theory supplies the reference construction; post-Hartree--Fock methods such as coupled cluster and Møller--Plesset perturbation theory then recover correlation energy missing from the mean-field approximation. Their accuracy can be high, but their cost grows steeply with system size.

### From the many-electron Hamiltonian to Hartree--Fock

Under the Born--Oppenheimer approximation, the nuclear kinetic term disappears and the nuclear repulsion is constant for a fixed geometry. The difficult piece is still the two-electron repulsion. Hartree--Fock replaces the interacting problem by one-electron equations in which every electron feels an average field generated by the others:

$$
\hat F_i\phi_i=\varepsilon_i\phi_i.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s11-image10.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Born-Oppenheimer electronic Hamiltonian with the two-electron interaction replaced by one-electron Fock operators." caption="Hartree--Fock replaces the explicit two-electron potential with self-consistent one-electron Fock operators. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 11." %}

A first attempt is the Hartree product

$$
\psi(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e})=\phi_1(r_1)\cdots\phi_{N_e}(r_{N_e}).
$$

With this factorization, the Fock operator contains a one-electron core Hamiltonian together with Coulomb and exchange terms computed from the occupied orbitals. In a closed-shell notation, one commonly writes

$$
\hat F_i=\hat h_i+\sum_j\left(2\hat J_j-\hat K_j\right).
$$

The Coulomb operator $$\hat J_j$$ is the classical average repulsion generated by orbital $$j$$. The exchange operator $$\hat K_j$$ is nonlocal and arises from antisymmetry rather than from a classical charge density.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s12-image11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Hartree-product wavefunction and Coulomb and exchange contributions to the Fock operator." caption="The mean-field construction converts the interacting equation into orbital-dependent Coulomb and exchange operators. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 12." %}

The orbitals determine the Fock operator, yet the Fock operator determines the orbitals. Hartree--Fock is therefore solved by a self-consistent field procedure:

1. choose an initial set of orbitals;
2. construct the Fock operator from them;
3. solve the resulting one-electron eigenvalue problem;
4. update the orbitals and repeat until the input and output agree.

In practical electronic-structure codes, orbitals and operators are expanded in a finite basis, turning the update into a generalized matrix eigenvalue problem.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s13-image12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Self-consistent Hartree-Fock loop between orbitals and the Fock operator." caption="Hartree--Fock closes the circular dependence between orbitals and their mean field by fixed-point iteration. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 13." %}

### Antisymmetry and Slater determinants

The Hartree product has a fatal defect for electrons: it does not obey the Pauli antisymmetry principle. Exchanging two electron coordinates must reverse the sign of the wavefunction,

$$
\psi(\ldots,r_i,\ldots,r_j,\ldots)
=-\psi(\ldots,r_j,\ldots,r_i,\ldots).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s14-image13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Antisymmetric wavefunctions before and after exchanging two electron coordinates." caption="Fermionic exchange changes the sign of the wavefunction, a constraint the simple Hartree product cannot satisfy. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 14." %}

The Slater determinant enforces this property by construction:

$$
\psi(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e})
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_e!}}
\det\!\begin{bmatrix}
\phi_1(r_1)&\cdots&\phi_{N_e}(r_1)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_1(r_{N_e})&\cdots&\phi_{N_e}(r_{N_e})
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Swapping two electrons swaps two rows and flips the determinant sign. If two occupied one-electron states coincide, two columns coincide and the determinant vanishes, giving the exclusion property.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s15-image14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Slater-determinant parameterization of an antisymmetric many-electron wavefunction." caption="A determinant turns one-electron orbitals into an antisymmetric many-electron state. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 15." %}

### FermiNet: neural orbitals inside determinants

FermiNet keeps the determinant logic but makes the orbitals expressive neural functions. Instead of assigning orbital $$i$$ only the coordinate $$r_i$$, the neural orbital can depend on every electron coordinate while remaining permutation-invariant to the non-$$i$$ electrons. This lets the representation respond to instantaneous electron configurations rather than only an average field.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s16-image16.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="FermiNet architecture for producing neural orbitals and determinant wavefunctions from electron and nuclear coordinates." caption="FermiNet learns configuration-dependent orbitals and combines them through determinants to preserve antisymmetry. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 16." %}

The final state is a weighted sum of determinants,

$$
\psi_\theta(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e})
=\sum_k w_k\det \Phi_\theta^{(k)}(r_1,\ldots,r_{N_e}),
$$

where each determinant provides an antisymmetric component. Multiple determinants enlarge the variational family and recover correlation that a single mean-field determinant misses.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s17-image19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Practical FermiNet construction with permutation-aware neural orbitals and multiple determinant channels." caption="The practical FermiNet ansatz combines all-electron neural features with a weighted determinant expansion. ### Variational Monte Carlo

FermiNet is trained through the variational principle. For any admissible trial state, its Rayleigh quotient upper-bounds the exact ground-state energy:

$$
E_\theta
=\frac{\int \psi_\theta^*(r)\hat H\psi_\theta(r)\,dr}
{\int |\psi_\theta(r)|^2\,dr}
\ge E_0.
$$

Define the normalized sampling density $$p_\theta(r)\propto |\psi_\theta(r)|^2$$ and the local energy

$$
E_{\mathrm L}(r)=\frac{\hat H\psi_\theta(r)}{\psi_\theta(r)}.
$$

Then $$E_\theta=\mathbb E_{r\sim p_\theta}[E_{\mathrm L}(r)]$$. Variational Monte Carlo samples electron configurations, evaluates local energies, and uses stochastic gradients to reduce this expectation. The optimization is difficult because both the samples and their local energies depend on the current wavefunction.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s18-image20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Variational Monte Carlo objective and sampling procedure for a neural wavefunction." caption="VMC turns the variational energy into an expectation over configurations sampled from the neural wavefunction itself. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 18." %}

FermiNet substantially improves over earlier neural VMC ansatzes and captures much of the correlation energy missing from Hartree--Fock.

## Learning the universal functional in density functional theory

Wavefunction theory works with a function of all electron coordinates. Density functional theory starts from the Hohenberg--Kohn result: the ground-state density uniquely determines the external potential, and therefore all ground-state observables. In particular, the ground-state energy can be written as a functional $$E[\rho]$$ of a three-dimensional field.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s24-image29.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Many-body electronic problem mapped to a three-dimensional electron density in density functional theory." caption="DFT replaces the many-electron wavefunction with the ground-state electron density as the central variable. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 24." %}

This theorem establishes existence, not a usable formula. Kohn--Sham DFT provides the practical construction.

### Kohn--Sham orbitals and the exchange-correlation remainder

Kohn and Sham introduce a fictitious noninteracting electron system with the same ground-state density as the interacting system. Its orbitals satisfy

$$
\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}[\rho]\phi_i=\varepsilon_i\phi_i,
\qquad
\rho(r)=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{occ}}|\phi_i(r)|^2.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/ks-correspondence.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Kohn-Sham construction mapping an interacting many-electron problem to noninteracting orbitals with the same density." caption="Kohn--Sham DFT reintroduces orbitals as mathematical devices for producing the interacting system's density. Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 25." %}

The Kohn--Sham Hamiltonian is

$$
\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}[\rho]
=-\frac12\nabla^2+v_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)+v_{\mathrm H}[\rho](r)+v_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho](r).
$$

The external potential comes from the nuclei, and the Hartree potential is the classical electrostatic contribution

$$
v_{\mathrm H}[\rho](r)=\int\frac{\rho(r')}{\lVert r-r'\rVert}\,dr'.
$$

Everything not captured by these known pieces is bundled into the exchange-correlation potential $$v_{\mathrm{xc}}$$. The Kohn--Sham orbitals themselves are not the interacting many-electron wavefunction; their purpose is to reproduce the density.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/ks-xc-functional.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Known Kohn-Sham kinetic, external, and Hartree terms together with the unknown exchange-correlation potential." caption="The exchange-correlation term is the residual that must account for the interacting physics missing from the fictitious noninteracting construction. Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 26." %}

After solving the Kohn--Sham equations, the total energy is decomposed as

$$
E[\rho]=T_s[\rho]+E_{\mathrm{ext}}[\rho]+E_{\mathrm H}[\rho]+E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho],
$$

with

$$
v_{\mathrm{xc}}(r)=\frac{\delta E_{\mathrm{xc}}[\rho]}{\delta\rho(r)}.
$$

The quality of Kohn--Sham DFT therefore depends decisively on the approximation to $$E_{\mathrm{xc}}$$.

### Self-consistency returns

Kohn--Sham DFT has the same circular structure encountered in Hartree--Fock. The density defines the Hamiltonian, the Hamiltonian defines the orbitals, and the orbitals define a new density. A self-consistent calculation proceeds as follows:

1. initialize $$\rho$$;
2. construct $$\hat H_{\mathrm{KS}}[\rho]$$ and solve for occupied orbitals;
3. form $$\rho_{\mathrm{new}}(r)=\sum_i|\phi_i(r)|^2$$;
4. mix or replace the density and repeat until convergence.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/ks-scf-loop.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Self-consistent Kohn-Sham loop from molecule and initial density through orbitals to an updated converged density." caption="Kohn--Sham calculation is a fixed-point procedure because the density is both input to and output from the Hamiltonian. Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 28." %}

This loop matters for machine learning. A learned functional is not merely evaluated once on a frozen reference density. Its errors alter the Hamiltonian, which alters the orbitals, which changes the next density presented to the model.

### Skala: a data-driven exchange-correlation functional

Traditional functionals are often organized as Jacob's ladder. Local-density approximations use $$\rho$$; generalized-gradient approximations add $$\nabla\rho$$; meta-GGAs add ingredients such as kinetic-energy density $$\tau$$; hybrids incorporate exact exchange; higher rungs include more nonlocal information.

Skala asks whether a neural network can learn the exchange-correlation functional itself. The model takes density-derived features and returns an energy contribution. Its targets come from accurate wavefunction calculations, so the learned functional transfers expensive many-body information into a Kohn--Sham computation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s30-image55.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Skala pipeline training a neural exchange-correlation functional against high-level wavefunction-theory energies." caption="Skala learns the XC contribution from density features while using accurate wavefunction calculations as supervision. Its local feature vector follows a meta-GGA-style parameterization,

$$
x[\rho](r)=\big(\rho(r),|\nabla\rho(r)|,\tau(r)\big),
$$

and the architecture converts these local descriptors into an exchange-correlation energy density.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/skala-parameterization.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Skala meta-GGA feature construction and neural exchange-correlation parameterization." caption="Skala retains the feature hierarchy of a meta-GGA while replacing the hand-designed functional form with a learned map. Training requires ground-truth solutions accurate enough to improve upon ordinary DFT, such as CCSD(T) near the complete-basis-set limit. The model is trained through the SCF procedure: run self-consistency with the current functional, compare the resulting energy with the wavefunction-theory target, and differentiate the error through the electronic-structure calculation.

## Supervised Hamiltonians and electron densities

The final part of the lecture changes the problem once more. Rather than embedding a learned functional inside a full quantum calculation, supervised models learn outputs produced by previous DFT calculations. The predicted object may be a Hamiltonian matrix, a pointwise density, or coefficients of a density basis. Each choice changes both the symmetry constraints and how the prediction accelerates downstream computation.

### QHNet: predicting the Hamiltonian

QHNet introduces a dataset of DFT-computed Hamiltonians and trains an equivariant neural network to predict the Hamiltonian matrix from molecular geometry. Matrix blocks transform according to the atomic orbital basis attached to each atom; predicting scalar energies alone would not preserve this richer transformation law.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s37-image64.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="QHNet architecture predicting equivariant atomic-orbital Hamiltonian blocks from a molecular structure." caption="QHNet predicts the structured Hamiltonian output of a DFT calculation rather than only a scalar molecular property. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 37." %}

The output can be diagonalized to obtain orbitals and orbital energies, or used as an informed starting point for later electronic-structure calculations.

### QHFlow: a distribution over Hamiltonians

QHFlow replaces direct regression with an equivariant flow-matching model. The motivation is that Hamiltonians possess structured, correlated variation that a generative model can learn. A sampled or predicted Hamiltonian is then used to initialize an SCF solver, turning the learned model into an acceleration mechanism rather than the final physical authority.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s38-image66.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="QHFlow equivariant flow-matching model for quantum Hamiltonians and SCF initialization." caption="QHFlow learns Hamiltonian structure generatively and evaluates its utility through faster self-consistent convergence. ### DeepDFT: querying density at a coordinate

DeepDFT predicts the electron density directly. Given a molecule and a query coordinate $$r$$, the model returns $$\rho(r)$$. This resembles an implicit neural representation: the continuous field is represented by a network that can be evaluated at arbitrary spatial points.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s39-image68.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="DeepDFT message-passing architecture and examples of predicted three-dimensional electron densities." caption="DeepDFT represents electron density as a coordinate-query function conditioned on the molecular structure. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol quantum chemistry lecture, slide 39." %}

The interface is flexible, but dense volumetric evaluation can require a very large number of network queries.

### GPWNO: predicting a density field through basis coefficients

GPWNO takes a neural-operator route. Instead of evaluating an implicit model independently at every grid point, it predicts a linear combination of basis functions for the full density field.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-11/s40-image70.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Electron-density field represented around a molecule by atom-centered features." caption="A field model must connect discrete atomic geometry to a continuous three-dimensional density. The basis representation amortizes spatial queries, but the quality of the density now depends on whether the chosen basis can resolve chemically important features.

### A recipe based on Gaussian orbitals

The final model makes that basis choice more chemical. Gaussian-type orbitals are placed at atom centers and bond centers, and the network predicts their coefficients. Atom-centered functions capture core and local valence density; bond-centered functions provide a direct way to represent density accumulated between bonded atoms.

The lecture therefore ends where it began: representation determines tractability. Hartree--Fock uses orbitals and determinants to constrain a many-electron wavefunction. FermiNet learns those ingredients while retaining antisymmetry. Kohn--Sham DFT replaces the interacting state with a density and an unknown exchange-correlation remainder. Skala learns that remainder inside self-consistency. QHNet, QHFlow, DeepDFT, GPWNO, and Gaussian-basis models instead learn different outputs of electronic-structure calculations. Machine learning does not erase the quantum problem; it chooses which quantum object to approximate, which constraints to preserve, and which computation should remain downstream.
