Diffusion models and flow matching appear to define generation in opposite ways. A diffusion model adds noise with a stochastic differential equation and then learns how to reverse it. Flow matching chooses a deterministic probability path from noise to data and learns its velocity. At the level of density evolution, however, the two constructions use the same move: define a conditional process whose local target is known, then average that target over the unknown posterior to obtain the marginal field needed for generation.

The stochastic half of the story begins with the Fokker--Planck equation. It takes us from an SDE for individual trajectories to a PDE for their density, identifies the score correction in a reverse-time SDE, and leads to denoising score matching. The deterministic half replaces Fokker--Planck with the continuity equation. It separates conditional and marginal probability paths, derives the marginalization trick, and leads to conditional flow matching. Keeping those pairs of objects separate is the main work.

## Density evolution and Langevin dynamics

Consider the one-dimensional Itô SDE

$$
dX_t=f(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(t)\,dW_t.
$$

The drift $$f$$ moves probability through space. The diffusion coefficient $$\sigma$$ spreads it. If $$p_t(x)$$ denotes the density of $$X_t$$, the Fokker--Planck--Kolmogorov equation is

$$
\frac{\partial p_t(x)}{\partial t}
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\left[f(x,t)p_t(x)\right]
+\frac12\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}
\left[\sigma^2(t)p_t(x)\right].
$$

This is a deterministic equation for a density even though the trajectories are random. The two terms are probability currents of different kinds. The first transports mass with the local velocity $$f$$. The second redistributes mass because nearby trajectories receive different Brownian increments. A derivation from Itô calculus appears in [The Fokker--Planck Equation]({% post_url 2026-02-04-fokker-planck-equation %}); here we need the equation as the bridge from forward to reverse dynamics.

Writing the equation as a conservation law makes its role more explicit. Define the one-dimensional probability current

$$
J_t(x)
=f(x,t)p_t(x)
-\frac12\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\left[\sigma^2(t)p_t(x)\right].
$$

Then

$$
\partial_t p_t(x)=-\partial_xJ_t(x).
$$

For an interval $$[a,b]$$,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_a^bp_t(x)\,dx
=J_t(a)-J_t(b).
$$

Probability inside the interval changes only through its boundaries. The same local rule governs both the relaxation of Langevin dynamics and the deliberately chosen noising path of a diffusion model. What changes is whether we seek a stationary density or a complete time-indexed family $$\{p_t\}$$.

In $$d$$ dimensions, write

$$
d\mathbf X_t
=\mathbf f(\mathbf X_t,t)\,dt
+\mathbf G(\mathbf X_t,t)\,d\mathbf W_t,
$$

where $$\mathbf G$$ maps Brownian increments into state space. The density then evolves as

$$
\frac{\partial p_t(\mathbf x)}{\partial t}
=-\sum_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}
\left[f_i(\mathbf x,t)p_t(\mathbf x)\right]
+\frac12\sum_{i,j}
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}
\left[
(\mathbf G\mathbf G^{\mathsf T})_{ij}(\mathbf x,t)
p_t(\mathbf x)
\right].
$$

The matrix $$\mathbf G\mathbf G^{\mathsf T}$$ is the local diffusion covariance. For independent elementwise noise, $$\mathbf G=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_d)$$, the off-diagonal entries vanish and the diffusion term reduces to

$$
\frac12\sum_i
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i^2}
\left[\sigma_i^2(t)p_t(\mathbf x)\right].
$$

This distinction becomes useful when moving from a scalar noise schedule, where $$\mathbf G=g(t)\mathbf I$$, to geometry-aware models with anisotropic or state-dependent noise. The lecture focuses on the scalar schedule because it makes the reverse drift especially clean.

For $$\mathbf G=g(t)\mathbf I$$, the diffusion covariance is $$g^2(t)\mathbf I$$ and the density equation becomes

$$
\partial_t p_t
=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf f p_t)
+\frac12g^2(t)\Delta p_t.
$$

The scalar coefficient can be taken outside the spatial derivatives because it depends on time alone. This exact form reappears twice below: once with a positive Laplacian for the forward process and once with a negative Laplacian when the same density path is described against a backward clock.

### Langevin dynamics turns an energy into a stationary density

Let $$U(\mathbf x)$$ be an energy function. Overdamped Langevin dynamics lowers the energy while Brownian noise prevents every trajectory from collapsing into a local minimum:

$$
d\mathbf X_t
=-\nabla U(\mathbf X_t)\,dt
+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma\,d\mathbf W_t.
$$

The minus sign is the energy-lowering convention. Substitution into Fokker--Planck gives

$$
\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t}
=\nabla\cdot\left(p_t\nabla U\right)
+\sigma^2\Delta p_t
=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_t,
$$

with probability current

$$
\mathbf J_t
=-p_t\nabla U-\sigma^2\nabla p_t.
$$

A stationary density with zero current satisfies

$$
p_\star(\mathbf x)\nabla U(\mathbf x)
+\sigma^2\nabla p_\star(\mathbf x)=0.
$$

Divide by $$p_\star$$ and recognize a logarithmic gradient:

$$
\nabla\log p_\star(\mathbf x)
=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\nabla U(\mathbf x).
$$

Integration yields

$$
p_\star(\mathbf x)
=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left[-\frac{U(\mathbf x)}{\sigma^2}\right],
$$

where $$Z$$ normalizes the density. The drift and diffusion do not vanish separately at stationarity. Their probability currents cancel.

The yellow trace in the left panel is one realization, not the density. It can wander between regions of high probability because the Brownian term acts at every step. The right panel estimates the density from many visited states. Agreement between that histogram and the target is a distributional claim; no individual path is expected to resemble the density contours.

The example establishes the first recurring pattern. We prescribe a local stochastic rule, but the distribution it produces is determined at the density level. Diffusion models use the same relationship in the opposite direction: they prescribe a forward SDE that destroys structure and then infer the reverse local rule from the shared density path.

## Forward diffusion and reverse-time SDEs

A continuous-time diffusion model starts from data,

$$
\mathbf X_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},
$$

and evolves it under a known forward SDE,

$$
d\mathbf X_t
=\mathbf f(\mathbf X_t,t)\,dt
+g(t)\,d\mathbf W_t.
$$

The schedule is chosen so that $$p_T$$ is close to a distribution we can sample, usually $$\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$$. A standard variance-preserving choice is

$$
\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)=-\frac12\beta(t)\mathbf x,
\qquad
g(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)}.
$$

For this linear SDE, define

$$
\alpha_t
=\exp\left[-\frac12\int_0^t\beta(s)\,ds\right].
$$

Conditioned on $$\mathbf X_0=\mathbf x_0$$, the forward transition is Gaussian:

$$
p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf x_0)
=\mathcal N\left(
\mathbf x_t;
\alpha_t\mathbf x_0,
(1-\alpha_t^2)\mathbf I
\right).
$$

The mean and covariance follow directly from the linear SDE. If $$\mathbf m_t=\mathbb E[\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_0=\mathbf x_0]$$, then

$$
\dot{\mathbf m}_t
=-\frac12\beta(t)\mathbf m_t,
\qquad
\mathbf m_0=\mathbf x_0,
$$

so $$\mathbf m_t=\alpha_t\mathbf x_0$$. If $$\mathbf C_t$$ is the conditional covariance, Itô's formula gives

$$
\dot{\mathbf C}_t
=-\beta(t)\mathbf C_t+\beta(t)\mathbf I,
\qquad
\mathbf C_0=\mathbf 0,
$$

whose solution is $$\mathbf C_t=(1-\alpha_t^2)\mathbf I$$. The same schedule both attenuates the data signal and fills in exactly the variance needed to approach a unit Gaussian.

As the accumulated noise grows, $$\alpha_t$$ approaches zero. Dependence on $$\mathbf x_0$$ disappears and the conditional covariance approaches $$\mathbf I$$. This explains the lecture's claim that a long enough forward process reaches an approximately standard Gaussian; it is a property of the chosen linear drift and noise schedule, not of arbitrary SDEs.

The continuous-time score-based formulation is developed by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456'>Song et al. (2021)</a>; author lecture diagram." %}

Sampling the terminal Gaussian is easy. Generation requires the reverse journey from $$p_T$$ to $$p_0=p_{\mathrm{data}}$$. Simply changing the sign of the integration limits does not reverse an Itô SDE. The forward Itô integral evaluates its integrand at the left endpoint of each Brownian increment. A reverse-time stochastic integral uses the opposite endpoint convention. Brownian motion has nonzero quadratic variation, so the discrepancy survives even as the partition becomes arbitrarily fine.

The asymmetry is visible in the simplest nonconstant integrand. With forward Itô integration,

$$
\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t
=\frac12W_T^2-\frac12T.
$$

Using right endpoints, as required by the backward Itô convention, gives

$$
\int_0^T W_t\,d^-W_t
=\frac12W_T^2+\frac12T.
$$

The difference is the quadratic variation $$T$$. Reversing an ordinary integral only changes its orientation; reversing an Itô integral also changes which side of each random increment supplies the integrand. The reverse drift must compensate for that surviving correction.

### Two clocks for the reverse process

It helps to distinguish a process whose clock runs backward from its ordinary forward-time reparameterization. Write the reverse-time SDE as

$$
d\bar{\mathbf X}_t
=\bar{\mathbf f}(\bar{\mathbf X}_t,t)\,dt
+g(t)\,d\bar{\mathbf W}_t,
$$

and integrate it from $$t=T$$ down to $$t=0$$. Here $$\bar{\mathbf W}_t$$ denotes a reverse-time Wiener process. The backward integral over an interval is not the negative of the forward Itô integral over that interval.

We can instead introduce an increasing clock $$s=T-t$$ and define

$$
\mathbf Y_s=\bar{\mathbf X}_{T-s}.
$$

Then $$\mathbf Y_s$$ follows an ordinary forward-time SDE,

$$
d\mathbf Y_s
=-\bar{\mathbf f}(\mathbf Y_s,T-s)\,ds
+g(T-s)\,d\mathbf W_s.
$$

Its density $$q_s(\mathbf y)=p_{T-s}(\mathbf y)$$ traverses the same family of marginals in reverse order. Differentiating $$q_s=p_{T-s}$$ contributes a minus sign, while the forward-time Fokker--Planck equation for $$\mathbf Y_s$$ contributes the usual positive diffusion term. This is the bookkeeping behind the backward density equation used in the lecture.

More explicitly,

$$
\partial_s q_s(\mathbf y)
=\nabla\cdot\left[
\bar{\mathbf f}(\mathbf y,T-s)q_s(\mathbf y)
\right]
+\frac12g^2(T-s)\Delta q_s(\mathbf y).
$$

Since $$\partial_s q_s=-\partial_t p_t$$ at $$t=T-s$$, multiplication by minus one yields

$$
\partial_t p_t(\mathbf x)
=-\nabla\cdot\left[
\bar{\mathbf f}(\mathbf x,t)p_t(\mathbf x)
\right]
-\frac12g^2(t)\Delta p_t(\mathbf x).
$$

The negative diffusion term does not describe an ordinary forward SDE that spontaneously concentrates probability. It appears because $$t$$ labels a family that the process traverses from larger values to smaller ones.

### Nelson's identity identifies the missing drift

Suppose the forward and reverse processes share the same marginal density $$p_t$$ at every time. In one dimension, their density equations are

$$
\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t}
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(f p_t)
+\frac12g^2(t)\frac{\partial^2p_t}{\partial x^2}
$$

for the forward SDE, and

$$
\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t}
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\bar f p_t)
-\frac12g^2(t)\frac{\partial^2p_t}{\partial x^2}
$$

for the reverse-time SDE written against the decreasing clock. Equating the two right-hand sides gives

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\left[(f-\bar f)p_t\right]
=g^2(t)\frac{\partial^2p_t}{\partial x^2}.
$$

Integrate once in space. Under the usual vanishing-current boundary condition, the integration constant is zero:

$$
(f(x,t)-\bar f(x,t))p_t(x)
=g^2(t)\frac{\partial p_t(x)}{\partial x}.
$$

Dividing by the density produces Nelson's identity,

$$
f(x,t)-\bar f(x,t)
=g^2(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log p_t(x).
$$

For isotropic diffusion in several dimensions,

$$
\boxed{
\bar{\mathbf f}(\mathbf x,t)
=\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)
-g^2(t)\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_t(\mathbf x)
}.
$$

The vector field

$$
\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x)=\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_t(\mathbf x)
$$

is the score of the noisy marginal. The forward drift $$\mathbf f$$ and noise schedule $$g$$ were chosen by us. The score is the only unknown term in the reverse drift.

The identity is a density statement. It does not claim that a forward sample path retraces itself under reverse dynamics. It says that the reverse process has marginal $$p_t$$ when run through the density path in the opposite direction. Different Brownian increments generate different individual trajectories.

## Denoising score matching

Direct access to $$\nabla\log p_t$$ would solve the reverse-SDE problem, but $$p_t$$ is the marginal

$$
p_t(\mathbf x)
=\int p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_0)
p_0(\mathbf x_0)\,d\mathbf x_0.
$$

Even when the conditional transition $$p_{t\mid0}$$ is Gaussian, this mixture over the data distribution is generally intractable. Its score nevertheless has a tractable conditional representation. Differentiate the marginal under the integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\mathbf x}p_t(\mathbf x)
&=\int \nabla_{\mathbf x}
p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_0)
p_0(\mathbf x_0)\,d\mathbf x_0\\
&=\int p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_0)
\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_0)
p_0(\mathbf x_0)\,d\mathbf x_0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by $$p_t(\mathbf x)$$ and use Bayes' rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_t(\mathbf x)
&=\int
\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_0)
p(\mathbf x_0\mid\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x_0\\
&=\mathbb E\left[
\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf X_0)
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the denoising score identity. The marginal score is a posterior average of conditional scores. We cannot evaluate the posterior explicitly, but we can sample from the joint distribution: draw a data point $$\mathbf X_0$$, draw time $$t$$, and corrupt the data according to the known transition to obtain $$\mathbf X_t$$.

For the variance-preserving transition above, write

$$
\mathbf X_t
=\alpha_t\mathbf X_0
+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I).
$$

The conditional score has the closed form

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf x_t}
\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf x_0)
=-\frac{\mathbf x_t-\alpha_t\mathbf x_0}{1-\alpha_t^2}
=-\frac{\boldsymbol\epsilon}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}}.
$$

A neural field $$\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x,t)$$ can therefore be trained with

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,\mathbf X_0,\mathbf X_t}
\left[
\left\lVert
\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\nabla_{\mathbf x_t}
\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_0)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

The lecture states the same regression in terms of the reverse drift. Since

$$
\bar{\mathbf f}(\mathbf x,t)
=\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)-g^2(t)\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x),
$$

a tractable conditional reverse-drift target is

$$
\mathbf f(\mathbf X_t,t)
-g^2(t)
\nabla_{\mathbf x_t}
\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_0).
$$

Training $$\bar{\mathbf f}_\theta$$ against this target gives

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{drift}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert
\bar{\mathbf f}_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\mathbf f(\mathbf X_t,t)
+g^2(t)\nabla_{\mathbf x_t}
\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_0)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

If the model is parameterized as $$\bar{\mathbf f}_\theta=\mathbf f-g^2\mathbf s_\theta$$, this is a score loss weighted by $$g^4(t)$$. “Predict the score” and “predict the reverse drift” are algebraically convertible, but they do not impose the same time weighting unless the loss is adjusted accordingly.

At the population optimum, squared-error regression returns the conditional expectation of its target given the network input. Hence

$$
\mathbf s_{\theta^\star}(\mathbf x,t)
=\mathbb E\left[
\nabla_{\mathbf x}
\log p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf X_0)
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x
\right]
=\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_t(\mathbf x).
$$

The learned score completes the reverse drift,

$$
\bar{\mathbf f}_\theta(\mathbf x,t)
=\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)-g^2(t)\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x,t).
$$

One may equivalently train a network to predict the reverse drift directly. The score parameterization is cleaner because it isolates the unknown density-dependent quantity from the prescribed forward dynamics.

The conditional target is noisy: two clean samples can produce the same corrupted state yet imply different denoising directions. That noise is not a mistake. The regression average is exactly the marginal score needed by the reverse SDE. The flow-matching half of the lecture repeats this logic with velocities instead of scores.

The target may use $$\mathbf X_0$$ during training, but an unconditional score network receives only $$\mathbf X_t$$ and $$t$$. It cannot return a separate direction for every clean ancestor compatible with one noisy state. Squared-error regression forces it to return their posterior mean, which is exactly the marginal score.

## Continuity equations and deterministic flows

Remove the Brownian term and consider the ODE

$$
d\mathbf X_t
=\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t)\,dt.
$$

Its density follows the continuity equation

$$
\frac{\partial p_t(\mathbf x)}{\partial t}
=-\nabla\cdot
\left[\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)p_t(\mathbf x)\right].
$$

This is the Fokker--Planck equation with zero diffusion. Probability is transported by the velocity field without stochastic spreading. A small region may still expand or contract because neighboring velocities can diverge or converge, but every particle follows a deterministic trajectory once its initial state is fixed.

The equation again expresses local conservation. For a region $$\Omega$$ with outward normal $$\mathbf n$$,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega p_t(\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x
=-\int_{\partial\Omega}
p_t(\mathbf x)\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$

Unlike the SDE case, there is no diffusive current. All probability crosses the boundary by following the deterministic velocity field.

Let $$\boldsymbol\psi_t$$ be the flow map of the ODE. It sends an initial point $$\mathbf X_0$$ to its state at time $$t$$,

$$
\mathbf X_t=\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf X_0),
$$

and satisfies

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf x)
=\mathbf u_t(\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf x)).
$$

Under standard regularity conditions on $$\mathbf u_t$$, the map is one-to-one. The associated push-forward relation is

$$
(\boldsymbol\psi_t)_\#p_0=p_t.
$$

Pointwise, the push-forward can be written through the change-of-variables formula

$$
p_t(\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf x_0))
\left\lvert
\det\nabla_{\mathbf x_0}\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf x_0)
\right\rvert
=p_0(\mathbf x_0).
$$

The Jacobian determinant accounts for local expansion and contraction. Differentiating this relation along trajectories recovers the continuity equation, so the flow-map and density-PDE views describe the same deterministic transport at different levels.

Flow matching chooses $$p_0$$ as the noise distribution and $$p_1=p_{\mathrm{data}}$$. This endpoint convention is the reverse of the diffusion section, where $$t=0$$ was data and $$t=T$$ was noise. The change is only notation, but forgetting it flips every interpretation of $$\mathbf X_0$$ and $$\mathbf X_1$$.

The velocity field required to transport an arbitrary noise density into an arbitrary data density is not known. Flow matching avoids solving for it directly by building a family of conditional paths whose velocities are known.

## Conditional probability paths before marginal probability paths

Let $$\mathbf X_1=\mathbf x_1$$ be a data sample. For each possible endpoint, choose a conditional probability path

$$
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1),
$$

which starts near the noise distribution and ends at that endpoint. It is generated by a conditional velocity field

$$
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
$$

through the conditional continuity equation

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
=-\nabla\cdot\left[
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
\right].
$$

Average the conditional densities over data endpoints:

$$
p_t(\mathbf x)
=\int p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
q(\mathbf x_1)\,d\mathbf x_1,
$$

where $$q=p_{\mathrm{data}}$$. If the conditional boundaries satisfy

$$
p_{0\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)=p_0(\mathbf x),
\qquad
p_{1\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)=\delta_{\mathbf x_1}(\mathbf x),
$$

then the mixture begins at $$p_0$$ and ends at the data density:

$$
p_1(\mathbf x)
=\int\delta_{\mathbf x_1}(\mathbf x)q(\mathbf x_1)\,d\mathbf x_1
=q(\mathbf x).
$$

Each conditional path has a flow map $$\boldsymbol\psi_t(\cdot\mid\mathbf x_1)$$ satisfying

$$
\frac{d}{dt}
\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf x_0\mid\mathbf x_1)
=\mathbf u_t\left(
\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf x_0\mid\mathbf x_1)
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathbf x_1
\right).
$$

Conversely, a differentiable invertible flow determines its Eulerian velocity:

$$
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
\boldsymbol\psi_t\left(
\boldsymbol\psi_t^{-1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathbf x_1
\right).
$$

The flow map describes where a labeled particle goes. The velocity field describes the instantaneous motion at a fixed spatial location. The probability path describes the distribution of the particle after pushing forward its random initial condition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-14/s18-conditional-marginal-objects.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Four panels comparing conditional probability path, marginal probability path, conditional velocity, and marginal velocity." caption="The conditional path and conditional velocity are designed for one endpoint; marginalization produces the aggregate path and velocity. Figure from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Flow Matching Guide and Code</a>." %}

The four objects in the figure should not be merged into one generic “flow.” The conditional path $$p_{t\mid1}$$ is tractable by design. Its conditional velocity $$\mathbf u_t(\cdot\mid\mathbf x_1)$$ is tractable as well. The marginal path $$p_t$$ is a mixture over the data distribution and is generally intractable. The marginal velocity $$\mathbf u_t$$ that transports that mixture is also intractable pointwise. Training works because the last object is a conditional expectation of the second.

Their relationships can be read as a square:

| | Velocity field | Probability path |
|---|---|---|
| Conditional on $$\mathbf X_1=\mathbf x_1$$ | $$\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)$$, chosen in closed form | $$p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)$$, generated by the conditional field |
| Marginal over $$\mathbf X_1$$ | $$\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)$$, unknown before marginalization | $$p_t(\mathbf x)$$, the mixture of conditional paths |

The horizontal arrows are continuity equations: a velocity generates a probability path. The right vertical arrow is ordinary marginalization over $$\mathbf x_1$$. The left vertical arrow is the missing step supplied by the marginalization trick. It is not an unweighted average; it uses the posterior of $$\mathbf X_1$$ conditioned on the current state.

The conditional objects are not approximations to the marginal ones. They are deliberately simpler components whose exact mixture and posterior-weighted velocity produce the marginal transport.

### The linear Gaussian conditional path

The simplest construction draws independent endpoints

$$
\mathbf X_0\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I),
\qquad
\mathbf X_1\sim q,
$$

and interpolates between them:

$$
\mathbf X_t
=(1-t)\mathbf X_0+t\mathbf X_1.
$$

Condition on $$\mathbf X_1=\mathbf x_1$$. Because $$\mathbf X_0$$ is standard Gaussian,

$$
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
=\mathcal N\left(
\mathbf x;
t\mathbf x_1,
(1-t)^2\mathbf I
\right).
$$

The Lagrangian velocity along one interpolating particle is constant:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf X_t}{dt}
=\mathbf x_1-\mathbf X_0.
$$

To express it as a field at the current state $$\mathbf x$$, solve

$$
\mathbf X_0
=\frac{\mathbf x-t\mathbf x_1}{1-t}.
$$

Substitution gives

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
=\frac{\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x}{1-t}
}.
$$

The denominator approaches zero near $$t=1$$, but the numerator shrinks at the same rate along a conditional trajectory. Indeed, substituting $$\mathbf x=(1-t)\mathbf x_0+t\mathbf x_1$$ recovers $$\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_0$$. The field looks singular only if $$t$$ and $$\mathbf x$$ are considered independently of the conditional path.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-14/s20-linear-conditional-path.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A Gaussian source density transported along a cone toward one conditioned data endpoint." caption="Conditioning on \(X_1=x_1\) produces a Gaussian path whose mean moves toward \(x_1\) while its variance contracts. Figure from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Flow Matching Guide and Code</a>,." %}

This construction is conditional on the destination. Different endpoints define different cones and different velocity fields. Once those paths overlap, the current state $$\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x$$ no longer reveals which endpoint generated it. The marginal field must average the competing conditional directions using the endpoint posterior.

## The marginalization trick

The required marginal velocity is

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)
=\int
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
p_{1\mid t}(\mathbf x_1\mid\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x_1
=\mathbb E\left[
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_1)
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x
\right]
}.
$$

The proof begins from the marginal density rather than from individual trajectories:

$$
p_t(\mathbf x)
=\int p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
q(\mathbf x_1)\,d\mathbf x_1.
$$

Differentiate and substitute the conditional continuity equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial p_t(\mathbf x)}{\partial t}
&=\int
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
q(\mathbf x_1)\,d\mathbf x_1\\
&=-\nabla\cdot\int
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
q(\mathbf x_1)\,d\mathbf x_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Bayes' rule factors the joint density inside the integral:

$$
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)q(\mathbf x_1)
=p_{1\mid t}(\mathbf x_1\mid\mathbf x)p_t(\mathbf x).
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial p_t(\mathbf x)}{\partial t}
&=-\nabla\cdot\left[
p_t(\mathbf x)
\int \mathbf u_t(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
p_{1\mid t}(\mathbf x_1\mid\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x_1
\right]\\
&=-\nabla\cdot\left[
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)p_t(\mathbf x)
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The posterior average of conditional velocities generates the mixture path. The proof does not require the linear Gaussian construction; it requires conditional paths that obey their continuity equations and marginalize to $$p_t$$.

### The conditioning variable is a design choice

Conditioning on the data endpoint $$\mathbf X_1$$ is convenient, not mandatory. Introduce a general condition $$Z$$ with density $$p_Z$$. If

$$
p_t(\mathbf x)
=\int p_t(\mathbf x\mid z)p_Z(z)\,dz,
$$

then the same calculation gives

$$
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)
=\mathbb E\left[
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t\mid Z)
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x
\right].
$$

The lecture displays three instances:

1. **Target conditioning:** $$Z=\mathbf X_1$$. A conditional path collapses onto one data endpoint.
2. **Source conditioning:** $$Z=\mathbf X_0$$. A conditional path begins at one source point and spreads toward the target distribution.
3. **Endpoint-pair conditioning:** $$Z=(\mathbf X_0,\mathbf X_1)$$. A conditional path connects a chosen source--target pair, often deterministically.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-14/s23-conditioning-choices.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three flow-matching constructions conditioned on X one, X zero, or the pair X zero X one." caption="The marginalization identity works for target, source, or endpoint-pair conditioning; what changes is the tractable conditional path supplied to training. Figure from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Flow Matching Guide and Code</a>." %}

The condition changes what information is available to define the tractable field, but it is not an input to the final unconditional model. At inference, $$\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf x,t)$$ receives the current state and time. Its population target is the conditional velocity averaged over all latent conditions compatible with that state.

## Flow matching and conditional flow matching losses

If the marginal velocity were available, one could train with the flow-matching loss

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,\mathbf X_t\sim p_t}
\left[
\left\lVert
\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

But evaluating $$\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)$$ requires the intractable posterior $$p_{1\mid t}(\mathbf x_1\mid\mathbf x)$$. Conditional flow matching uses samples from the tractable joint instead:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,\mathbf X_1,
\mathbf X_t\sim p_{t\mid1}(\cdot\mid\mathbf X_1)}
\left[
\left\lVert
\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_1)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

The two losses are not numerically equal. Their gradients with respect to $$\theta$$ are equal. Let

$$
\mathbf J_\theta(\mathbf x,t)
=\nabla_\theta\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf x,t)
$$

be the model Jacobian. Differentiating the conditional objective gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}
&=2\,\mathbb E\left[
\mathbf J_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)^{\mathsf T}
\left(
\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_1)
\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Condition on $$\mathbf X_t$$. Both the network and its Jacobian are fixed under this inner expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}
&=2\,\mathbb E_{t,\mathbf X_t}\left[
\mathbf J_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)^{\mathsf T}
\left(
\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\mathbb E[
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_1)
\mid\mathbf X_t]
\right)
\right]\\
&=2\,\mathbb E_{t,\mathbf X_t}\left[
\mathbf J_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)^{\mathsf T}
\left(
\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t)
\right)
\right]\\
&=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Equivalently, the conditional loss decomposes as

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)
=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
+\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert
\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_1)
-\mathbf u_t(\mathbf X_t)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

The second term is conditional target variance and does not depend on $$\theta$$. This is why the losses have the same population minimizers and gradients even though conditional regression has extra target noise. Mini-batch gradients still have variance, finite models still have approximation error, and numerical ODE integration still introduces sampling error. The identity removes the intractable target; it does not remove those other sources of error.

## Gaussian and affine conditional flows

The lecture collects the flow map, velocity, density, boundary conditions, and loss into one diagram because each column answers a different question. A flow map $$\boldsymbol\psi_t$$ pushes initial points forward. Differentiating it gives a velocity $$\mathbf u_t$$. The continuity equation connects velocity to the probability path $$p_t$$, but the inverse mapping from a path to a velocity need not be unique. Conditioning makes the lower row tractable; marginalization and conditional expectation then recover the upper row.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-14/s26-gaussian-cfm-map.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Diagram relating conditional and marginal flow maps, velocity fields, probability paths, boundary conditions, and losses." caption="Conditional paths make the lower row tractable; push-forward, differentiation, continuity, marginalization, and conditional expectation connect it to the marginal flow above. Figure from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Flow Matching Guide and Code</a>." %}

The linear interpolation is one member of a broader affine family:

$$
\boldsymbol\psi_t(\mathbf x_0\mid\mathbf x_1)
=\alpha_t\mathbf x_1+\sigma_t\mathbf x_0.
$$

The schedules satisfy the endpoint conditions

$$
\alpha_0=0,
\qquad
\sigma_0=1,
\qquad
\alpha_1=1,
\qquad
\sigma_1=0.
$$

The lecture assumes that $$\alpha_t$$ increases monotonically from zero to one and $$\sigma_t$$ decreases monotonically from one to zero. For standard Gaussian $$\mathbf X_0$$ and fixed endpoint $$\mathbf x_1$$,

$$
p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_1)
=\mathcal N\left(
\mathbf x;
\alpha_t\mathbf x_1,
\sigma_t^2\mathbf I
\right).
$$

Differentiating the affine trajectory gives the conditional target

$$
\frac{d\mathbf X_t}{dt}
=\dot\alpha_t\mathbf X_1
+\dot\sigma_t\mathbf X_0.
$$

Conditional flow matching can therefore sample both endpoints, form

$$
\mathbf X_t
=\alpha_t\mathbf X_1+\sigma_t\mathbf X_0,
$$

and minimize

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{affine}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E
\left[
\left\lVert
\mathbf u_\theta(\mathbf X_t,t)
-\left(
\dot\alpha_t\mathbf X_1
+\dot\sigma_t\mathbf X_0
\right)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

At the population optimum,

$$
\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)
=\dot\alpha_t
\mathbb E[\mathbf X_1\mid\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x]
+\dot\sigma_t
\mathbb E[\mathbf X_0\mid\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x].
$$

Both conditional expectations appear because the affine velocity depends on both endpoints. The next algebra shows that either one is sufficient once the current state and schedules are known.

### Velocity, data, and noise parameterizations

From

$$
\mathbf x=\alpha_t\mathbf X_1+\sigma_t\mathbf X_0,
$$

solve for the noise endpoint:

$$
\mathbf X_0
=\frac{\mathbf x-\alpha_t\mathbf X_1}{\sigma_t}.
$$

Substituting this into the affine velocity yields a data-prediction parameterization:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)
=\frac{\dot\sigma_t}{\sigma_t}\mathbf x
+\left(
\dot\alpha_t
-\alpha_t\frac{\dot\sigma_t}{\sigma_t}
\right)
\mathbb E[\mathbf X_1\mid\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x]
}.
$$

Alternatively, solve for the data endpoint:

$$
\mathbf X_1
=\frac{\mathbf x-\sigma_t\mathbf X_0}{\alpha_t}.
$$

This gives a noise-prediction parameterization:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)
=\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}\mathbf x
+\left(
\dot\sigma_t
-\sigma_t\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}
\right)
\mathbb E[\mathbf X_0\mid\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x]
}.
$$

Thus a model may predict the velocity directly, predict the clean endpoint $$\mathbf X_1$$ and convert it to velocity, or predict the noise endpoint $$\mathbf X_0$$ and convert that to velocity. The three heads contain the same population information away from schedule singularities, but their regression targets and time-dependent scaling differ. Near $$t=0$$, division by $$\alpha_t$$ is ill-conditioned; near $$t=1$$, division by $$\sigma_t$$ is ill-conditioned. Implementations choose parameterizations and loss weights with these endpoint effects in mind.

## One conditional-expectation pattern, two generators

The diffusion and flow constructions end at the same statistical pattern while retaining different dynamics.

| Construction | Tractable conditional object | Required marginal object | Identity | Generative dynamics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diffusion | $$p_{t\mid0}(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf x_0)$$ and its conditional score | $$\nabla\log p_t(\mathbf x)$$ | Marginal score is the posterior mean of the conditional score | Reverse-time SDE |
| Flow matching | $$p_{t\mid1}(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf x_1)$$ and its conditional velocity | $$\mathbf u_t(\mathbf x)$$ | Marginal velocity is the posterior mean of the conditional velocity | ODE |

In diffusion, Fokker--Planck compares the forward and reverse density equations. Nelson's identity turns their drift difference into the marginal score. Denoising score matching replaces that intractable score with regression to conditional transition scores.

In flow matching, the continuity equation is applied to every conditional path and then marginalized. The marginalization trick turns the unknown marginal velocity into a posterior average. Conditional flow matching replaces that intractable velocity with regression to conditional velocities.

The conditional process is not merely a convenient way to sample training pairs. It defines the probability path, the local target, and the variance of the regression problem. The learned network removes the condition by averaging over all compatible endpoints. Once that distinction is kept explicit, reverse diffusion and flow matching become two instances of the same density-level strategy rather than two unrelated recipes.

The shared conditional-expectation structure is the durable idea; schedules, parameterizations, and solvers determine how that idea behaves numerically.
