## Why convolution is a special linear map

An unconstrained linear layer applies

$$
y=Wx,
\qquad
y(i)=\sum_{j=1}^N W(i,j)x(j).
$$

A one-dimensional convolution is also linear,

$$
y=w*x,
\qquad
y(i)=\sum_{j=1}^N w(j)x(i-j),
$$

but its matrix has a repeated, shifted pattern. For a three-tap periodic kernel, it has the circulant form

$$
W=
\begin{pmatrix}
w_1&w_2&w_3&0&0\\
0&w_1&w_2&w_3&0\\
0&0&w_1&w_2&w_3\\
w_3&0&0&w_1&w_2\\
w_2&w_3&0&0&w_1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each output location applies the same kernel to a translated neighborhood. Convolutional structure therefore restricts the space of linear maps before any learning begins.

The graph question is how to obtain an equally principled restriction without a regular pixel grid. The lecture answers it twice. The first route generalizes Fourier analysis and filtering from time signals to signals on vertices. The second declares convolution to be any linear operator equivariant to the relevant transformations and characterizes that operator space directly.

## Route I: from graph signals to graph Fourier analysis

A discrete-time signal $$f=[f(0),\ldots,f(N-1)]$$ can be treated as a graph signal $$f:V\to\mathbb R$$ on a directed cyclic graph. This observation suggests beginning with ordinary signal processing and replacing the cycle by an arbitrary graph.

The Fourier transform moves from the original coordinate space to a frequency basis. In that basis, filtering and convolution become element-wise modifications of frequency coefficients. To generalize this recipe, a graph needs a notion of frequency, a Fourier basis, and a corresponding change of basis.

### Smoothness and the Laplacian

Classical Fourier modes are complex exponentials,

$$
\chi_\ell(n)=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i\ell n}{N}\right),
$$

whose index $$\ell$$ controls oscillation. The Laplacian supplies an intrinsic definition of this oscillation because Fourier modes are its eigenfunctions:

$$
\Delta\chi_\ell=\lambda_\ell\chi_\ell.
$$

Equivalently, a signal can be reconstructed from its Fourier coefficients,

$$
f(n)=\frac1N\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}\widehat f(\ell)
e^{2\pi i\ell n/N}.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/s10-frequency-decomposition.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Several simple waves combining into a signal, with amplitude represented over frequency." caption="Frequency measures the rate at which basis functions oscillate. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 10." %}

In one dimension, curvature is measured by the second derivative $$d^2f/dt^2$$. To match the positive-semidefinite graph Laplacian $$L=D-A$$, it is convenient to use the negative operator $$\Delta=-d^2/dt^2$$. Its discrete analogue compares $$f(n)$$ with its neighbors:

$$
(\Delta f)(n)
=\big(f(n)-f(n-1)\big)-\big(f(n+1)-f(n)\big).
$$

Thus $$2f(n)\approx f(n-1)+f(n+1)$$ at a locally smooth point. A rapidly varying signal produces a large response; a locally smooth one produces a small response.

The same interpretation extends beyond a line. On a surface or manifold, the Laplacian compares a function value with the average over a small surrounding sphere or ball. The lecture's continuum formula below uses the opposite sign convention from the positive-semidefinite graph Laplacian; the eigenfunctions and smoothness interpretation are unchanged:

$$
\Delta u(x_0)\propto
\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\frac1{\varepsilon^2}
\left(
\frac1{|S_\varepsilon(x_0)|}
\int_{S_\varepsilon(x_0)}u(x)\,dx-u(x_0)
\right).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/s12-higher-dimensional-laplacian.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A function over a surface compared with its average over a small surrounding region." caption="The higher-dimensional Laplacian retains the local-average interpretation. Physical equations provide another intuition. The heat equation

$$
\frac{d}{dt}u=\Delta u
$$

moves each value toward the average of nearby values. Its stationary condition is the Laplace equation, $$\Delta u=0$$.

For a graph with adjacency $$A$$ and degree matrix $$D$$, the combinatorial Laplacian is

$$
L=D-A,
$$

and its action is

$$
(Lf)(n)=\sum_{m\in N(n)}\big(f(n)-f(m)\big).
$$

The normalized form $$D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}$$ adjusts this comparison for vertex degree.

### The graph Fourier basis

Given a Laplacian, define Fourier modes as eigenfunctions or eigenvectors. For ordinary periodic time data,

$$
\chi_\ell(n)=e^{2\pi i\ell n/N},
\qquad
\Delta\chi_\ell=\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{N}\right)^2\chi_\ell.
$$

The eigenvalue quantifies smoothness normalized by the function value.

For a graph, solve

$$
L\chi_\ell=\lambda_\ell\chi_\ell.
$$

The quadratic form

$$
f^\top Lf=\frac12\sum_{(n,m)\in E}\big(f(n)-f(m)\big)^2
$$

measures variation across edges. Eigenvectors with larger eigenvalues vary more strongly between connected vertices and are interpreted as higher-frequency graph modes.

The lecture visualizes this ordering on molecular and graph structures: low-frequency modes vary slowly across edges, whereas high-frequency modes alternate more rapidly.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/s17-graph-fourier-modes.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Low-, intermediate-, and high-frequency Laplacian eigenvectors plotted on a graph." caption="Laplacian eigenvectors provide a graph-specific spectrum from smooth to rapidly varying signals. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 17." %}

Collect the orthonormal eigenvectors into $$U=[\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_N]$$ so that

$$
L=U\Lambda U^\top.
$$

The graph Fourier transform computes coefficients $$\widehat f=U^\top f$$, and the inverse reconstructs $$f=U\widehat f$$.

This gives a one-to-one dictionary. The one-dimensional Laplace operator becomes the graph Laplacian; complex exponential eigenfunctions become Laplacian eigenvectors; Fourier-series coefficients become $$U^\top f$$.

On a cycle graph, the graph construction recovers the discrete Fourier transform. Its Laplacian eigenpairs may be written

$$
\lambda_\ell=2-2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{N}\right),
\qquad
\chi_\ell=\left[1,\omega^\ell,\omega^{2\ell},\ldots,
\omega^{(N-1)\ell}\right]^\top,
\quad \omega=e^{2\pi i/N}.
$$

Putting the eigenvectors into columns gives the DFT matrix. The second lecture case is a path rather than a cycle. Its real eigenvectors are cosine modes,

$$
\lambda_\ell=2-2\cos\left(\frac{\pi\ell}{N}\right),
\qquad
\chi_\ell(i)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}
\cos\left(\frac{\pi\ell(i-\tfrac12)}{N}\right),
$$

with the constant mode normalized separately. Their eigenvector matrix is the DCT-II used in JPEG compression.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/s21-path-dct-modes.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Cosine Laplacian eigenvectors of a path graph from low to high frequency." caption="A path graph produces the DCT-II basis rather than the periodic DFT basis. ### Filtering, convolution, and spectral GNNs

Filtering modifies frequency coefficients. For a spectral response $$\widehat h$$,

$$
\widehat g=\widehat h\odot\widehat f,
\qquad
g=U\operatorname{diag}(\widehat h)U^\top f.
$$

Suppressing high-frequency coefficients smooths the signal.

For ordinary discrete time,

$$
(h*f)(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}h(n-m)f(m),
$$

and the convolution theorem states $$\widehat{h*f}(k)=\widehat h(k)\widehat f(k)$$. The pipeline above therefore describes convolution as well as filtering.

Graph signal processing adopts the same spectral operation as its definition:

$$
g=U\operatorname{diag}(\widehat h)U^\top f.
$$

Thus a graph convolution matrix is any $$C=U\operatorname{diag}(\widehat h)U^\top$$ sharing eigenvectors with $$L$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/s24-graph-filter-pipeline.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph Fourier transform, multiplication by a spectral response, and inverse graph Fourier transform." caption="Graph convolution transforms into the Laplacian basis, multiplies frequency coefficients, and transforms back. Original PowerPoint diagram from slide 24." %}

Directly learning one coefficient per eigenvalue is expensive: the eigendecomposition costs substantial computation, and the parameter vector $$\theta\in\mathbb R^{|V|}$$ depends on the number of vertices. It also ties the filter to one graph basis.

ChebNet replaces a free diagonal response with a polynomial of the rescaled eigenvalues,

$$
\widehat h(\Lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\theta_kT_k(\widetilde\Lambda),
$$

where $$T_k$$ is a Chebyshev polynomial. Because $$U T_k(\widetilde\Lambda)U^\top=T_k(\widetilde L)$$, the filter can be evaluated without explicitly computing eigenvectors. The GCN construction keeps a first-order form.

The recurrence

$$
T_k(x)=2xT_{k-1}(x)-T_{k-2}(x)
$$

and rescaling $$\widetilde\Lambda=2\Lambda/\lambda_{\max}-I$$ make this a stable polynomial evaluation on the Laplacian spectrum.

With self-loops and symmetric degree normalization, the resulting GCN layer is

$$
H^{(\ell+1)}=\sigma\left(
\widetilde D^{-1/2}\widetilde A\widetilde D^{-1/2}
H^{(\ell)}W_\ell
\right),
$$

where $$\widetilde A=A+I$$. The familiar neighborhood update therefore arrives as an approximation of graph spectral convolution.

The same basis also explains Laplacian positional encodings. Graph eigenvectors generalize the sinusoidal basis, and $$\chi_\ell(n)$$ supplies a frequency-indexed coordinate for node $$n$$.

## Route II: characterize equivariant linear operators

The spectral construction is persuasive when one graph supports many signals. It becomes less natural when the graph itself varies across examples, because each input has a different Laplacian basis. The second route begins from a broader definition: convolution is a linear operator equivariant to a chosen transformation group.

Ordinary convolutions in one, two, and three dimensions are translation-equivariant linear maps. This suggests asking for the complete space of linear maps equivariant to permutation rather than starting from a frequency basis.

### Shift equivariance recovers convolution

For periodic discrete time, let $$A$$ be the cyclic shift. The translation group is generated by $$I,A,A^2,\ldots,A^{N-1}$$. A linear operator $$C$$ is shift-equivariant when

$$
ACx=CAx
$$

for every $$x$$, equivalently $$AC=CA$$. Commuting matrices share eigenvectors, connecting this route back to the spectral one.

A matrix commutes with the cyclic shift if and only if it is circulant. Multiplication by a circulant matrix is precisely periodic discrete convolution. The equivariance definition therefore reproduces ordinary convolution rather than inventing a different operator.

Explicitly, every such map has the form

$$
C=
\begin{pmatrix}
c_0&c_{N-1}&\cdots&c_2&c_1\\
c_1&c_0&\cdots&c_3&c_2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
c_{N-1}&c_{N-2}&\cdots&c_1&c_0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

### Permutation-equivariant maps on nodes

Now require equivariance to every permutation matrix $$P$$:

$$
PCx=CPx\qquad\text{for all }P.
$$

For scalar node features, the complete solution is

$$
C=w_1I+w_2\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top.
$$

The identity term transforms each node independently; the all-ones term pools globally and broadcasts the result back to every node.

### Higher-order tensors and fixed points

Graphs also carry edge-valued matrices and higher-order tensors. If $$X\in\mathbb R^{n^\ell}$$ has degree $$\ell$$, then a linear operator between such tensors has more indices and potentially more equivariant basis elements.

The desired condition is that applying the permutation before or after the linear map gives the same result. Invariant maps remove the output action; equivariant maps retain it.

For an edge-to-edge map, vectorizing the matrix-valued feature makes the action $$P\otimes P$$. Equivariance becomes

$$
L(P\otimes P)=(P\otimes P)L.
$$

After conjugation and vectorization, this is a fixed-point equation:

$$
(P\otimes P\otimes P\otimes P)\operatorname{vec}(L)
=\operatorname{vec}(L).
$$

Simultaneous permutation does not change which indices are equal. The operator entries must therefore be constant within each equality pattern: all four indices equal, two equal pairs, three equal and one different, and so on.

For example, $$L_{7,7,7,7}=L_{8,8,8,8}$$ because both entries have four equal indices. Likewise, $$L_{7,7,1,2}=L_{8,8,1,2}$$ whenever the permutation changes the repeated label while preserving the equality pattern.

For degree-$$\ell$$ input and output tensors, the number of basis elements is the Bell number $$\operatorname{Bell}(2\ell)$$, the number of set partitions of the $$2\ell$$ operator indices. Each partition defines a binary indicator tensor for one equality pattern, and any equivariant linear map is a learned linear combination of these bases.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/s40-bell-basis.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Set partitions and their corresponding equality-pattern tensors for a permutation-equivariant operator basis." caption="Set partitions index a complete basis for higher-order permutation-equivariant linear maps. Original PowerPoint diagram from slide 40." %}

An interpretable view rewrites these bases as combinations of pooling selected indices and broadcasting into new axes. The abstract equality-pattern classification therefore corresponds to concrete tensor operations.

If $$p$$ is a set of tensor axes, pooling sums over those indices. Broadcasting then copies the pooled tensor along a chosen set $$B$$ of output axes. The complete linear map can be written schematically as

$$
W(X)=\sum_{p,B}w_{B,p}\,
\operatorname{Bcast}_{B,M'}\!\left(\operatorname{Pool}_p(X)\right).
$$

The learnable coefficients $$w_{B,p}$$ weight the finite collection of valid pool-and-broadcast patterns.

The lecture illustrates how different equality-pattern bases retain diagonals, pool rows or columns, broadcast summaries, or extract global statistics from a matrix-valued feature.

## Why ordinary GNNs are less restricted

For node features alone, a purely linear operator equivariant to every permutation can only combine identity with global pooling and broadcasting. A typical GNN layer instead uses the adjacency matrix as an input-dependent equivariant tensor:

$$
X\longmapsto \sigma(AXW).
$$

The product $$AX$$ is bilinear in two equivariant inputs rather than one fixed linear map of $$X$$. Node-wise linear maps and nonlinearities also preserve permutation equivariance without belonging to the restricted global linear commutant.

The two derivations therefore answer different questions. Graph signal processing defines convolution relative to the Laplacian of one graph and yields spectral filters and their polynomial approximations. The equivariant-operator route classifies maps that commute with an entire symmetry group. Ordinary message-passing GNNs combine both ideas with an input graph, tensor products, shared nonlinear maps, and local computation.
