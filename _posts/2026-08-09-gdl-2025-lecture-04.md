## Why convolution is a special linear map

An unconstrained linear layer applies $$y=Wx$$. A one-dimensional convolution is also linear, but its weight matrix has a repeated, shifted pattern: each output location applies the same kernel to a translated neighborhood. Convolutional structure therefore restricts the space of linear maps before any learning begins.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-4.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Dense MLP matrix compared with the repeated banded matrix of a convolution." caption="A CNN is an MLP whose linear map has a shared shift-structured weight matrix. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 4." %}

The graph question is how to obtain an equally principled restriction without a regular pixel grid. The lecture answers it twice. The first route generalizes Fourier analysis and filtering from time signals to signals on vertices. The second declares convolution to be any linear operator equivariant to the relevant transformations and characterizes that operator space directly.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Question of deriving graph convolution from ordinary signal processing." caption="Graph convolution asks what should replace the translation-based operator used for images and time signals. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 7." %}

## Route I: from graph signals to graph Fourier analysis

A discrete-time signal $$f=[f(0),\ldots,f(N-1)]$$ can be treated as a graph signal $$f:V\to\mathbb R$$ on a directed cyclic graph. This observation suggests beginning with ordinary signal processing and replacing the cycle by an arbitrary graph.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A time signal represented as a signal over a directed cyclic graph." caption="A periodic one-dimensional signal is already a graph signal on a directed cycle. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 8." %}

The Fourier transform moves from the original coordinate space to a frequency basis. In that basis, filtering and convolution become element-wise modifications of frequency coefficients. To generalize this recipe, a graph needs a notion of frequency, a Fourier basis, and a corresponding change of basis.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-9.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Signal, Fourier transform, frequency coefficients, and graph analogue." caption="The spectral route seeks graph frequencies so that filtering becomes simple coefficient-wise multiplication. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 9." %}

### Smoothness and the Laplacian

Classical Fourier modes are complex exponentials,

$$
\chi_\ell(n)=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i\ell n}{N}\right),
$$

whose index $$\ell$$ controls oscillation. The Laplacian supplies an intrinsic definition of this oscillation because Fourier modes are its eigenfunctions:

$$
\Delta\chi_\ell=\lambda_\ell\chi_\ell.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-10.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Classical Fourier modes and Laplacian eigenfunction equation." caption="Fourier frequency can be defined through the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian rather than through coordinates alone. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 10." %}

In one dimension, the Laplacian measures how far a value deviates from its infinitesimal neighborhood. A discrete analogue compares $$f(n)$$ with the neighboring values $$f(n-1)$$ and $$f(n+1)$$. A rapidly varying signal produces a large response; a locally smooth one produces a small response.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Continuous and discrete Laplacians as local deviation operators." caption="The Laplacian measures local deviation from neighboring values and therefore acts as a smoothness operator. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 11." %}

The same interpretation extends beyond a line. On a surface or manifold, the Laplacian compares a function value with the average over a small surrounding sphere or ball.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Laplacian on a surface compared with the average over a small ball." caption="Higher-dimensional Laplacians retain the local-average interpretation without relying on a one-dimensional order. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 12." %}

Physical equations provide another intuition. In the heat equation, the time derivative of temperature is proportional to its Laplacian, so each point moves toward the average of nearby values. The Laplace equation describes the corresponding stationary condition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Heat equation and Laplace equation examples of Laplacian smoothing." caption="Heat diffusion makes the Laplacian's local averaging behavior physically visible. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 13." %}

For a graph with adjacency $$A$$ and degree matrix $$D$$, the combinatorial Laplacian is

$$
L=D-A,
$$

and its action is

$$
(Lf)(n)=\sum_{m\in N(n)}\big(f(n)-f(m)\big).
$$

The normalized form $$D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}$$ adjusts this comparison for vertex degree.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph Laplacian matrix and its local difference action." caption="The graph Laplacian sums differences between each node and its neighbors. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 14." %}

### The graph Fourier basis

Given a Laplacian, define Fourier modes as eigenfunctions or eigenvectors. For ordinary periodic time data, the complex exponentials satisfy the eigenvalue equation; the eigenvalue quantifies their normalized smoothness.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Fourier basis defined by Laplacian eigenfunctions." caption="The Laplacian eigenvalue orders Fourier modes by oscillation or lack of smoothness. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 15." %}

For a graph, solve

$$
L\chi_\ell=\lambda_\ell\chi_\ell.
$$

The quadratic form

$$
f^\top Lf=\frac12\sum_{(n,m)\in E}\big(f(n)-f(m)\big)^2
$$

measures variation across edges. Eigenvectors with larger eigenvalues vary more strongly between connected vertices and are interpreted as higher-frequency graph modes.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-16.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph Laplacian eigenvectors ordered by frequency and smoothness." caption="Graph frequency is defined by variation across edges, measured by the Laplacian quadratic form. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 16." %}

The lecture visualizes this ordering on molecular and graph structures: low-frequency modes vary slowly across edges, whereas high-frequency modes alternate more rapidly.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-17.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Low and high graph Fourier modes on graph structures." caption="Laplacian eigenvectors provide a graph-specific spectrum from smooth to rapidly varying signals. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 17." %}

Collect the orthonormal eigenvectors into $$U=[\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_N]$$ so that

$$
L=U\Lambda U^\top.
$$

The graph Fourier transform computes coefficients $$\widehat f=U^\top f$$, and the inverse reconstructs $$f=U\widehat f$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-18.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph Fourier transform as a change of basis into Laplacian eigenvectors." caption="Graph Fourier analysis is an eigenvector change of basis defined by the graph Laplacian. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 18." %}

This gives a one-to-one dictionary. The one-dimensional Laplace operator becomes the graph Laplacian; complex exponential eigenfunctions become Laplacian eigenvectors; Fourier-series coefficients become $$U^\top f$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Dictionary between classical and graph Fourier transforms." caption="Classical and graph Fourier transforms share the same Laplacian-eigenbasis construction. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 19." %}

On a cycle graph, the graph construction recovers the familiar discrete Fourier modes. The unordered graph Laplacian has eigenvalues corresponding to sinusoidal frequencies, and a complex eigenbasis can be chosen to recover the DFT matrix.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Cycle-graph Laplacian eigenvalues and discrete Fourier eigenvectors." caption="The cycle graph links the abstract graph transform back to ordinary discrete Fourier analysis. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 20." %}

The lecture then displays the corresponding matrix explicitly, emphasizing that the DFT is one special Laplacian eigenvector transform rather than an unrelated construction.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-21.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Discrete Fourier transform matrix for the cycle graph." caption="A suitable complex eigenbasis of the cycle Laplacian yields the standard DFT matrix. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 21." %}

### Filtering, convolution, and spectral GNNs

Filtering modifies frequency coefficients. For a spectral response $$\widehat h$$,

$$
\widehat g=\widehat h\odot\widehat f,
\qquad
g=U\operatorname{diag}(\widehat h)U^\top f.
$$

Suppressing high-frequency coefficients smooths the signal.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-22.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Frequency-domain filtering and its matrix form." caption="A graph filter changes Laplacian-basis coefficients element by element. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 22." %}

For ordinary discrete time, convolution in the original domain becomes multiplication in the Fourier domain. This is the convolution theorem.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Discrete convolution and the convolution theorem." caption="The convolution theorem turns convolution into coefficient-wise multiplication after a Fourier transform. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 23." %}

Graph signal processing adopts the same spectral operation as its definition:

$$
g=U\operatorname{diag}(\widehat h)U^\top f.
$$

Thus a graph convolution matrix is any $$C=U\operatorname{diag}(\widehat h)U^\top$$ sharing eigenvectors with $$L$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph convolution implemented by graph Fourier transform, spectral multiplication, and inverse transform." caption="Graph convolution is defined by transforming into the Laplacian basis, multiplying frequencies, and transforming back. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 24." %}

Directly learning one coefficient per eigenvalue is expensive: the eigendecomposition costs substantial computation, and the parameter vector depends on the number of vertices. It also ties the filter to one graph basis.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-25.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Spectral GNN parameterization and its computational limitations." caption="A free spectral response requires Laplacian eigenvectors and graph-size-dependent parameters. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 25." %}

ChebNet replaces a free diagonal response with a polynomial of the rescaled eigenvalues,

$$
\widehat h(\Lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\theta_kT_k(\widetilde\Lambda),
$$

where $$T_k$$ is a Chebyshev polynomial. Because $$U T_k(\widetilde\Lambda)U^\top=T_k(\widetilde L)$$, the filter can be evaluated without explicitly computing eigenvectors. The GCN construction keeps a first-order form.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Chebyshev approximation of a spectral graph filter and first-order GCN." caption="Polynomial spectral filters avoid explicit eigendecomposition; GCN retains a first-order approximation. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 26." %}

With self-loops and symmetric degree normalization, the resulting GCN layer is

$$
H^{(\ell+1)}=sigma\left(
\widetilde D^{-1/2}\widetilde A\widetilde D^{-1/2}
H^{(\ell)}W_\ell
\right),
$$

where $$\widetilde A=A+I$$. The familiar neighborhood update therefore arrives as an approximation of graph spectral convolution.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Final normalized GCN update derived from first-order graph convolution." caption="The standard GCN propagation rule is the endpoint of the first-order spectral derivation. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 27." %}

The same basis also explains Laplacian positional encodings. Graph eigenvectors generalize the sinusoidal basis, and $$\chi_\ell(n)$$ supplies a frequency-indexed coordinate for node $$n$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-28.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Laplacian eigenvectors used as graph positional encodings." caption="Laplacian positional encodings reuse graph Fourier modes as node coordinates. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 28." %}

## Route II: characterize equivariant linear operators

The spectral construction is persuasive when one graph supports many signals. It becomes less natural when the graph itself varies across examples, because each input has a different Laplacian basis. The second route begins from a broader definition: convolution is a linear operator equivariant to a chosen transformation group.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-31.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Limits of graph signal processing when graph structure varies with input." caption="A graph-specific Fourier basis does not align cleanly with datasets in which the graph itself changes. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 31." %}

Ordinary convolutions in one, two, and three dimensions are translation-equivariant linear maps. This suggests asking for the complete space of linear maps equivariant to permutation rather than starting from a frequency basis.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-32.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Translation-equivariant convolution and permutation-equivariant graph operator question." caption="The alternative definition treats convolution as a linear map that commutes with the domain symmetry. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 32." %}

### Shift equivariance recovers convolution

For periodic discrete time, let $$A$$ be the cyclic shift. The translation group is generated by $$I,A,A^2,\ldots,A^{N-1}$$. A linear operator $$C$$ is shift-equivariant when

$$
ACx=CAx
$$

for every $$x$$, equivalently $$AC=CA$$. Commuting matrices share eigenvectors, connecting this route back to the spectral one.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-33.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Cyclic shift group and commutation condition for a linear operator." caption="Shift equivariance is the commutation relation between a linear map and the cyclic shift. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 33." %}

A matrix commutes with the cyclic shift if and only if it is circulant. Multiplication by a circulant matrix is precisely periodic discrete convolution. The equivariance definition therefore reproduces ordinary convolution rather than inventing a different operator.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-34.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Circulant matrix as the complete class of shift-equivariant linear maps." caption="The commutant of the cyclic shift is the space of circulant convolution matrices. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 34." %}

### Permutation-equivariant maps on nodes

Now require equivariance to every permutation matrix $$P$$:

$$
PCx=CPx\qquad\text{for all }P.
$$

For scalar node features, the complete solution is

$$
C=w_1I+w_2\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top.
$$

The identity term transforms each node independently; the all-ones term pools globally and broadcasts the result back to every node.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-35.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Identity and all-ones bases for permutation-equivariant linear node maps." caption="For scalar node features, identity plus global pooling and broadcasting span every permutation-equivariant linear map. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 35." %}

### Higher-order tensors and fixed points

Graphs also carry edge-valued matrices and higher-order tensors. If $$X\in\mathbb R^{n^\ell}$$ has degree $$\ell$$, then a linear operator between such tensors has more indices and potentially more equivariant basis elements.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Node, edge, and higher-order tensor features on graphs." caption="Moving from node features to edges and higher-order relations enlarges both the feature space and the linear operator. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 36." %}

The desired condition is that applying the permutation before or after the linear map gives the same result. Invariant maps remove the output action; equivariant maps retain it.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-37.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Permutation action on higher-order tensors and invariant or equivariant linear maps." caption="Higher-order operator spaces are defined by the same commuting diagram with the appropriate tensor action. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 37." %}

For an edge-to-edge map, vectorizing the matrix-valued feature makes the action $$P\otimes P$$. Equivariance becomes

$$
L(P\otimes P)=(P\otimes P)L.
$$

After conjugation and vectorization, this is a fixed-point equation:

$$
(P\otimes P\otimes P\otimes P)\operatorname{vec}(L)
=\operatorname{vec}(L).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-38.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Reduction of edge-to-edge equivariance to a tensor fixed-point equation." caption="Vectorization turns the commutation constraint into invariance of the operator tensor under four simultaneous index permutations. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 38." %}

Simultaneous permutation does not change which indices are equal. The operator entries must therefore be constant within each equality pattern: all four indices equal, two equal pairs, three equal and one different, and so on.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-39.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Equality patterns induced by the higher-order fixed-point equation." caption="Permutation orbits of operator entries are determined by equality patterns among their indices. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 39." %}

For degree-$$\ell$$ input and output tensors, the number of basis elements is the Bell number $$\operatorname{Bell}(2\ell)$$, the number of set partitions of the $$2\ell$$ operator indices. Each partition defines a binary indicator tensor for one equality pattern, and any equivariant linear map is a learned linear combination of these bases.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-40.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Bell-number basis of permutation-equivariant tensor operators." caption="Set partitions index a complete basis for higher-order permutation-equivariant linear maps. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 40." %}

An interpretable view rewrites these bases as combinations of pooling selected indices and broadcasting into new axes. The abstract equality-pattern classification therefore corresponds to concrete tensor operations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-41.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Pooling and broadcasting interpretation of equivariant tensor bases." caption="Permutation-equivariant linear maps can be expressed through index pooling and broadcasting. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 41." %}

The lecture illustrates how different equality-pattern bases retain diagonals, pool rows or columns, broadcast summaries, or extract global statistics from a matrix-valued feature.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-42.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Examples of matrix pooling and broadcasting basis operations." caption="Concrete degree-two bases act by selecting, pooling, and broadcasting matrix entries in permutation-consistent ways. ## Why ordinary GNNs are less restricted

For node features alone, a purely linear operator equivariant to every permutation can only combine identity with global pooling and broadcasting. A typical GNN layer instead uses the adjacency matrix as an input-dependent equivariant tensor:

$$
X\longmapsto \sigma(AXW).
$$

The product $$AX$$ is bilinear in two equivariant inputs rather than one fixed linear map of $$X$$. Node-wise linear maps and nonlinearities also preserve permutation equivariance without belonging to the restricted global linear commutant.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-04/slide-43.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Comparison of globally permutation-equivariant linear maps and adjacency-conditioned GNN layers." caption="GNNs gain locality and flexibility by multiplying equivariant adjacency and feature tensors and then applying shared node-wise transformations. Cropped from the 2025 GDL graph-convolution lecture, slide 43." %}

The two derivations therefore answer different questions. Graph signal processing defines convolution relative to the Laplacian of one graph and yields spectral filters and their polynomial approximations. The equivariant-operator route classifies maps that commute with an entire symmetry group. Ordinary message-passing GNNs combine both ideas with an input graph, tensor products, shared nonlinear maps, and local computation.
