## ODEs, SDEs, and probability paths

The lecture begins in one dimension, with $$x_t\in\mathbb R$$, so that the distinction between deterministic and stochastic motion is visible without vector notation. An ordinary differential equation specifies a deterministic velocity field,

$$
dx_t=f(x_t,t)\,dt.
$$

The differential notation abbreviates an integral relation,

$$
x_{t+h}-x_t=\int_t^{t+h}f(x_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

Given an initial value and suitable regularity, the trajectory is fixed. A random initial condition therefore induces a time-indexed probability distribution even though each conditional trajectory is deterministic.

On a time mesh with step $$h$$, the integral relation suggests the Euler update

$$
x_{t+h}\approx x_t+h f(x_t,t).
$$

The ODE is the continuous limit of these local displacements. Numerical generation later uses the same idea: a neural network supplies the velocity, while an ODE solver turns local predictions into a complete trajectory. The solver and velocity model are separate objects.


A stochastic differential equation adds noise,

$$
dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t.
$$

The drift $$\mu$$ describes systematic motion. The diffusion coefficient $$\sigma$$ sets the noise scale. The differential notation stands for

$$
X_{t+h}-X_t
=\int_t^{t+h}\mu(X_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau
+\int_t^{t+h}\sigma(X_\tau,\tau)\,dW_\tau.
$$

The first integral is ordinary. The second is interpreted through Itô calculus because a Brownian path has no ordinary time derivative.


### Brownian motion

A Wiener process starts from $$W_0=0$$, has Gaussian increments

$$
W_{t+\Delta}-W_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\Delta),
$$

and has independent increments on non-overlapping time intervals. On a partition of $$[0,1]$$ into $$n$$ pieces, one may take

$$
\Delta W_i\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\frac1n\right),
\qquad
W_{k/n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta W_i.
$$

The continuous limit gives Brownian motion. A typical path is continuous but nowhere differentiable, which is why $$dW_t$$ cannot be treated as an ordinary velocity times $$dt$$.

### The Fokker–Planck equation

The SDE describes random trajectories; the Fokker–Planck–Kolmogorov equation describes their density. In one dimension,

$$
\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\mu(x,t)p(x,t)\right)
+\frac12\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}
\!\left(\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)\right).
$$

An ODE is the special case $$\sigma=0$$.


It is useful to define probability current

$$
J(x,t)=\mu(x,t)p(x,t)
-\frac12\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\!\left(\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)\right),
$$

so that $$\partial_t p=-\partial_xJ$$. Probability in a short interval changes by the incoming current minus the outgoing current. The drift term transports mass at velocity $$\mu$$. The diffusion term moves mass down gradients created by neighboring density and noise strength.

Integrating over an interval makes the conservation statement explicit:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_a^b p(x,t)\,dx
=J(a,t)-J(b,t).
$$

When $$\sigma$$ is constant, the diffusive current is $$-(\sigma^2/2)\partial_xp$$. It points from higher density toward lower density. When $$\sigma$$ varies with position, the gradient acts on $$\sigma^2p$$, so spatially varying noise also contributes to the flux.

The lecture visualizes the two contributions separately. The drift current $$\mu p$$ moves a density profile coherently at the local velocity $$\mu$$. Diffusion can be pictured as opposing local stochastic motions. Those motions cancel in a spatially uniform density; when neighboring densities differ, their imbalance produces the net current that smooths the profile.




### Langevin dynamics and continuity

For an energy $$U(x)$$, overdamped Langevin dynamics has the form

$$
dX_t=-\nabla U(X_t)\,dt+\sqrt{2\tau}\,dW_t.
$$

Its stationary density is $$p_\infty(x)\propto\exp[-U(x)/\tau]$$ under suitable boundary conditions. Substituting $$\mu=-\nabla U$$ and $$\sigma^2=2\tau$$ into the probability current gives

$$
J=-p\nabla U-\tau\nabla p.
$$

At equilibrium with zero current, $$\nabla\log p=-\nabla U/\tau$$, which integrates to the Boltzmann form. The energy supplies an attracting drift while diffusion prevents collapse to one minimum.

Lecture plot by the author." %}

For a deterministic velocity $$u(x,t)$$ and random initial condition $$X_0\sim p_0$$,

$$
dX_t=u(X_t,t)\,dt
$$

induces the continuity equation

$$
\partial_t p_t(x)=-\nabla\cdot\bigl(u(x,t)p_t(x)\bigr).
$$

This is the zero-diffusion limit of Fokker–Planck. It is also the governing equation behind continuous normalizing flows and flow matching. The equation constrains a pair, not either object alone: a prescribed velocity determines a density path from its initial condition, while a prescribed density path can admit more than one velocity unless further structure is imposed.


## Diffusion models and reverse-time SDEs

A diffusion model begins from data and evolves it toward a simple noise law. A variance-preserving example is

$$
dX_t=-\frac12\beta(t)X_t\,dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t,
\qquad X_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}.
$$

Define

$$
\alpha_t=\exp\!\left[-\frac12\int_0^t\beta(s)\,ds\right].
$$

The linear SDE has the conditional law

$$
X_t\mid X_0=x_0
\sim\mathcal N\!\left(
\alpha_t x_0,
(1-\alpha_t^2)I
\right).
$$

As the integrated noise schedule grows, $$\alpha_T$$ approaches zero. The endpoint forgets $$x_0$$ and approaches a standard Gaussian. The generative problem is to begin from that Gaussian and recover the data distribution by reversing the same one-time marginal path.

Reversing an Itô process is not achieved by placing a minus sign in front of a stochastic integral. Itô integration is time-oriented, and the reverse process needs a different drift.

### Forward and backward descriptions

Write the forward SDE as

$$
dX_t=f(X_t,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW_t.
$$

A backward SDE traversing the same one-time marginal densities can be written with a decreasing clock and a backward Wiener process,

$$
d\bar X_t=\bar f(\bar X_t,t)\,dt+g(t)\,d\bar W_t,
\qquad t:T\longrightarrow 0.
$$

Equivalently, define a new forward clock $$s=T-t$$ and $$Y_s=\bar X_{T-s}$$. Brownian symmetry absorbs the sign of the noise increment, giving

$$
dY_s=-\bar f(Y_s,T-s)\,ds+g(T-s)\,dW_s.
$$

The two notations differ in signs because one integrates from $$T$$ down to zero while the other runs $$s$$ from zero to $$T$$. Keeping the clock convention explicit prevents an apparent contradiction between reverse-SDE formulas.

Substituting Nelson's drift into the forward-clock form gives the generative SDE

$$
dY_s=
\left[
-f(Y_s,T-s)
+g(T-s)^2\nabla_y\log p_{T-s}(Y_s)
\right]ds
+g(T-s)\,dW_s.
$$

The score points toward regions of higher probability under the partially noised distribution. It is not a direct prediction of the clean sample; it modifies the drift at every noise level so the reversed marginal path matches the forward one.

### Nelson's identity

Let $$f$$ and $$\bar f$$ be the forward and backward drifts associated with the same density path and diffusion coefficient $$g(t)$$. Nelson's identity gives

$$
f(x,t)-\bar f(x,t)=g(t)^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x).
$$

The extra term is the score of the current marginal density. For scalar $$g(t)$$, the forward and backward density equations are

$$
\partial_t p_t
=-\nabla\cdot(fp_t)+\frac12g(t)^2\Delta p_t,
$$

$$
\partial_t p_t
=-\nabla\cdot(\bar f p_t)-\frac12g(t)^2\Delta p_t.
$$

Equating them gives

$$
\nabla\cdot\left[(f-\bar f)p_t-g(t)^2\nabla p_t\right]=0.
$$

Under the zero-current boundary condition used in the lecture, the quantity inside the divergence vanishes. Dividing by $$p_t$$ yields Nelson's identity.



The reverse-time model must therefore estimate

$$
\bar f(x,t)=f(x,t)-g(t)^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x).
$$

The forward drift and schedule are known. Learning reduces to estimating the score $$\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$$.


### Denoising score matching

Although the marginal score is intractable, the corruption kernel $$p_{t|0}(x_t\mid x_0)$$ is chosen to be tractable. The denoising identity is

$$
\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)
=
\mathbb E\!\left[
\nabla_{x_t}\log p_{t|0}(x_t\mid X_0)
\mid X_t=x_t
\right].
$$

The identity follows directly from the mixture density:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)
&=\frac{1}{p_t(x_t)}
\int \nabla_{x_t}p_{t|0}(x_t\mid x_0)p_0(x_0)\,dx_0\\
&=\int \nabla_{x_t}\log p_{t|0}(x_t\mid x_0)
p(x_0\mid x_t)\,dx_0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The conditioning posterior is intractable to integrate explicitly, but it appears automatically when examples are sampled from the joint corruption process.

Training samples a data point, a time, and a corrupted state, then minimizes

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\|s_\theta(X_t,t)-
\nabla_{x_t}\log p_{t|0}(X_t\mid X_0)
\right\|^2
\right].
$$

The conditional target has the same conditional expectation as the marginal score, so squared regression learns the required marginal object without evaluating it directly. Once trained, the score model is inserted into $$\bar f=f-g^2s_\theta$$ and the reverse SDE is integrated from the Gaussian endpoint back toward data.


## Flow matching in Euclidean space

A continuous normalizing flow seeks an ODE

$$
dX_t=u_\theta(X_t,t)\,dt
$$

whose probability path connects a simple prior to the data. The ODE defines an invertible flow map $$X_t=\psi_t(X_0)$$ and the push-forward relation $$p_t=(\psi_t)_\#p_0$$. Unlike the forward diffusion convention above, flow matching takes $$X_0$$ to be noise and $$X_1$$ to be data. Direct maximum likelihood requires integrating both the state and a divergence term, which can be expensive during training.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s23-image21.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Continuous normalizing flow transporting a simple density into a data density." caption="A continuous normalizing flow transports a simple source density into a target density through an invertible ODE. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Neklyudov et al. (2024), Flow Matching: A Guide and Code</a>." %}

Flow matching avoids simulating the learned ODE during training. It begins by choosing a family of tractable conditional probability paths $$p_t(x\mid z)$$, each tied to a target example $$z$$, with a known conditional velocity $$u_t(x\mid z)$$.

The four objects in the lecture's diagram have distinct roles. The conditional velocity generates each conditional path through

$$
\partial_t p_t(x\mid z)
=-\nabla\cdot\left(u_t(x\mid z)p_t(x\mid z)\right).
$$

Mixing the conditional paths produces the marginal path. The missing object is the marginal velocity that generates that mixture. Flow matching is the problem of learning this fourth object from the other three.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s24-image23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Several conditional probability paths leading from a prior to different data examples." caption="Conditional paths are tractable, while their mixture and posterior-averaged velocity define the desired marginal flow. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Neklyudov et al. (2024), Flow Matching: A Guide and Code</a>." %}

Marginalizing the target example creates the desired probability path,

$$
p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid z)p_{\mathrm{data}}(z)\,dz,
$$

with $$p_0$$ equal to the prior and $$p_1$$ equal to the data distribution under the chosen convention.


### Gaussian conditional paths

The simplest construction samples independent endpoints $$x_0\sim p_0$$ and $$x_1\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$$ and interpolates

$$
x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1.
$$

The conditional velocity is constant,

$$
u_t(x_t\mid x_0,x_1)=x_1-x_0.
$$

If the path is conditioned only on the data endpoint $$x_1$$ and $$x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$$ is marginalized out, the same construction becomes

$$
p_t(x\mid x_1)
=\mathcal N\!\left(x\,\middle|\,t x_1,(1-t)^2I\right),
\qquad
u_t(x\mid x_1)=\frac{x_1-x}{1-t}.
$$

Along a sampled straight path, $$(x_1-x_t)/(1-t)=x_1-x_0$$. The endpoint-pair and Gaussian-path views therefore describe the same interpolation at different conditioning levels. Other Gaussian schedules can retain a nonzero endpoint width; changing the schedule changes the conditional target and path geometry, but not the marginalization principle.

### The marginalization trick

The marginal velocity is not an unconditional average of conditional velocities. At a particular location $$x$$, it is the posterior average over paths that pass through that location:

$$
u_t(x)=\int u_t(x\mid z)\,p(z\mid X_t=x)\,dz.
$$

Substituting this expression into the continuity equation shows that it generates the marginal density $$p_t$$. Indeed,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t p_t(x)
&=\int \partial_t p_t(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz\\
&=-\nabla\cdot\int u_t(x\mid z)p_t(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz\\
&=-\nabla\cdot\left[p_t(x)
\int u_t(x\mid z)p(z\mid x)\,dz\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The posterior weighting is essential: two conditional paths that visit different regions should not contribute equally at the same $$x$$.


The same identity applies at three conditioning levels shown in the lecture. Conditioning on $$X_1$$ produces a path $$p_{t\mid1}(x\mid x_1)$$ and averages velocities over $$p(x_1\mid X_t=x)$$. Conditioning on $$X_0$$ uses $$p_{t\mid0}(x\mid x_0)$$ and the posterior over source points. Conditioning on the pair $$(X_0,X_1)$$ uses a coupling $$\pi_{0,1}$$ and

$$
u_t(x)=
\mathbb E\!\left[
u_t(X_t\mid X_0,X_1)
\,\middle|\,X_t=x
\right].
$$

Independent coupling is convenient but not mandatory. An informed coupling can shorten trajectories or align related structures while preserving the same marginal endpoint distributions.

### Conditional flow matching

Regressing directly onto $$u_t(x)$$ would require the intractable posterior. The ideal loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,X_t\sim p_t}\left[
\left\|u_\theta(X_t,t)-u_t(X_t)\right\|^2
\right],
$$

where

$$
u_t(X_t)=\int u_t(X_t\mid z)p(z\mid X_t)\,dz.
$$

The posterior inside this target cannot usually be evaluated. Instead train with

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{t,z,X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid z)}
\left[
\left\|u_\theta(X_t,t)-u_t(X_t\mid z)\right\|^2
\right].
$$

The conditional and marginal squared losses differ by a term independent of $$\theta$$. Expanding the conditional square, then conditioning on $$X_t=x$$, replaces the cross term with the posterior mean $$u_t(x)$$. The remaining conditional-target variance contains no model parameters, so

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)
=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta).
$$

This is the flow analogue of the denoising-score identity: both learn an intractable marginal field by regression on a tractable conditional field.



Gaussian conditional flow matching is therefore a simulation-free training recipe. Draw

$$
x_0\sim p_0,
\qquad
x_1\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},
\qquad
t\sim\operatorname{Uniform}[0,1],
$$

construct $$x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1$$, and minimize

$$
\mathbb E\left[
\left\|u_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\right\|^2
\right].
$$

No learned ODE is solved during training. Sampling still begins with $$X_0\sim p_0$$ and numerically integrates $$\dot X_t=u_\theta(X_t,t)$$ to $$t=1$$.

## Geometric flow matching on manifolds

Protein structures do not live in one unconstrained Euclidean vector space. A backbone with $$N$$ residue frames is naturally represented in $$SE(3)^N$$: each residue contributes a translation and a local orientation around its $$C_\alpha$$ atom. Side-chain torsions live in products of $$SO(2)$$ circles, while amino-acid identities are discrete. A model that interpolates all of these as ordinary vectors can leave the valid state space or ignore periodic equivalences.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s34-image44.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Protein represented by residue frames, side-chain angles, and discrete sequence identities." caption="Protein generation combines residue frames, periodic side-chain torsions, and discrete sequence variables. Exact composite source unclear." %}

Many structure-design models inherit the frame-based representation introduced by AlphaFold2. Flow-based examples include FoldFlow and FrameFlow, followed by systems such as Proteus, MultiFlow, PEPFlow, and PPFlow. Their details differ, but they share the need to define motion on the geometry of the represented variables.

The borrowed representation does not make these models AlphaFold2 variants in the predictive sense. AlphaFold2 maps sequence, MSA, and template information to a structure estimate. A flow model instead assigns a time-dependent tangent velocity to a partially generated structure and integrates that field. The common interface is the residue-level frame representation; the supervision and downstream computation are different.

For a product state space, the geometry is applied component by component. Translational velocities remain Euclidean, rotational velocities lie in tangent spaces of $$SO(3)$$, and torsional velocities wrap around circles. One neural model may predict all of them, but each output is integrated with the operation appropriate to its state variable.
This keeps each numerical update inside the valid product space rather than repairing invalid coordinates after generation.

### Manifolds and charts

A manifold $$\mathcal M$$ is a topological space that locally resembles Euclidean space. Each chart

$$
\phi_\alpha:U_\alpha\subset\mathcal M\to V_\alpha\subset\mathbb R^d
$$

provides coordinates on a patch. The manifold need not be a vector space: adding two rotations or two points on a sphere does not generally produce another valid element.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s36-image47.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Curved manifold covered by local coordinate patches and maps to Euclidean charts." caption="A manifold is covered by local Euclidean coordinate charts even though the global space need not be linear. Exact visual source unclear." %}

A smooth manifold requires transition maps

$$
\phi_\beta\circ\phi_\alpha^{-1}:
\phi_\alpha(U_\alpha\cap U_\beta)
\longrightarrow
\phi_\beta(U_\alpha\cap U_\beta)
$$

to be infinitely differentiable wherever two charts overlap. This compatibility condition makes derivatives independent of which chart is used. A single coordinate system need not cover the whole manifold; calculus is assembled consistently from overlapping local descriptions.

### Tangent spaces and the metric

At $$p\in\mathcal M$$, the tangent space $$T_p\mathcal M$$ is a vector space of local directions. If a curve $$\gamma$$ satisfies $$\gamma(0)=p$$, its velocity defines the directional-derivative operator

$$
X_{\gamma,p}[f]
=\left.\frac{d}{dt}(f\circ\gamma)(t)\right|_{t=0}
$$

on smooth test functions $$f$$. A tangent vector belongs to $$T_p\mathcal M$$, not generally to the manifold itself.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s38-image51.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent plane attached to a point on a sphere." caption="The tangent plane contains the locally available linear directions at one point on the sphere. Exact visual source unclear." %}

A Riemannian metric assigns an inner product

$$
g_p:T_p\mathcal M\times T_p\mathcal M\to\mathbb R
$$

smoothly at every point. It defines vector norms, curve lengths, angles, and gradients.

In local coordinates the metric has a positive-definite matrix $$G(p)$$, so

$$
g_p(u,v)=u^\top G(p)v.
$$


A geodesic is a locally length-minimizing curve. For $$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal M$$,

$$
L(\gamma)=\int_0^1\sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t))}\,dt.
$$

On a sphere, great-circle arcs are geodesics; straight chords are not manifold paths.

### Exponential and logarithmic maps

The exponential map sends a tangent vector $$v\in T_p\mathcal M$$ along the geodesic starting at $$p$$ with initial velocity $$v$$ and returns its point at unit time:

$$
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=\gamma_v(1).
$$

In Euclidean space this reduces to ordinary addition, $$\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=p+v$$. On a curved manifold the result remains a valid point of $$\mathcal M$$ even though $$p+v$$ is not defined intrinsically.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s41-image57.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent vector on a sphere mapped along a geodesic by the exponential map." caption="The exponential map follows a tangent vector along its geodesic to a point on the manifold. Exact visual source unclear." %}

The logarithmic map locally reverses this operation,

$$
\operatorname{Log}_p(q)\in T_p\mathcal M,
\qquad
\operatorname{Exp}_p(\operatorname{Log}_p(q))=q,
$$

when a unique chosen geodesic connects $$p$$ to $$q$$. At a cut locus the logarithm can be nonunique.

### Riemannian flow matching

An ODE on a manifold requires its velocity to lie in the current tangent space:

$$
\dot x_t=u_t(x_t),
\qquad
u_t(x_t)\in T_{x_t}\mathcal M.
$$

The notation $$dx_t=u_t\,dt$$ is shorthand for finding a curve whose derivative is the given tangent vector field.

A geometric Euler step uses the exponential map,

$$
x_{t+h}\approx\operatorname{Exp}_{x_t}\!\left(h\,u_t(x_t)\right),
$$

which stays on the manifold. A constant-speed geodesic from $$x_0$$ to target $$z$$ is

$$
x_t=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}
\!\left(t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(z)\right).
$$

At the current point, its conditional velocity can be written

$$
u_t(x_t\mid z)=\frac{1}{1-t}\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(z).
$$

The factor $$1/(1-t)$$ converts the remaining geodesic displacement into a velocity per unit remaining time. Although the logarithmic displacement shrinks to zero as $$t\to1$$, their ratio stays equal to the constant-speed tangent transported along the geodesic. This is the manifold replacement for $$(x_1-x_t)/(1-t)$$ in the Euclidean Gaussian path.

Exact visual source unclear." %}


The corresponding conditional flow map is

$$
\psi_t(x_0\mid z)
=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}\!\left(
t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(z)
\right).
$$

In Euclidean space, the Gaussian conditional path is centered on this curve. On a manifold, the probability path must instead be defined with respect to the manifold geometry and its volume measure; a Euclidean Gaussian cannot simply be pasted onto the coordinates globally.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s47-image66.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Conditional flow following a geodesic toward a target on a curved manifold." caption="A geometric conditional path follows a geodesic while its velocity remains in the current tangent space. Related method: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03660'>Chen & Lipman (2023), Riemannian Flow Matching</a>; exact visual source unclear." %}

The conditional path induces a marginal density exactly as in Euclidean flow matching. The loss replaces the Euclidean norm with the Riemannian metric,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E\left[
g_{X_t}\!\left(
u_\theta(X_t,t)-u_t(X_t\mid Z),
u_\theta(X_t,t)-u_t(X_t\mid Z)
\right)
\right].
$$

At a fixed current point $$x$$, every conditional velocity lies in the same vector space $$T_x\mathcal M$$. The posterior average is therefore well-defined:

$$
u_t(x)=\mathbb E\!\left[
u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x
\right]\in T_x\mathcal M.
$$

The intractable marginal loss measures $$u_\theta(x,t)-u_t(x)$$ with the same metric. Expanding the conditional squared metric norm leaves a conditional variance term independent of $$\theta$$, so

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta)
=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RFM}}(\theta).
$$

The marginalization trick is statistical; the tangent constraint, Exp/Log maps, and metric are the geometric replacements needed to make that statistic meaningful on $$\mathcal M$$.


With suitable exponential and logarithmic maps, the construction applies to $$SO(3)$$ rotations. The learned field produces an angular velocity in the tangent space of the current rotation, and integration stays on the rotation group. A probability simplex is also a manifold in its interior; Fisher flow matching relaxes categorical data to the simplex and equips it with the Fisher–Rao metric. This relaxation keeps a continuous path while respecting the geometry induced by categorical probabilities.

## Discrete flow matching

Some variables should remain discrete throughout generation. Sequence tokens, atom types, bonds, and graph edits can evolve through a continuous-time Markov chain while coordinates evolve through a continuous flow. The lecture develops the discrete construction by translating the same three questions: choose a conditional probability path and velocity, marginalize the conditional velocity, and train with a tractable conditional loss.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s52-image70.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Molecular structures changing discrete atom and bond identities while retaining three-dimensional geometry." caption="FlowMol compares continuous simplex paths and discrete CTMC paths for atom identities while a shared flow evolves molecular geometry. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19739'>Dunn & Koes (2024), Mixed Continuous and Categorical Flow Matching for 3D De Novo Molecule Generation</a>." %}

### Continuous-time Markov chains

Let the vocabulary be $$\mathcal T$$ and a length-$$d$$ state be $$x=(x^1,\ldots,x^d)\in\mathcal S=\mathcal T^d$$. The lecture's toy vocabulary is the alphabet and the states are fixed-length words such as “Cat,” “Dog,” and “Car.” A continuous-time Markov chain is specified by transition rates $$u_t(y,x)$$ from current state $$x$$ to destination $$y$$. For a small step $$h$$,

$$
\Pr(X_{t+h}=y\mid X_t=x)
=\delta_{yx}+h\,u_t(y,x)+o(h).
$$

Off-diagonal rates must be nonnegative, and the diagonal is set so that $$\sum_yu_t(y,x)=0$$. The state remains unchanged between random jump events.

The diagonal rate is therefore

$$
u_t(x,x)=-\sum_{y\ne x}u_t(y,x).
$$

Over a short interval, the probability of no jump is $$1+h\,u_t(x,x)+o(h)$$, while the probability of jumping to a particular $$y\ne x$$ is $$h\,u_t(y,x)+o(h)$$. Larger total outgoing rate means a shorter expected waiting time before the next event.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s53-image71.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three fixed-length word states labeled Cat, Dog, and Car in a discrete state space." caption="A fixed-length word space provides a finite toy state space for discrete flow matching. Lecture example by the author." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s54-image72.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Discrete state lattice with arrows from one state to possible destinations labeled by transition rates." caption="A CTMC assigns an instantaneous rate from the current state to every allowed destination. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Neklyudov et al. (2024), Flow Matching: A Guide and Code</a>." %}

The probability path obeys the Kolmogorov forward equation,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y)=\sum_xu_t(y,x)p_t(x).
$$

This is a discrete continuity equation: probability flows into and out of each state along graph edges in state space.

Using the zero-column-sum convention, the same equation separates into incoming and outgoing flux:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y)
=\sum_{x\ne y}u_t(y,x)p_t(x)
-\sum_{x\ne y}u_t(x,y)p_t(y).
$$


### Conditional and marginal rates

Choose a conditional jump process with rates $$u_t(y,x\mid z)$$ and probability path $$p_t(x\mid z)$$ ending at target $$z$$. Marginalizing over targets gives $$p_t(x)=\sum_zp_t(x\mid z)p(z)$$. The rate analogue of the velocity identity is

$$
u_t(y,x)=\sum_z u_t(y,x\mid z)\,p(z\mid X_t=x).
$$

The proof substitutes the conditional master equations into the derivative of the mixture:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}p_t(y)
&=\sum_z\sum_x u_t(y,x\mid z)p_t(x\mid z)p(z)\\
&=\sum_x\left[
\sum_z u_t(y,x\mid z)p(z\mid x)
\right]p_t(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The bracketed posterior average is exactly the marginal rate. The calculation mirrors the continuous proof, with a finite sum replacing the divergence of a probability current.


Directly fitting the marginal rate would require that posterior. The marginal objective

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,X_t}\left[
\left\|u_t(\cdot,X_t)-u_\theta(\cdot,X_t,t)\right\|
\right]
$$

is therefore intractable. Discrete conditional flow matching replaces it with

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CDFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E_{t,Z,X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid Z)}\left[
\left\|u_t(\cdot,X_t\mid Z)-u_\theta(\cdot,X_t,t)\right\|
\right].
$$

As in continuous flow matching, the tractable conditional objective targets the marginal generator after averaging. The norm or divergence must be chosen for rate vectors, but the conditional-to-marginal argument is the same.


### Factorized paths and rates

The full state space has size $$|\mathcal T|^d$$, so enumerating every destination is impossible for long sequences. Even a 100-dimensional binary state has $$2^{100}$$ possibilities. A factorized generator permits one coordinate to change at a time:

$$
u_t(y,x)=
\sum_{i=1}^d
\mathbf 1[y^{-i}=x^{-i}]
u_t^i(y^i,x).
$$

Here $$y^{-i}=x^{-i}$$ means that every coordinate except $$i$$ is unchanged. The model outputs $$d$$ categorical rate vectors rather than one vector over the exponential joint space. Dependence is not removed: each coordinate rate $$u_t^i(y^i,x)$$ may still condition on the entire current state $$x$$.


For a small Euler step, the coordinate-wise kernels multiply to first order,

$$
\Pr(X_{t+h}=y\mid X_t=x)
=\prod_{i=1}^{d}
\left[
\delta_{y^i,x^i}+h\,u_t^i(y^i,x)+o(h)
\right].
$$

Simultaneous changes in two coordinates have probability $$O(h^2)$$, so the product kernel agrees with the single-jump CTMC up to $$o(h)$$. The corresponding conditional loss is a sum over coordinate-wise rate errors,

$$
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{i=1}^{d}
\left\|u_t^i(\cdot,X_t\mid Z)-u_\theta^i(\cdot,X_t,t)\right\|
\right].
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-06/s60-image74.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two coordinate-wise transition matrices used to form a factorized Euler step." caption="Coordinate factorization replaces one exponentially large rate vector with one categorical rate vector per coordinate. Source: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06264'>Neklyudov et al. (2024), Flow Matching: A Guide and Code</a>." %}

A practical conditional path begins from a coupling $$\pi_{0,1}(x_0,x_1)$$ and factorizes across coordinates,

$$
p_t(x\mid x_0,x_1)=\prod_i p_t^i(x^i\mid x_0,x_1).
$$

A simple schedule chooses the endpoint token with probability $$\kappa_t$$ and the source token with probability $$1-\kappa_t$$:

$$
p_t^i(a\mid x_0,x_1)
=\kappa_t\delta_{a,x_1^i}
+(1-\kappa_t)\delta_{a,x_0^i}.
$$

The boundary conditions $$\kappa_0=0$$ and $$\kappa_1=1$$ make the coordinate equal to its source token at the beginning and its target token at the end. Intermediate states are still categorical: a coordinate is not a fractional blend of two symbols. Only the probability of selecting each symbol changes continuously.

Differentiating the path and enforcing the master equation gives

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x^i\mid x_0,x_1)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\left[
\delta_{y^i,x_1^i}-\delta_{y^i,x^i}
\right].
$$


After marginalization, it is sufficient to learn a posterior over target tokens given the current state:

$$
u_t^i(y^i,x)
=\frac{\dot\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_t}
\sum_{x_1}
\left[
\delta_{y^i,x_1^i}-\delta_{y^i,x^i}
\right]
p(x_1\mid x).
$$

Plugging the predicted posterior into this analytic expression yields the model generator. This mirrors denoising diffusion: predict the clean endpoint from a corrupted state, then convert that prediction into the local reverse or generative dynamics.

Generation alternates between rate evaluation and a CTMC update. At the current state, the network predicts target-token posteriors, the analytic conversion produces coordinate-wise rates, and a small-time kernel or event-driven sampler chooses the next jump. Coordinates may be updated independently in a discretized step, but the continuous-time limit retains the one-jump-at-a-time generator derived above.


The deck's four parts are variations of one construction. A probability path is a family of one-time distributions; an ODE, SDE, or CTMC generator is a mechanism that realizes that path. These objects should not be conflated. The path says where probability mass must be at each time, while the velocity, drift, diffusion, or jump rates say how it moves between neighboring times.

Diffusion fixes a stochastic data-to-noise path and learns the score term needed to reverse it. Euclidean flow matching instead chooses tractable noise-to-data conditional paths and regresses their velocities. The learned field is not one conditional velocity: it is their posterior average at the current state. That conditional-to-marginal identity is why training can use labeled endpoint pairs even though sampling later runs an unconditional dynamics.

Riemannian flow matching preserves the statistical identity but changes every geometric operation. Velocities live in tangent spaces, Exp replaces vector addition, Log replaces endpoint subtraction, geodesics replace straight interpolation, and the Riemannian metric replaces the Euclidean squared norm. Discrete flow matching changes the dynamical object instead: a rate vector replaces a velocity, and the Kolmogorov equation replaces the continuity equation. Factorization makes the exponential product space computationally accessible without claiming that coordinates are statistically independent. The state space changes across the lecture; the distinction between a tractable conditional process and its learned marginal generator remains the organizing principle.
