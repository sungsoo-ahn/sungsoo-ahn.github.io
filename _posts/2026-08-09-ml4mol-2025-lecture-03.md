Drug discovery and material discovery sound like different disciplines. One begins with disease and biological targets; the other begins with desired electronic, mechanical, chemical, or transport behavior. Yet both face the same basic problem: an enormous space of candidates, incomplete measurements, costly physical experiments, and several constraints that must be satisfied at once.

Before asking where machine learning belongs, we need a rough map of the territory. What does a drug act on? What makes a target druggable? What do pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics measure? How does a hit become a lead? On the materials side, what distinguishes a conductor from an insulator, a stable phase from a metastable one, or a hypothetical structure from something that can be synthesized? This chapter follows those questions in the order in which they arise in discovery.

The drug-discovery discussion draws in part on MIT's [Machine Learning for Computational Biology lecture series](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLypiXJdtIca4gtioEPLIExlAKvu64z7rc). The emphasis here is selective rather than encyclopedic: the goal is to establish the biological, pharmacological, experimental, and physical vocabulary needed to interpret molecular-machine-learning papers.

## Part 1: drugs begin with cells

### From a human body to molecular machinery

A human body is organized across scales. Organs contain tissues; tissues contain specialized cells; cells contain organelles and molecular machinery. Cells are the basic structural and functional units of life, but they are not interchangeable units. Blood cells, neurons, intestinal epithelial cells, muscle cells, and liver cells have different shapes, locations, expression programs, and physiological roles. A molecular perturbation that is harmless in one cell type may be ineffective or toxic in another.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s05-cell-types.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Human body surrounded by illustrations of brain, blood, intestinal, liver, and muscle cells." caption="A drug's molecular action is filtered through cell identity, tissue context, and whole-body physiology. Source: <a href='https://depositphotos.com/vector/diagram-of-common-cell-types-115174296.html'>interactimages / Depositphotos</a>." %}

One important route from molecular information to human phenotype is the central dogma. DNA stores instructions. Transcription copies selected DNA regions into RNA. Messenger RNA carries protein-coding instructions to ribosomes, which translate the nucleotide sequence into an amino-acid chain. The chain then folds, is often chemically modified, and is transported to the location where it functions.

This path explains why changes in DNA can affect visible characteristics such as eye color, height, or hair texture, but it is not a one-way assembly line. Transcription is regulated; RNA is spliced and degraded; proteins are modified and turned over. A drug can intervene at many of these stages, and its effect depends on which molecular species is actually present in the relevant cell.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s06-central-dogma.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Plant cell, DNA transcription, messenger RNA processing, ribosome, transfer RNA, and growing protein chain." caption="Transcription and translation connect genetic information to the protein machinery on which many therapies act. Source: <a href='https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10408398.2023.2279696'>Plant-based proteins review, Fig. 1</a>." %}

Proteins are often called the workhorses inside and outside cells. Three recurring classes are especially useful for pharmacology:

- **Receptors** detect a signal and convert binding outside or inside a cell into a response.
- **Enzymes** accelerate chemical reactions and thereby control metabolic and signaling fluxes.
- **Antibodies** recognize molecular surfaces with high specificity and can block, recruit, label, or eliminate their targets.

A membrane receptor illustrates the separation between recognition and response. An extracellular ligand binds; the receptor changes state; intracellular components relay the change. The drug need not enter the cell if it can alter the first step at the surface.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s07-cell-signaling.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Extracellular molecules binding membrane receptors and generating an intracellular signal." caption="Cell signaling separates extracellular recognition, membrane transduction, and intracellular response. An exact published occurrence is available from <a href='https://habr.com/ru/articles/507512/'>Habr</a>; the original illustrator is unclear." %}

Proteins are only one biomolecular family. Carbohydrates provide structural units, recognition motifs, and readily mobilized energy. Lipids form membranes, store energy, and act as signals. Nucleic acids encode and regulate information. Proteins provide catalysis, structure, transport, and signaling. The term *biomolecule* is used loosely for a molecule produced by a living organism and essential to one or more biological processes. That looseness matters: modern therapeutics also include synthetic molecules, engineered macromolecules, and cells that do not fit a single tidy category.

### What counts as a drug?

A practical definition is simple: a drug is a chemical substance that changes how the body works. The category includes traditional small molecules and several kinds of biologics. Protein-based therapeutics are biologics; so are live biotherapeutic agents containing organisms such as bacteria, and cell therapies in which living cells are introduced to treat or cure disease.

That definition classifies by intervention, not by size alone. A small molecule may block an intracellular enzyme. An antibody may bind a surface receptor. A bacterium may reshape the chemical environment of the gut. An engineered cell may sense a disease marker and respond. All change biological function, but their design, delivery, manufacturing, and evaluation problems are very different.

#### Small-molecule drugs

Small-molecule drugs have relatively low molecular weights, typically below about 900 daltons. They can often be synthesized through conventional chemical processes and formulated for oral administration, though intravenous and other routes are also common. Their compact size can permit diffusion across cell membranes and access to enzyme active sites or receptor pockets. These are tendencies, not guarantees: permeability, solubility, charge state, transporters, metabolism, and formulation determine what a specific compound can actually do.

Examples include aspirin for pain relief, metformin for diabetes, and statins for cholesterol control. Their structures are different, but all are compact enough to be optimized by medicinal chemistry.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s10-aspirin-model.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Ball-and-stick molecular model of aspirin." caption="Aspirin is a compact organic molecule, representative of the scale addressed by small-molecule medicinal chemistry. Source: <a href='https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aspirin-3D-balls.png'>Ben Mills / Wikimedia Commons</a>, public domain." %}

Small molecules have several practical advantages. Oral bioavailability is possible; manufacturing is generally cheaper and simpler than for large biologics; and storage and distribution can be easier. The same broad accessibility that makes them useful can create disadvantages. A compound may bind unintended targets and cause toxicity. Pathogens or tumors may also evolve resistance, as seen with many antibiotics and anticancer agents. “Small molecule” is therefore a modality, not a promise of safety, selectivity, or convenience.

#### Biologics

Biologics are large, complex products derived from or produced using living cells, commonly proteins and antibodies. They are usually administered by injection or infusion because they have poor oral bioavailability: digestive enzymes and acidic conditions can degrade them, while their size and polarity impede absorption across the intestinal epithelium. Their large interaction surfaces can make them highly specific to receptors, ligands, or other biological targets.

Monoclonal antibodies such as trastuzumab, marketed as Herceptin for certain breast cancers, are a prominent example. Insulin and vaccines illustrate other biologic classes.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s12-monoclonal-antibodies.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Monoclonal antibodies surrounding a target before and after making it harmless." caption="Multiple copies of one antibody recognize the same target surface, enabling a highly specific intervention. Source: <a href='https://wellcome.org/news/what-are-monoclonal-antibodies-can-they-treat-covid-19'>Wellcome</a>." %}

Specificity is the central advantage of many biologics. They can act on targets that are hard to address with small molecules and have transformed treatment of autoimmune disease and cancer. Their disadvantages are equally concrete: expensive manufacture, complex quality control, cold-chain and administration requirements, and the possibility of immunogenicity. An immune reaction can neutralize the therapy, alter exposure, or cause harm. The appropriate modality is therefore dictated by the target and disease context, not by a blanket preference for small or large molecules.

## Pharmacokinetics: what the body does to a drug

Pharmacokinetics, abbreviated PK, describes the time course of a drug in the body. PK informs dose, dosing frequency, and potential side effects because a drug cannot act where it never arrives, and even a useful molecule becomes unsafe if exposure is too high or too prolonged.

The standard organizing framework is **ADME**:

1. **Absorption:** entry from the administration site into the bloodstream.
2. **Distribution:** movement through blood, fluids, organs, and tissues.
3. **Metabolism:** chemical transformation, often but not exclusively in the liver.
4. **Excretion:** removal of the parent drug and its metabolites.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s14-adme-overview.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Human-body diagram labeling absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion." caption="ADME describes four coupled processes that determine exposure at the site of action. Source: <a href='https://learning.eupati.eu/mod/book/view.php?chapterid=351&amp;id=357'>EUPATI, The principles of ADME</a>." %}

### Absorption

Absorption is movement from the site of administration into the bloodstream. Intravenous administration places a drug directly into systemic circulation, whereas an oral drug must dissolve, survive the gastrointestinal environment, cross biological barriers, and often pass through the liver before reaching the rest of the body.

The route of administration, solubility, formulation, local pH, and blood flow at the absorption site all change the rate and extent of uptake. The outcome is summarized by **bioavailability**: the fraction of the administered dose that reaches systemic circulation in an active form. High nominal dose and high bioavailability are not the same thing.

### Distribution

Distribution is dispersion through tissues and body fluids. Perfusion matters: highly vascular organs receive a compound faster than poorly perfused tissues. Binding to plasma proteins such as albumin changes the free fraction available to leave the bloodstream. Biological barriers impose another filter; the blood–brain barrier, for example, excludes many otherwise active molecules.

The **volume of distribution**, $$V_d$$, compares the amount of drug in the body with the measured plasma concentration:

$$
V_d = \frac{\text{amount of drug in the body}}{\text{plasma drug concentration}}.
$$

It is an apparent volume, not necessarily a literal anatomical compartment. A large value suggests extensive partitioning into tissues or strong tissue binding; a small value suggests that much of the drug remains in plasma.

### Metabolism

Metabolism chemically transforms a drug, primarily in the liver but also in the gut, blood, kidneys, and other tissues. Phase I reactions such as oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis introduce or expose functional groups. Cytochrome P450 enzymes are major Phase I catalysts. Phase II reactions conjugate the molecule—for example by glucuronidation—to make it more water-soluble and easier to excrete.

Metabolism does not always deactivate a drug. It can destroy activity, convert a prodrug into its active form, or create another active species. It can also create toxic metabolites. Acetaminophen is a familiar warning: most is safely conjugated at ordinary doses, while a reactive minor metabolite becomes dangerous when protective pathways are overwhelmed.

### Excretion and half-life

Excretion removes drugs and metabolites, primarily through the kidneys into urine or through the liver and bile into feces. Glomerular filtration, tubular secretion, and reabsorption all influence renal clearance. Hepatic function affects biliary clearance. Lung, sweat, saliva, and breast milk can also contribute for particular substances.

The **half-life** is the time required for a concentration to fall by 50% during a specified phase of the profile. It helps set dosing intervals and the time needed to approach steady state or wash out a drug. Half-life is not a universal property independent of the patient: clearance, distribution, organ function, age, and interactions can all change it.

### The absorption–elimination curve

After a non-intravenous dose, blood concentration commonly rises while absorption exceeds elimination, reaches a maximum concentration $$C_{\max}$$ at time $$T_{\max}$$, and then falls when elimination dominates. The area under the concentration–time curve, AUC, measures total systemic exposure. The curve separates quantities that answer different questions: $$C_{\max}$$ concerns the peak, AUC concerns cumulative exposure, and half-life concerns the terminal decline.

## Pharmacodynamics: what a drug does to the body

Pharmacodynamics, abbreviated PD, connects drug concentration to biological response. Its recurring questions concern drug–receptor interactions, dose–response relationships, mechanism of action, therapeutic effects, and toxic effects. PK delivers a time-varying concentration to a site; PD converts that concentration into an effect. Confusing the two makes a model hard to interpret: poor efficacy can arise because the compound does not reach the target or because target engagement does not produce the desired response.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s21-pk-pd-relationship.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Pharmacokinetics from administration to circulation, tissue distribution, metabolism and excretion, followed by pharmacodynamics at the site of action." caption="Pharmacokinetics governs the concentration reaching a site; pharmacodynamics governs the resulting response. An exact published occurrence is available from <a href='https://www.simplypsychedu.com/community/public/posts/370579-pharmacokinetics-and-pharmacodynamics-explained'>Simply Psych</a>; the original illustrator is unclear." %}

### Drug–receptor interactions

Receptors are proteins to which drugs bind to initiate or block effects. Important target families include G-protein-coupled receptors, ion channels, enzymes, and nuclear receptors. An **agonist** activates a receptor to produce a response. An **antagonist** occupies or otherwise blocks receptor activation without producing the same response. **Affinity** describes the strength of the drug–receptor interaction.

For a reversible equilibrium,

$$
D + R \rightleftharpoons DR,
$$

the dissociation constant is

$$
K_d = \frac{[D][R]}{[DR]}.
$$

A lower $$K_d$$ corresponds to tighter equilibrium binding under this simple model. But affinity alone does not determine the clinical effect. Receptor abundance, tissue expression, kinetics, downstream coupling, competing ligands, and exposure all matter.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s22-drug-receptor-interactions.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Natural ligand, agonist drug, and antagonist drug interacting with membrane receptor sites." caption="An agonist enhances receptor signaling, whereas an antagonist blocks the receptor from producing its normal response. Source: <a href='https://books.byui.edu/bio_461_principles_o/muscle_pharmacology'>BYU–Idaho, Muscle Pharmacology</a>." %}

### Dose, potency, and efficacy

A dose–response curve relates drug dose to the magnitude of the effect. The **ED50** is the dose that produces 50% of the maximum effect in the specified setting; **Emax** is the maximum effect achievable by that drug in the assay or system. **Potency** describes how much drug is needed for a given effect, whereas **efficacy** describes how large an effect the drug can achieve.

These are not synonyms. A left-shifted curve indicates greater potency because less drug is required. A higher plateau indicates greater efficacy. A highly potent partial agonist can act at low concentration yet never reach the maximal response of a less potent full agonist.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s23-dose-response.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sigmoidal response as a function of logarithmic drug dose." caption="The sigmoidal dose–response curve exposes both the concentration scale of activity and the saturating response. Source: <a href='https://theory.labster.com/dose-response/'>Labster</a>." %}

The **therapeutic window** is the range of doses or exposures that produce useful effects without unacceptable toxicity. The **therapeutic index** summarizes safety by comparing a toxic and an effective dose:

$$
\mathrm{TI} = \frac{\mathrm{TD}_{50}}{\mathrm{ED}_{50}}.
$$

A wide index indicates greater separation between desired and toxic effects. A narrow index means that modest changes in dose, clearance, adherence, or drug interactions can move exposure into a dangerous range. It is a coarse population summary, not a replacement for patient-specific monitoring.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s24-therapeutic-index.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Therapeutic and toxic response curves labeled ED50, TD50, and therapeutic index." caption="The therapeutic index compares the median toxic dose with the median effective dose. Source: <a href='https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8512302/'>Adepu and Ramakrishna (2021), Fig. 17</a>, CC BY 4.0." %}

### Mechanism of action and signaling

The **mechanism of action** explains how a drug produces an effect at the molecular level. Beta blockers provide a concrete example. By blocking beta-adrenergic receptors, they reduce catecholamine signaling, slow heart rate, reduce cardiac workload, and can lower blood pressure. This connects a binding event to organ-level physiology.

Specificity describes how selectively a drug interacts with its intended target. Higher specificity often reduces off-target effects, although a target-specific perturbation can still cause mechanism-based toxicity if the same target is important in healthy tissue. Therapeutic effects are desired clinical outcomes, such as pain relief or lower blood pressure. Adverse effects are unintended harmful outcomes, such as nausea or liver toxicity.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s25-beta-blocker-moa.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Beta blockers occupying adrenergic receptors, slowing the heart, and dilating arteries." caption="Beta-receptor blockade links molecular competition at a receptor to slower cardiac pacing and lower vascular resistance. Source: <a href='https://www.medillsb.com/illustration_image_details.aspx?AID=335&amp;IID=209517'>Fran Milner, How Beta Blockers Work</a>." %}

Drugs often act through signal-transduction pathways. Receptor binding activates intracellular relays; second messengers such as cyclic AMP and calcium ions can amplify the signal. Downstream consequences include changes in gene expression, as for steroid signaling; enzyme activation or inhibition; and cellular responses such as contraction or secretion.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s26-signal-transduction.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Extracellular signal binding a receptor, activating second messengers and effector proteins, and altering gene expression." caption="A receptor can amplify one extracellular binding event through second messengers before changing effector activity or gene expression. Source: <a href='https://theory.labster.com/es/signal-transduction/'>Labster, Signal Transduction</a>." %}

Repeated exposure can change the response itself. **Tolerance** is a reduced response after prolonged use, sometimes requiring a higher dose for the same effect. **Sensitization** is an increased response after repeated use. Receptor internalization, altered expression, pathway adaptation, and learning can all contribute, so a static dose–response curve need not predict long-term behavior.

### Drug combinations

Multiple drugs can interact pharmacodynamically:

- **Synergism:** the combined effect is greater than the sum of individual effects. Alcohol and sedatives can dangerously reinforce central nervous system depression.
- **Antagonism:** one drug reduces or blocks another. Naloxone antagonizes opioid receptors and can reverse opioid effects.
- **Additivity:** the combined effect is approximately the sum of the individual effects. Two antihypertensive drugs may lower blood pressure through complementary mechanisms.

The binding mechanism also matters. A competitive inhibitor contests the main site; an allosteric activator or inhibitor binds elsewhere and changes receptor behavior. These mechanisms reshape the dose–response curve in different ways.

## Part 2: the drug-discovery process

The pharmacology above describes what a successful drug must eventually do. Discovery works backward from that requirement through a sequence of increasingly demanding filters:

1. target identification and validation;
2. hit identification;
3. hit-to-lead optimization;
4. lead identification;
5. lead optimization;
6. preclinical and clinical testing.

Names and boundaries vary among organizations, but the logic is stable. Early stages explore many possibilities with weak evidence. Later stages examine fewer candidates with stronger and more expensive evidence.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s30-discovery-process.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Timeline from hit finding and validation through hit to lead, lead optimization, and preclinical regulatory work." caption="A discovery program converts a hit into a lead candidate before committing to preclinical regulatory studies. Source: <a href='https://www.oncodesign-services.com/explore/target-identification-validation/'>Oncodesign Services</a>." %}

### Target identification

Target identification asks which molecular entity is involved in the disease and could be modulated to change its course. Targets may be proteins such as enzymes or receptors, or genes and RNA species. Evidence comes from genomics and proteomics, genome-wide association studies, analysis of disease pathways, and literature mining.

There are two broad strategies. Target-based discovery begins with a molecular target, constructs an assay, screens molecules, and tests whether the resulting perturbation changes disease biology. Phenotype-based discovery begins with a cellular or organismal disease model, screens for compounds that improve the phenotype, and only then identifies the responsible target and mechanism. The former starts with mechanism; the latter can discover unexpected mechanisms but leaves target deconvolution for later.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s31-target-identification.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Target-based and phenotype-based routes from validation or disease model through screening to candidate compounds and mechanism studies." caption="Target-based and phenotype-based discovery reverse the order of screening and mechanism identification. Source: <a href='https://www.nature.com/articles/nchembio.1199'>Schenone et al. (2013)</a>." %}

### Druggability

Identification does not establish **druggability**: the likelihood that a target can be modulated by a therapeutic modality. A deep, chemically complementary protein pocket may bind a small molecule. Intrinsically disordered proteins, broad protein–protein interfaces, and transcription factors lacking conventional pockets are often harder to address with traditional molecules.

“Undruggable” should therefore be read as modality- and technology-dependent, not permanent. Antibodies can recognize large extracellular surfaces. Degraders can recruit cellular machinery rather than inhibit an active site. Gene-based therapies, including CRISPR-derived approaches, can intervene upstream. The key question is whether a controllable intervention exists with adequate selectivity, delivery, and safety.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s32-druggability-pocket.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Molecular surface pocket being evaluated for druggability by candidate drug-like compounds." caption="A druggable pocket must combine suitable geometry and chemistry for ligand binding. Source: <a href='https://pockdrug.rpbs.univ-paris-diderot.fr/cgi-bin/index.py?page=Home'>PockDrug server</a>." %}

### Target validation

Target validation asks whether changing the target actually changes disease. Gene knockout or knockdown with CRISPR or siRNA can reduce function; overexpression can increase it. Researchers test whether those perturbations alter the disease state in cellular and animal models. Biomarkers help connect target modulation to a measurable response.

The argument must be causal. A protein can correlate with disease because it is a consequence rather than a driver. A target can also improve one marker while worsening another pathway. Validation therefore combines genetics, perturbation, model systems, and biomarkers rather than treating any single association as proof.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s33-target-validation.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Blood drop linked to iron, cholesterol, blood sugar, and inflammation measurements." caption="Biomarkers turn biological state into measurements that can support target validation and response monitoring. Original source not identified." %}

### Hit identification

Hit identification finds the first molecules with evidence of binding or biological activity. High-throughput screening can test hundreds of thousands or millions of compounds. Fragment-based drug discovery screens much smaller chemical pieces whose weak interactions can later be elaborated. Natural-product screening searches plants, microorganisms, and other biological sources for active chemistry.

A hit needs sufficient affinity for the target and evidence of activity, but it is not yet a drug. Assay artifacts, aggregation, nonspecific reactivity, and interference must be excluded. Orthogonal measurements and counterscreens are essential because the cost of following a false hit compounds downstream.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s34-hit-identification.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Compound library being screened against a protein target to identify a bound hit." caption="Hit identification searches a compound library for molecules that bind or modulate a target. Source: <a href='https://www.aiffinity.com/platform/hit-identification'>AI|ffinity</a>." %}

### Hit to lead

Hit-to-lead work converts initial activity into a compound series with improved drug-like properties. Structure–activity relationship, or SAR, studies modify chemical structure and measure how each change affects activity. Early ADME screens reveal solubility, permeability, metabolic stability, and other liabilities before a program invests deeply in potency.

The main metrics at this stage are potency, selectivity for the intended target, and an early safety profile. Optimization is multi-objective. A substitution that improves affinity may reduce solubility; a lipophilic group that helps permeability may increase nonspecific binding or metabolic clearance. A lead is a balanced candidate, not simply the molecule with the smallest assay $$IC_{50}$$.

### Lead identification and optimization

Lead optimization refines efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, and formulation. Potency should increase in a way that lowers the required dose; the original wording “increase target affinity and required dose” would reverse that relationship, so the intended objective is **greater affinity with a lower required dose**. Selectivity should minimize interactions with unintended targets. Bioavailability, half-life, and distribution must support the dosing plan. Toxicity and adverse effects must fall within an acceptable margin, and the compound must be deliverable in a stable formulation.

Methods include repeated SAR cycles, mechanistic assays, PK studies, toxicology, and preclinical *in vivo* studies. The process contains many failure modes: toxicity, insolubility, low specificity, oxidation, weak efficacy, or false positives in *in vitro* assays.

### Clinical trials

Clinical trials test candidates in humans. Phase 0 or exploratory studies may collect very early human PK or target-engagement evidence at limited exposure. Phase I focuses on safety, tolerability, dose, and PK, often in tens of participants. Phase II evaluates safety and preliminary efficacy in a larger patient group. Phase III confirms benefit and characterizes risks across hundreds or thousands of participants. Phase IV follows approval and monitors long-term and rare effects in real use.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s37-clinical-trial-phases.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Clinical-trial phases from preclinical work through safety, dosing, efficacy, and post-approval surveillance." caption="Clinical development broadens from early safety and dose finding to efficacy confirmation and post-approval surveillance. An exact published occurrence is available from <a href='https://library.algoreducation.com/it/docs/24718/introduzione-concetti-ricerca-scientifica'>Algor Library</a>; the original illustrator is unclear." %}

The whole process is a funnel. A representative count falls from more than 20,000 early possibilities to 5,000–20,000 discovery compounds, roughly 250 preclinical candidates, about five clinical candidates, and one approved drug. The exact numbers and costs vary, but the direction is reliable: candidate count collapses while the evidence and resources devoted to each remaining candidate increase. Feedback from *in silico*, *in vitro*, *in vivo*, and clinical work should update earlier decisions rather than flowing only forward.

Original source not identified." %}

### Personalized medicine

Population averages can hide clinically meaningful subgroups. Personalized medicine combines diagnostics with treatment selection. Genetic variants, biomarkers, lifestyle, and clinical measurements can identify patients who are more likely to respond or suffer adverse effects. A **companion diagnostic** is paired with a therapy to determine whether a patient has the relevant molecular feature.

The goal is not a unique drug for every individual. More often, it is a better-defined stratum: select the right therapy for a subgroup, choose dose using patient-specific PK, and monitor whether the expected molecular and clinical responses occur.

Source: <a href='https://www.genosalut.com/en/news/healthy-lifestyle/what-is-personalised-medicine/'>Genosalut</a>." %}

## Part 3: experimental techniques for drug discovery

Discovery depends on measurement. Modern experiments do more than ask whether one protein changes. They profile system-wide molecular states, resolve regulatory mechanisms, and determine three-dimensional structures that explain recognition.

### The omics revolution

Genomics studies DNA sequence and variation. Transcriptomics measures RNA abundance. Proteomics measures proteins, their abundance, and their modifications. Metabolomics measures small-molecule intermediates and products. Related layers include ionomics, hormonally focused measurements, and phenomics. Together they connect genotype, molecular state, and phenotype.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s41-omics.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Genes, messenger RNAs, proteins, metabolites, and phenotypes connected to genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and phenomics." caption="Multi-omics measurements connect genetic variation to RNA, protein, metabolite, and phenotypic state. Source: <a href='https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4780796/'>Shiratake and Suzuki (2016), Fig. 1</a>, CC BY 4.0." %}

The benefit is breadth, but breadth introduces statistical and causal difficulty. Thousands of changing measurements contain correlated consequences as well as drivers. Experimental design, replication, batch correction, perturbation, and independent validation determine whether an apparent molecular signature becomes a credible target or biomarker.

### RNA sequencing

RNA-seq begins by isolating RNA, converting it to complementary DNA, sequencing fragments, and mapping reads to a reference genome or transcriptome. The resulting counts provide quantitative gene-expression measurements. Comparing healthy and diseased states can identify differentially expressed genes, suggest pathways, reveal candidate targets, and yield biomarkers.

The pipeline is not a direct census of proteins. RNA extraction, library preparation, depth, mapping ambiguity, transcript isoforms, and normalization affect the result, while post-transcriptional regulation separates RNA abundance from protein activity.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s42-rna-seq.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="RNA sequencing workflow from isolation and fragmentation through cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, sequencing, and read mapping." caption="RNA-seq converts cellular RNA into mapped sequence reads from which transcript abundance can be estimated. Original source not identified." %}

### ATAC-seq

ATAC-seq maps the regulatory landscape of the genome by measuring chromatin accessibility. A transposase preferentially enters open chromatin, cuts accessible DNA, and inserts sequencing adapters in a “cut-and-tag” operation. Sequencing the tagged fragments reveals regions likely to contain active promoters and enhancers that control gene expression in a disease state.

Accessibility is not identical to regulatory activity. It narrows the search to physically open regions, which should be combined with motif analysis, expression, perturbation, and cell-type context.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s43-atac-seq.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="ATAC sequencing overview and workflow from open chromatin tagging through amplification, sequencing, and accessibility peaks." caption="ATAC-seq tags accessible chromatin and converts the resulting fragments into a genome-wide accessibility profile. Workflow template: <a href='https://www.biorender.com/template/atac-sequencing'>BioRender</a>; the original source of the left-hand overview is not identified." %}

### Protein three-dimensional structures

Protein structures are central to understanding drug–target interactions. A structure can reveal an active-site pocket, an allosteric site, an interface, solvent exposure, and the conformational changes coupled to binding. The Protein Data Bank collects experimentally determined structures and makes them reusable as scientific data. Its growth also charts the changing experimental landscape: X-ray crystallography dominates historically, while cryo-electron microscopy has expanded rapidly.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s44-protein-structure-data.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Stacked plot of structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank from 1990 to 2020 by X-ray crystallography, NMR, and cryo-EM." caption="The Protein Data Bank grew through crystallography, NMR, and increasingly cryo-EM, turning structural experiments into a shared corpus. Source: <a href='https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8669736/'>Derry, Carpenter, and Altman (2022), Fig. 1</a>, CC BY-NC 4.0." %}

X-ray crystallography is the classic method for atomic structure determination. A purified protein or material is crystallized; X-rays scatter from its electron density; the diffraction pattern is converted into an electron-density map after solving the phase problem; an atomic model is fitted and refined against the measurements. Crystallization and conformational heterogeneity can be limiting, and the resulting model is an inference constrained by data rather than a literal photograph.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s45-xray-crystallography.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="X-rays pass through a crystal to produce a diffraction pattern, phases, an electron-density map, and a refined atomic model." caption="Crystallography infers an atomic model from diffraction intensities through phasing, density reconstruction, fitting, and refinement. Cryogenic electron microscopy flash-freezes protein particles in a thin layer of vitreous ice. An electron microscope records many noisy two-dimensional projections at different orientations, which are aligned and combined into a three-dimensional density. Cryo-EM avoids the need for a crystal and has become especially powerful for large complexes and multiple conformational states.

## Part 4: materials and their properties

### What is materials science?

Materials science is the interdisciplinary study of material structure, properties, processing, and performance. Its objects include metals, ceramics, polymers, semiconductors, composites, nanostructures, and biomaterials. The central challenge is to design matter with targeted behavior—often for energy or electronics—and then scale it from a laboratory preparation to an industrial process.

Even a familiar semiconductor shows why structure matters. Replacing a silicon atom with boron changes the carrier population because boron contributes one fewer valence electron. Composition, atomic arrangement, and defects jointly determine the macroscopic response.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s48-materials-science.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Boron atom in a silicon lattice accepting neighboring electrons and leaving a moving hole." caption="Substitutional boron creates an acceptor state and a mobile hole in silicon, linking atomic composition to electronic behavior. Source: <a href='https://www.shindengen.com/products/semi/column/basic/semi/semi_basic.html'>Shindengen, Semiconductor Basics</a>." %}

### Why discovery is hard

The design space is vast. Millions of plausible compounds and structures can be proposed, while only a fraction have been synthesized. Traditional trial-and-error synthesis is slow and costly. First-principles simulations such as density functional theory can predict useful properties and crystal energetics, but evaluating every candidate at high accuracy remains expensive.

The inverse-design problem reverses the usual calculation. Forward prediction asks, “Given this material, what properties does it have?” Inverse design asks, “Given the properties I want, which composition and structure should I make?” Databases and machine learning add a fourth, data-driven paradigm to empirical observation, analytical theory, and computation. They do not remove synthesis and measurement; they choose where those resources should be spent.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s49-materials-discovery-loop.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Traditional experimental, theoretical, and computational approaches compared with a database-driven approach to new materials." caption="Data-driven discovery augments experiment, theory, and simulation by learning from accumulated materials data. Source: <a href='https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/ma/d2ma00067a'>Singh et al. (2022), Fig. 9</a>, CC BY 3.0." %}

### Categorization by composition and bonding

One useful categorization separates metals, ceramics, polymers, and composites. Metals such as iron and copper are generally good electrical conductors, ductile, and often crystalline. Ceramics such as alumina and silicon carbide are inorganic compounds that can tolerate high temperature but are often brittle. Polymers such as polyethylene and PTFE are long-chain organic molecules that are lightweight and flexible. Composites combine constituents to obtain a property combination that no component supplies alone.

### Categorization by electronic properties

Electronic behavior separates conductors, semiconductors, and insulators. Conductors such as metals have accessible electronic states and free carriers, with no insulating band gap at the Fermi level. Insulators such as many ceramics and polymers have no free carriers under ordinary conditions and a large band gap. Semiconductors lie between: their conductivity can be tuned by temperature, light, composition, and doping.

The **band gap** is the energy difference between the top of the valence band—the highest occupied states associated with bound electrons—and the bottom of the conduction band—the lowest states in which carriers can move through the solid. For finite molecules, the HOMO–LUMO gap is a related conceptual quantity, though a periodic solid's bands and a molecule's discrete orbitals are not identical descriptions.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s51-band-gap-comparison.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Band diagrams for a metal with overlapping bands, a semiconductor with a moderate gap, and an insulator with a large gap." caption="Metals have accessible states at the Fermi level, whereas semiconductors and insulators separate valence and conduction bands by increasingly large gaps. Source: <a href='https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Band_gap_comparison.svg'>Inductiveload / Wikimedia Commons</a>, CC BY-SA 2.5." %}

### Categorization by optical properties

Transparency occurs when a material does not absorb visible light strongly. A sufficiently wide band gap can prevent visible photons from driving electronic transitions, as in glass or quartz, although scattering, defects, and vibrational absorption also affect real optical behavior.

Luminescence is emission after a material absorbs energy. Electron–hole recombination can produce photoluminescence; molecular fluorescence and phosphorescence involve different excited-state kinetics; defects and dopants can introduce additional emitting states. The original shorthand grouped defect emission with fluorescence, but these mechanisms should not be treated as synonyms.

An organic light-emitting diode illustrates the coupling among optical gaps, charge injection, transport, and recombination. Electrons and holes cross different layers, meet in an emissive layer, and release energy as light.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s52-oled-properties.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Layered OLED device and energy-level diagram with hole transport, emissive, and electron transport layers." caption="OLED emission depends on injecting electrons and holes through different layers and recombining them in the emissive layer. Source: <a href='https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2021/ma/d0ma00903b'>Schlisske et al. (2021), Fig. 2</a>." %}

### Categorization by thermal properties

Thermal conductors move heat through mobile electrons, lattice vibrations, or both. Metals benefit from their electronic carriers; electrically insulating diamond conducts heat efficiently through phonons. Strong atomic bonds and structures that support long mean free paths can enhance thermal conductivity.

Thermal insulators impede electron or phonon transport. Disorder, porosity, interfaces, and low density increase scattering or interrupt connected pathways. Electrons are charged particles; phonons are quantized collective vibrations of the lattice. Both can carry energy, but only electrons carry electrical charge.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s53-thermal-transport.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Vibrational waves propagating through an ordered lattice and scattering in a disordered lattice." caption="Order permits more coherent vibrational transport, whereas structural disorder increases phonon scattering. Original source not identified." %}

### Categorization by magnetic properties

Magnetic classes distinguish local electronic moments and their collective order. Diamagnetic materials develop a weak response opposing an external field. Paramagnetic materials contain unpaired electrons whose moments align weakly with the field. Ferromagnets exhibit spontaneous parallel ordering in domains. Antiferromagnets have opposing moments that largely cancel, while ferrimagnets have opposing sublattices of unequal magnitude and retain a net magnetization. MnO and $$\mathrm{Fe}_3\mathrm{O}_4$$ are familiar antiferromagnetic and ferrimagnetic examples, respectively.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s54-magnetic-classes.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Diamagnetic and paramagnetic electron pairing, spin alignment, force response, and magnetic-field distortion." caption="Diamagnetic and paramagnetic behavior differ in electron pairing and in the direction of the field-induced response. Source: <a href='https://www.chemdunn.com/paramagnestism-diamagnetism'>ChemDunn</a>." %}

### Categorization by size

A nanomaterial has at least one characteristic dimension between roughly 1 and 100 nm; a bulk particle exceeds 100 nm in all dimensions under this operational definition. Dimensionality matters as much as the label. Nanosheets confine one dimension, nanotubes two, and nanoparticles three. Reducing size changes the surface-to-volume ratio, confinement, defect population, and accessible structures, which can change properties even at fixed composition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s55-size-scales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Microscopy and atomic models of boron nitride nanosheet, nanotube, and nanoparticle structures." caption="The same elements can form sheet, tube, and particle morphologies with different dimensionality and surface structure. Source: <a href='https://doi.org/10.1007/s12274-017-1635-y'>Mateti et al. (2017), Fig. 5</a>." %}

### Mechanical properties

Mechanical descriptors answer different questions. **Hardness** is resistance to localized deformation or scratching. **Elasticity** is the ability to return to the original shape after stress is removed. **Plasticity** is permanent deformation under stress, common in metals because dislocations can move. **Toughness** is resistance to fracture and reflects a balance between strength and ductility. A ceramic can be hard and strong yet brittle, so hardness alone does not imply toughness.

### Stability

The formation energy measures the energy change for forming a compound from constituent elements in their standard reference states. A negative formation energy makes the compound favorable relative to those elements, but stability must also be tested against *all competing compounds*. The energy above the convex hull measures the gap between a candidate and the lowest-energy combination of phases at the same overall composition:

$$
E_{\mathrm{hull}} = E_{\mathrm{candidate}} - E_{\mathrm{best\ competing\ mixture}}.
$$

A point on the hull is thermodynamically stable within the chosen approximation and conditions. A positive value indicates metastability, not automatic impossibility. Kinetic barriers can preserve a metastable phase long enough to synthesize and use it.

Source: <a href='https://utc.pressbooks.pub/mineralogy/chapter/crystals-and-crystallization/'>Perkins et al., Mineralogy, Fig. 4.7</a>, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0." %}

## Part 5: synthesis, measurement, and applications

Prediction proposes a material; synthesis creates it; measurement determines what was created. These stages cannot be collapsed into one score because a low calculated energy does not specify a route, and a nominal recipe does not guarantee phase purity.

### Solid-phase synthesis

Solid-phase synthesis is often summarized as “shake and bake”: mix powdered precursors and heat them so diffusion and reaction form a new crystalline material. Grinding increases contact and homogenizes the mixture; calcination, sintering, and annealing supply thermal energy. Temperature, time, particle size, atmosphere, and cooling rate affect which phase forms and how defects and grains develop.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s59-solid-phase-synthesis.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Metal precursor and iron oxide ground together, calcined, and converted into metal-coated magnetite core-shell nanostructures." caption="A solid-state route combines physical grinding with calcination to transform mixed precursors into new crystalline nanostructures. Source: <a href='https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-43116-7'>Roy et al. (2019), Fig. 1</a>, CC BY 4.0." %}

### Liquid-phase synthesis

Liquid-phase synthesis carries out reactions in a solvent. Dissolved or suspended precursors can react through precipitation, sol–gel chemistry, hydrothermal treatment, or other solution routes. Solvents, concentration, ligands, pH, mixing, and temperature control nucleation and growth. Separation, drying, and heat treatment then convert the product into the desired solid.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s60-liquid-phase-synthesis.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Lithium sulfide and phosphorus pentasulfide reacting in organic solvent to form solution or suspension, then drying and heat treatment to make sulfide solid electrolytes." caption="Liquid-phase routes use solvent processing before drying and heat treatment to produce sulfide solid electrolytes. Source: <a href='https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/9/7/355'>Toward Scalable Liquid-Phase Synthesis of Sulfide Solid Electrolytes (2023), Fig. 1</a>, CC BY 4.0." %}

### Gas-phase synthesis

Gas-phase synthesis vaporizes starting materials and lets them react or condense into a solid, often a fine powder or thin film. In chemical vapor deposition, gaseous precursors reach a heated region or substrate, nucleate, and grow. Flow, temperature, pressure, precursor chemistry, and surface condition regulate morphology and composition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s61-gas-phase-synthesis.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Vapor and other reactants entering a heated chamber where nucleation and nanoparticle growth occur." caption="A gas-phase reactor controls nucleation and growth by combining vapor precursors, reactants, flow, and heat. Source: <a href='https://mrcet.com/downloads/digital_notes/HS/R20/Advanced%20material%20chemistry%20digital%20notes%281%29.pdf'>MRCET Advanced Material Chemistry notes</a>." %}

### X-ray diffraction

X-ray diffraction is a primary tool for determining crystal structure. A periodic lattice scatters incident X-rays into a pattern. Peak positions constrain lattice spacings; intensities constrain the distribution of electron density. In powder diffraction, phase identification often compares a measured pattern with reference patterns. In single-crystal analysis, diffraction intensities and phases are used to reconstruct density and refine an atomic model.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s62-xray-diffraction.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="X-rays incident on a crystal producing a diffraction pattern, Fourier-transformed electron density, and an atomic model." caption="Diffraction measurements are transformed into electron density and then an atomic structural model. ### Imaging

Imaging shows spatial structure that bulk averages conceal. Scanning electron microscopy, SEM, maps surface topography by scanning an electron beam and measuring emitted signals. Transmission electron microscopy, TEM, sends electrons through a thin sample and can resolve internal structure, lattice order, interfaces, and defects.

The two methods answer different questions. An SEM image may show particle morphology and grain surfaces; TEM can reveal atomic or nanoscale internal organization. Neither alone establishes composition or bulk phase, so microscopy is combined with diffraction, spectroscopy, and other measurements.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s63-electron-microscopy.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Electron micrographs of molybdenum boride films beside top and side atomic structure views." caption="Microscopy links observed film morphology to a proposed atomistic structure of ultrathin molybdenum boride. Source: <a href='https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2017/ta/c7ta08597d'>Ultrathin molybdenum boride films (2017), Fig. 1</a>." %}

### Batteries

A lithium-ion battery stores and releases energy by separating ion and electron paths. During discharge, lithium ions move from the anode through the electrolyte and separator toward the cathode. Electrons cannot cross the electronically insulating separator, so they travel through the external circuit and power a device. The cathode commonly contains a lithium transition-metal oxide; the anode often contains graphite with additives that improve electronic conduction. Current collectors, binders, electrolyte, and interfaces all affect performance.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s65-battery-cell.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Battery cell with cathode and anode active materials, current collectors, separator, conductive additives, lithium ions, and electrons." caption="A battery couples lithium-ion transport through the cell with electron transport through the external circuit. Source: <a href='https://www.iwatani.co.jp/eng/sustainability/environment/climate/'>Iwatani Corporation</a>." %}

Solid-state electrolyte discovery asks for crystal compounds that conduct lithium ions rapidly while remaining stable and synthesizable. Those objectives compete: an open framework may improve transport but destabilize the phase; a stable dense lattice may trap ions. Candidate generation therefore needs energetic, dynamic, transport, and synthetic validation.

Chemeleon proposed four metastable and dynamically stable candidates in the Li–P–S–Cl chemical system: $$\mathrm{Li}_5\mathrm{P}\mathrm{S}_4\mathrm{Cl}$$, $$\mathrm{Li}_3\mathrm{P}\mathrm{S}_3\mathrm{Cl}$$, $$\mathrm{Li}_6\mathrm{P}\mathrm{S}_4\mathrm{Cl}$$, and $$\mathrm{Li}_4\mathrm{P}\mathrm{S}_4\mathrm{Cl}$$. These structures are hypotheses for testing, not evidence by themselves that a practical electrolyte has been made.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s66-chemeleon-candidates.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Four generated lithium phosphorus sulfur chlorine crystal structures labeled Li5PS4Cl, Li3PS3Cl, Li6PS4Cl, and Li4PS4Cl." caption="Four generated Li–P–S–Cl structures satisfy computational metastability and dynamical-stability filters. ### Semiconductors

A semiconductor has conductivity between that of a conductor and an insulator, and its carrier concentration can be engineered. **n-type** material is doped with donor atoms such as phosphorus that contribute extra electrons. **p-type** material is doped with acceptors such as boron that create holes. Bringing the two together creates a depletion region and the rectifying junction central to diodes, transistors, solar cells, and many other devices.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s67-semiconductor-doping.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="N-type region with extra electrons and p-type region with holes separated by a depletion zone." caption="At a p–n junction, electrons and holes redistribute and leave a carrier-depleted region with an internal field. Published occurrence: <a href='https://www.electronicsandyou.com/blog/how-semiconductor-works.html'>Electronics and You</a>." %}

### Heterogeneous catalysis

Heterogeneous catalysis uses a catalyst in a different phase from the reactants, most often a solid surface in contact with gas or liquid. Reactants adsorb, bonds are weakened or reorganized at specific surface environments, products form, and products desorb. The catalyst is recovered and reused more easily than a molecular catalyst dissolved in the same phase, which helps explain its industrial importance in catalytic converters, ammonia synthesis, and fuel refining.

Surface identity is the heart of the problem. Facets, defects, oxidation state, adsorbate coverage, and operating conditions determine which active sites exist. A bulk composition alone does not specify catalytic behavior.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s68-heterogeneous-catalysis.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Ethene and hydrogen undergoing physisorption, chemisorption, surface bond formation, and product desorption." caption="A heterogeneous catalytic cycle moves from adsorption through surface reaction to desorption of the product. Source: <a href='https://www.cademix.org/applications-of-heterogeneous-catalysis-in-industry/'>Cademix</a>." %}

### Biocatalysts

Biocatalysis uses living systems or their components to accelerate chemical reactions. In molecular design, the main object is often an enzyme. Enzymes combine a structured binding pocket with catalytic residues and conformational dynamics, which can produce remarkable selectivity under mild conditions. Engineering seeks to change substrate scope, activity, stability, solvent tolerance, or product preference without destroying the fold and catalytic mechanism.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s69-biocatalysis.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Enzyme catalyst converting a molecular substrate into a product." caption="An enzyme binds a substrate in a structured active site and lowers the barrier to a selective product. Source: <a href='https://www.biosynth.com/chemicals/biocatalysis'>Biosynth</a>." %}

### Direct air capture

Direct air capture removes carbon dioxide directly from the atmosphere using large fans and chemical or physical separation. Air passes over sorbents or through solvent systems that bind dilute $$\mathrm{CO}_2$$. The capture medium is then heated, depressurized, humidified, or otherwise treated to release concentrated $$\mathrm{CO}_2$$ for storage or use, while $$\mathrm{CO}_2$$-depleted air returns to the atmosphere.

The material must do more than bind carbon dioxide strongly. It must distinguish $$\mathrm{CO}_2$$ from nitrogen, oxygen, and water; regenerate with acceptable energy; survive many cycles; and function at atmospheric concentrations. Process design and material design therefore cannot be separated.

## One discovery logic, two kinds of candidate

Drug and material discovery differ in their objects, measurements, and failure modes, but their evidence chains have the same shape. Begin with a desired intervention or property. Identify the molecular or structural variables that might produce it. Search a large design space. Test predictions with increasingly realistic experiments. Reject candidates that fail on activity, selectivity, stability, safety, synthesis, or scale. Feed the result back into the next design cycle.

This is where machine learning can help—and where it can be misunderstood. A binding predictor does not replace PK, a generated crystal does not establish synthesis, and a low calculated formation energy does not guarantee a useful device. A model is valuable when its output is attached to the correct stage of the discovery process and judged by the evidence required at that stage.
