## Drugs act on biological systems

Human physiology is organized across scales: organs contain tissues, tissues contain cells, and cells coordinate through molecular interactions. A drug changes physiology by perturbing some part of this hierarchy. Its immediate molecular interaction may be specific, but the resulting phenotype depends on signaling networks, transport, metabolism, tissue context, and time.

The central dogma summarizes one major information flow: DNA is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein. Regulation occurs at every stage. Genomic variants, chromatin accessibility, RNA abundance, protein modification, localization, and degradation all change the molecules available to a drug.

Receptors detect extracellular signals and convert them into intracellular responses. Enzymes catalyze chemical transformations. Antibodies bind molecular surfaces with high specificity and recruit or block biological processes. These three protein classes recur as targets and therapeutic modalities because their functions can be changed by binding.

Cells contain many other biomolecules: lipids form membranes, carbohydrates store energy and mediate recognition, nucleic acids encode and regulate information, and metabolites connect biochemical pathways. A therapeutic can target any of these directly or alter the proteins that create, transport, or consume them.

### Small molecules and biologics

A drug is a substance used to diagnose, prevent, or treat disease by changing biological function. Small-molecule drugs are usually low-molecular-weight organic compounds. Their size can permit oral dosing, membrane permeation, and access to pockets inside proteins, although exposure and selectivity remain compound-specific.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s10-image11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Chemical structures of several small-molecule medicines." caption="Small-molecule drugs span diverse scaffolds while remaining compact enough for conventional medicinal chemistry. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 10." %}

Biologics include antibodies, engineered proteins, peptides, nucleic-acid therapies, vaccines, and cell-based products. They can recognize large surfaces that are difficult for a small molecule to address. Their manufacturing, delivery, stability, and tissue penetration differ from those of small molecules.

## Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics

Pharmacokinetics asks what the body does to a drug. The usual ADME decomposition is absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. A molecule must enter the body, reach the relevant tissue in an active form, remain there long enough, and eventually be cleared without unacceptable accumulation or toxic metabolites.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s14-image19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Human-body diagram showing absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion." caption="ADME processes jointly determine the concentration that reaches a target tissue. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 14." %}

Absorption depends on administration route, dissolution, membrane permeability, transporters, and first-pass metabolism. Distribution depends on blood flow, plasma-protein binding, tissue barriers, and partitioning into different chemical environments. Metabolism, often concentrated in the liver, can deactivate a compound, activate a prodrug, or create reactive products. Excretion removes drug and metabolites through renal, biliary, pulmonary, and other routes.

After a dose, concentration commonly rises during absorption and falls as elimination dominates. The area under the concentration–time curve measures total systemic exposure. The peak concentration, time of the peak, and terminal half-life describe different aspects of the profile; none alone guarantees exposure at the site of action.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s20-image23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Drug concentration curve with absorption, peak exposure, elimination, and area under the curve." caption="A concentration–time profile separates absorption, peak exposure, total exposure, and elimination. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 20." %}

Pharmacodynamics asks what the drug does to the body. Binding to a receptor, enzyme, channel, or other target changes a molecular process; the downstream response can be amplified, buffered, delayed, or opposed by feedback.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s21-image24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Pharmacodynamic diagram linking drug concentration, target interaction, and physiological response." caption="Pharmacodynamics connects target engagement to a measurable biological effect. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 21." %}

For the reversible binding equilibrium $$D+R\rightleftharpoons DR$$, the dissociation constant is

$$
K_d=\frac{[D][R]}{[DR]}.
$$

Lower $$K_d$$ indicates tighter equilibrium binding. Agonists activate receptor signaling, antagonists block activation, inhibitors reduce enzymatic or transport activity, and allosteric modulators act away from the primary binding site. Binding affinity alone does not determine efficacy because residence time, receptor abundance, coupling, and competing ligands also matter.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s22-image25.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Receptor binding diagrams for agonists, antagonists, and related ligand effects." caption="Different ligands can occupy a target yet produce activation, inhibition, or modulation. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 22." %}

A dose–response curve links administered dose or local concentration to effect. Potency describes how much drug is required for a specified response, often summarized by an $$EC_{50}$$ or $$IC_{50}$$. Efficacy describes the maximum achievable response. Two compounds can therefore differ in potency while sharing efficacy, or reach different maximal effects at similar concentrations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s23-image27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sigmoidal dose-response curve labeled with half-maximal response." caption="Dose–response curves separate the concentration scale of activity from the maximal effect. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 23." %}

The therapeutic index compares harmful and beneficial dose scales. A wide separation permits exposure variation without crossing into toxicity; a narrow window demands careful dosing and monitoring.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s24-image28.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two dose-response plots contrasting wide and narrow therapeutic windows." caption="The therapeutic window is the exposure range between adequate efficacy and unacceptable toxicity. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 24." %}

The mechanism of action identifies how molecular engagement propagates into phenotype. Receptor binding may open a channel, change an enzyme, recruit an adaptor, alter transcription, or trigger a signaling cascade. Repeated dosing can induce tolerance or sensitization through receptor regulation, pathway adaptation, or changes in metabolism. Drug combinations can be additive, synergistic, or antagonistic, and they can also interact pharmacokinetically by changing transport or metabolism.

## The drug-discovery process

Drug discovery narrows a biological hypothesis into a candidate that can be manufactured, dosed, and tested in people. The sequence is target identification, target validation, hit identification, hit-to-lead work, lead optimization, preclinical development, and clinical trials. Attrition is expected because each stage introduces evidence unavailable at the previous one.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s30-image34.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Pipeline from target identification through hit discovery, lead optimization, and clinical development." caption="The discovery process progressively adds biological, chemical, safety, and clinical evidence. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 30." %}

Target identification proposes a gene, protein, pathway, or cellular state whose perturbation may change disease. Evidence can come from human genetics, expression and perturbation data, pathology, animal models, and prior pharmacology.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s31-image36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Multiple evidence sources converging on a therapeutic target." caption="Target identification combines human, molecular, and experimental evidence into a testable intervention hypothesis. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 31." %}

Druggability asks whether the target can be modulated by a feasible therapeutic modality. A deep pocket may admit a small molecule; an exposed protein surface may favor an antibody; intracellular location, tissue accessibility, selectivity, and functional redundancy can make either strategy difficult.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s32-image38.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Protein structure with a highlighted ligand-binding pocket." caption="Structural pockets and biological accessibility are central parts of druggability. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 32." %}

Target validation tests causality. Genetic knockout or knockdown, CRISPR perturbation, antibodies, chemical probes, rescue experiments, and disease-relevant models ask whether changing the target produces the intended phenotype without intolerable consequences.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s33-image39.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Target-validation wheel showing genetic, biochemical, cellular, and animal evidence." caption="Validation requires convergent perturbations rather than correlation with disease alone. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 33." %}

Hit identification searches for starting molecules using high-throughput screening, fragment screening, virtual screening, structure-based design, phenotypic assays, or natural products. A hit must be confirmed with orthogonal assays and checked for artifacts before chemistry is expanded around it.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s34-image41.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Assay and virtual-screening routes that produce initial chemical hits." caption="Hit discovery can begin from physical screening, computation, fragments, structures, or phenotypes. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 34." %}

Hit-to-lead work establishes reproducible activity and begins balancing potency, selectivity, solubility, permeability, metabolic stability, and synthetic tractability. Lead optimization iterates structure–activity relationships while adding pharmacokinetic, toxicological, formulation, and manufacturability constraints.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s36-image43.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Medicinal-chemistry optimization cycle balancing molecular properties." caption="Lead optimization is a repeated multi-property design cycle, not potency maximization alone. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 36." %}

Clinical trials shift the unit of evidence from models to people. Phase I emphasizes safety, tolerability, and exposure. Phase II tests dose and preliminary efficacy in the target population. Phase III compares benefit and risk at larger scale. Regulatory review and post-marketing surveillance extend the evidence beyond approval.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s37-image44.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Diagram of phase I, II, and III clinical trials." caption="Clinical phases progressively expand from safety and dose to comparative evidence of benefit and risk. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 37." %}

The funnel narrows because many candidates fail at every stage. Personalized medicine can change the funnel by defining molecularly distinct patient groups, matching therapies to biomarkers, and monitoring response with genomic, imaging, or physiological measurements.

## Experimental techniques for discovery

Modern discovery is powered by measurement. Omics methods profile large molecular populations rather than one target at a time. Genomics measures sequence variation, transcriptomics measures RNA, proteomics measures proteins and modifications, metabolomics measures small-molecule states, and single-cell methods resolve heterogeneity hidden by bulk averages.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s41-image48.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Timeline and overview of genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics." caption="Omics technologies broaden discovery from isolated targets to system-wide molecular measurements. RNA sequencing converts cellular RNA into sequence reads, aligns or assembles them, and estimates transcript abundance. ATAC-seq uses a transposase to insert sequencing adapters into accessible chromatin, providing a map of regulatory regions that are physically open in a cell population.

## Materials: properties, structure, and stability

Materials science studies substances whose collective structure produces useful electronic, optical, thermal, magnetic, mechanical, or chemical behavior. Relevant systems include crystalline solids, glasses, polymers, ceramics, composites, nanostructures, and biological materials. Their behavior depends on composition, bonding, order, dimensionality, defects, interfaces, and processing history.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s48-image57.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Overview of material classes and the relationship among processing, structure, properties, and performance." caption="Materials science connects composition and processing to structure, properties, and performance. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 48." %}

The search space is large because elements can combine at many compositions and structures. A proposed material must satisfy several properties simultaneously, remain stable or sufficiently long-lived, use acceptable resources, and admit a practical synthesis route.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-03/s49-image60.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Diagram of materials-discovery challenges spanning composition, structure, properties, and synthesis." caption="Materials discovery is constrained by a combinatorial design space and coupled performance requirements. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol drug and material discovery lecture, slide 49." %}

Materials can be classified by composition and structure—metals, ceramics, polymers, semiconductors, composites—or by the response relevant to an application. Electronic classification distinguishes conductors, semiconductors, and insulators through band occupation and gaps. Optical properties include absorption, reflection, transmission, luminescence, and refractive response. Thermal behavior includes conductivity, heat capacity, expansion, and phase transitions. Magnetic behavior includes dia-, para-, ferro-, antiferro-, and ferrimagnetism. Size and dimensionality distinguish bulk systems, thin films, wires, sheets, nanoparticles, and porous structures.

## Synthesis, characterization, and applications

Solid-phase synthesis mixes and heats solid precursors so diffusion and reaction form a target phase. Mechanical milling, sintering, annealing, and controlled atmospheres change grain size, defect concentration, and phase purity. Liquid-phase routes dissolve or suspend precursors and use precipitation, sol–gel chemistry, hydrothermal growth, or solution deposition to control nucleation and morphology. Gas-phase methods such as chemical or physical vapor deposition transport volatile species to a surface and grow films or nanostructures.

Drug and material discovery differ in their objects and experiments, but their evidence chains have the same shape. A candidate begins as a mechanistic or property hypothesis. Models and screens narrow possibilities. Synthesis or manufacture creates a physical object. Measurements test identity, function, safety, and stability. The result feeds back into the next design decision. Machine learning is useful only when its prediction is attached to the correct stage and tested by the evidence required there.
