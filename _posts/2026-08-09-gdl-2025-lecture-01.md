## Geometric deep learning as constrained function approximation

Geometric deep learning asks for a systematic recipe for neural networks on structured data. The 2021 keynote *Geometric Deep Learning: The Erlangen Programme of ML* frames that recipe through geometry and symmetry.

AlphaFold3 provides an immediate example of the stakes: one geometric model predicts joint structures involving proteins, RNA, DNA, ligands, and ions.

Machine learning begins with function approximation. Given an input $$x$$ and a desired output $$y$$, we choose a parameterized family $$\mathcal F$$ and fit a function $$f\in\mathcal F$$ so that $$f(x)$$ predicts $$y$$.

The universal approximation theorem says that sufficiently large neural networks can approximate continuous functions on compact domains. Modern parameter counts are large enough that expressivity alone is rarely the useful answer to architecture design.

The problem is statistical. High-dimensional data can be fit in many incompatible ways, and the information required for generalization grows rapidly with dimension. An unconstrained network can memorize the training set while behaving arbitrarily under transformations that should preserve meaning.

## Geometry means invariants under transformations

Geometry studies measurements, properties, and relationships among points, lines, angles, surfaces, and solids. Different geometries preserve different properties.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-01/s20-image25.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean plane, hyperbolic surface, and spherical Riemannian geometry." caption="Euclidean, hyperbolic, and Riemannian spaces support different notions of distance and transformation. Reused from the 2025 GDL introduction lecture, slide 20." %}

The unifying view is invariance. Euclidean geometry preserves distances and angles under translations and rotations. Affine geometry preserves parallelism. Projective geometry preserves incidence relationships.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-01/s21-image26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean, affine, and projective invariants under different transformations." caption="A geometry is characterized by the transformations it allows and the quantities those transformations preserve. Reused from the 2025 GDL introduction lecture, slide 21." %}

For data, a symmetry is a transformation that does not change object identity. Several translated or rotated photographs can depict the same cat.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-01/s22-image27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Several transformed images of the same cat grouped as one object identity." caption="Data geometry is defined by transformations under which the object should be recognized as unchanged. Reused from the 2025 GDL introduction lecture, slide 22." %}

A geometric neural network incorporates that structure. Invariant outputs remain unchanged under a symmetry; equivariant intermediate features transform predictably. The architecture therefore identifies inputs that the task says are equivalent.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-01/s23-image28.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Different data geometries connected to specialized neural architectures." caption="Geometric neural networks couple a data domain and its symmetries to an architecture designed for those transformations. Reused from the 2025 GDL introduction lecture, slide 23." %}

The same principle applies to generative modeling. A model should not waste capacity learning many coordinate descriptions of the same object, and its generated trajectory should respect the domain's geometry.

## Molecules are geometric data

Molecular science supplies several distinct geometric domains rather than one generic collection of point clouds.

### Small molecules

Small molecules consist of atoms and bonds. A two-dimensional graph records chemical connectivity; a three-dimensional representation adds atomic coordinates. Rigid rotation and translation should not change molecular identity.

These applications use different data and outputs, but the opening recipe is shared. Identify which transformations preserve the scientific object, choose representations that expose those symmetries, and construct neural or generative models whose behavior respects them. Geometric deep learning is the effort to make that recipe systematic.
