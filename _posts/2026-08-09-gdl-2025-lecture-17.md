## From domain-specific flows to generator matching

The preceding constructions used a different object for each state space: an ordinary velocity on $$\mathbb R^d$$, a tangent velocity on a Riemannian manifold, and a jump-rate matrix on a discrete space. Each defines a continuous-time Markov process, but the formulas look unrelated when written in their domain-specific coordinates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-4.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean Riemannian and discrete flow matching constructions." caption="Flow matching has domain-specific velocity descriptions on Euclidean, manifold, and discrete state spaces. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 4." %}

Generator matching seeks one framework for arbitrary data modalities. Its object is the infinitesimal generator of a continuous-time Markov process, broad enough to describe deterministic motion, stochastic diffusion, and discrete or continuous jumps.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-5.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flows diffusions and jumps unified by generator matching across data modalities." caption="A generator provides a common training target for continuous-time Markov processes over many state spaces. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 5." %}

The intended recipe mirrors conditional flow matching. Choose a conditional probability path and its conditional generator; marginalize over the condition; then train a parameterized marginal generator through a conditional objective. The generator replaces the velocity as the common representation of dynamics.

## Continuous-time Markov processes and generators

Let $$X_t\in\mathcal S$$ for $$t\in[0,1]$$. The process is Markov if the conditional law of a future state depends on the present state but not on the earlier history:

$$
\Pr(X_{t_{n+1}}\in A\mid X_{t_0},\ldots,X_{t_n})
=\Pr(X_{t_{n+1}}\in A\mid X_{t_n}).
$$

The state space $$\mathcal S$$ may be continuous, discrete, structured, or a product of several modalities.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Markov property for a continuous-time process on a general state space." caption="The present state is sufficient for the conditional law of the future. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 7." %}

The transition kernel records finite-time movement:

$$
p_{t+h\mid t}(A\mid x)
=\Pr(X_{t+h}\in A\mid X_t=x).
$$

Under the usual regularity assumptions, the initial distribution together with the transition kernels specifies the process law.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Markov process and transition kernel correspondence." caption="Transition kernels describe how the process propagates every current state over a finite time interval. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 8." %}

The generator captures the derivative of that propagation. For a test function $$f$$,

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{\mathbb E[f(X_{t+h})\mid X_t=x]-f(x)}{h}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\mathbb E[f(X_{t+h})\mid X_t=x]
=f(x)+h[\mathcal L_t f](x)+o(h).
$$

The generator is an operator on observables, not a direct state update.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-9.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Infinitesimal generator defined through transition kernels and test functions." caption="The generator is the first-order change of conditional test-function expectations. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 9." %}

## Flow, diffusion, and jump generators

For the deterministic ODE $$dX_t=u_t(X_t)dt$$, Taylor expansion gives

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)=\nabla f(x)^\top u_t(x).
$$

The familiar velocity appears as the coefficient multiplying the first derivative of the test function.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-10.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Derivation of the deterministic flow generator from an ODE." caption="A deterministic flow generator is the directional derivative along its velocity field. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 10." %}

For a drift-free Itô diffusion $$dX_t=\sigma_t(X_t)dW_t$$, the first-order stochastic increment averages to zero while its quadratic variation survives. Itô expansion yields

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\frac12\operatorname{tr}
\left(\sigma_t(x)\sigma_t(x)^\top\nabla^2f(x)\right).
$$

Diffusion therefore enters through second derivatives.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Derivation of the diffusion generator from an SDE." caption="Brownian quadratic variation converts stochastic increments into a second-order generator. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 11." %}

A jump process is described by a kernel $$Q_t(dy,x)$$ with total intensity

$$
\lambda_t(x)=\int Q_t(dy,x).
$$

Over a small interval $$h$$ it remains at $$X_t$$ with probability $$1-h\lambda_t(X_t)$$ and otherwise jumps to a destination drawn from the normalized kernel $$Q_t(dy,X_t)/\lambda_t(X_t)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Infinitesimal stay or jump mechanism for a continuous-time jump process." caption="A jump kernel separates how often a jump occurs from where it lands. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 12." %}

Conditioning on the jump event gives

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\int\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)Q_t(dy,x).
$$

The test function need not be differentiable because the jump compares finite values at distinct states.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Derivation of a jump process generator from its kernel." caption="The jump generator averages finite observable changes against the jump kernel. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 13." %}

On a Euclidean state space, a broad class of continuous-time Markov processes combines all three terms:

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\nabla f(x)^\top u_t(x)
+\frac12\operatorname{tr}
\left(a_t(x)\nabla^2 f(x)\right)
+\int\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)Q_t(dy,x),
$$

where $$a_t=\sigma_t\sigma_t^\top$$. On a discrete state space, the process is a CTMC and only the jump structure remains.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Universal generator composed of flow diffusion and jump terms." caption="First-order flow, second-order diffusion, and nonlocal jumps form the generator's three components. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 14." %}

## Conditional generator matching

Directly parameterizing an arbitrary operator is difficult. The lecture writes the generator as a known linear operator family

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)=\mathcal K\bigl(f(x),F_t(x)\bigr),
$$

and learns the finite-dimensional parameter $$F_t(x)$$ with a neural network. For a flow, $$F_t$$ is the velocity; for a diffusion it contains diffusion coefficients; for a jump process it contains the jump kernel or rates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Linear parameterizations of flow diffusion and jump generators." caption="A shared operator form exposes the learnable coefficient associated with each generator family. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 15." %}

Generator matching repeats the conditional construction but must respect this parameterization. First define $$\mathcal L_t^z$$ generating a conditional probability path. Then obtain the generator of the mixture. Finally train the neural parameter through a conditional loss rather than evaluating the marginal operator directly.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-16.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three steps of conditional generator matching and parameterization issue." caption="The generator version of conditional flow matching adds the question of which operator coefficient the network predicts. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 16." %}

If $$\mathcal L_t^z$$ generates $$p_t(x\mid z)$$, then the mixture $$p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid z)p(z)dz$$ is generated by

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\mathbb E\left[[\mathcal L_t^Z f](x)\mid X_t=x\right].
$$

This is the generator-level counterpart of posterior-averaging conditional velocities or jump rates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-17.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Marginalization trick for conditional generators." caption="Posterior averaging of conditional generators produces the generator of the marginal path. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 17." %}

For a compatible linear parameterization, the same identity holds for the learnable coefficient:

$$
F_t(x)=\mathbb E[F_t^Z(x)\mid X_t=x].
$$

The intractable loss $$D(F_t(x),F_t^\theta(x))$$ can therefore be replaced by the conditional loss $$D(F_t^Z(x),F_t^\theta(x))$$ when the divergence $$D$$ has the required conditional-mean property. This recovers squared flow matching and suitable objectives for diffusion and jumps within one scheme.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-18.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Marginal and conditional generator matching losses for learnable parameters." caption="A tractable conditional parameter target replaces the intractable marginal generator coefficient. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 18." %}

The framework supports discrete flow models, CTMC-based tractability improvements, and multimodal protein generation. A multimodal system can sum generator components for continuous geometry and discrete sequence while sharing a single time variable and conditioning network.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Applications of generator matching to discrete flow CTMC tractability and multimodal proteins." caption="Generator matching covers discrete generation and mixed discrete-continuous molecular processes. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 19." %}

## Couplings that straighten flow paths

Return now to deterministic flow matching. Choose conditional paths, posterior-average their velocities, and train through conditional regression.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-21.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Conditional flow matching construction recap." caption="The coupling discussion starts from the same conditional-to-marginal flow-matching recipe. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 21." %}

When the condition is an endpoint pair $$z=(x_0,x_1)$$, its joint law $$q(x_0,x_1)$$ may be any coupling with the desired marginals:

$$
\int q(x_0,x_1)dx_1=p_0(x_0),
\qquad
\int q(x_0,x_1)dx_0=p_1(x_1).
$$

The endpoint distributions alone do not determine which source sample should be paired with which data sample.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-22.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Source endpoint and paired endpoint conditioning choices in flow matching." caption="Endpoint-pair conditioning exposes the coupling as a free design choice. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 22." %}

The conditional loss then averages over $$(X_0,X_1)\sim q$$. Different couplings produce different conditional paths and targets even though they share the same endpoint marginals.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Many valid couplings between one prior and one data distribution." caption="A coupling chooses which source and target samples define each conditional transport. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 23." %}

A useful principle is to favor couplings whose conditional paths are straight and short. Such paths require a simpler marginal velocity and accumulate less numerical discretization error when the learned ODE is solved with finitely many steps. In practice, a minibatch of source and data samples provides a finite approximation to the coupling problem.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Curved and straight conditional paths under different couplings." caption="A better endpoint pairing can turn a complicated transport into shorter, straighter trajectories. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 24." %}

The expected marginal path length is intractable, so the lecture minimizes an upper bound based on endpoint distances. For Euclidean straight conditional paths, this reduces to a perfect matching problem between the two minibatches:

$$
\min_{\pi}\sum_i\lVert x_0^i-x_1^{\pi(i)}\rVert^2.
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-25.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Minibatch perfect matching as an upper bound on flow path length." caption="Endpoint-distance matching provides a tractable surrogate for shortening the learned transport. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 25." %}

Larger batches offer a better finite approximation to the optimal coupling, though the lecture's benchmark shows diminishing returns in high dimensions. The gain is more promising for lower-dimensional scientific state spaces such as protein backbone design.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sampling cost versus batch size for independent and multisample flow matching couplings." caption="Multisample coupling improves the transport surrogate most clearly in lower-dimensional regimes. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 26." %}

## Symmetry-aware flow couplings

Molecular coordinates introduce another equivalence: rotating the entire molecule or permuting indistinguishable atoms does not change the physical distribution. A raw Euclidean distance can therefore pair two equivalent structures poorly merely because their representatives use different orientations or orderings.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/slide-27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Molecular source and target point clouds related by rotations and atom permutations." caption="A geometric coupling should compare molecular orbits rather than arbitrary coordinate representatives. Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 27." %}

Before adding a pair to the coupling, minimize over the symmetry action:

$$
g^*=\arg\min_{g\in G}
\lVert x_0-\rho(g)x_1\rVert^2.
$$

The Hungarian algorithm handles atom permutation, while Kabsch alignment handles global rotation. Training then uses the aligned pair $$(x_0,\rho(g^*)x_1)$$ in the conditional flow loss. This does not add a new physical target; it chooses the shortest representatives of the same symmetry orbits and thereby removes avoidable motion from the learned path.

Cropped from the 2025 GDL generator-matching lecture, slide 28." %}
