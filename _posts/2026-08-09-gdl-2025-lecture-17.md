Flow matching changes form when the state space changes. In Euclidean space, we learn a velocity field. On a Riemannian manifold, that velocity must lie in a tangent space and use manifold operations. On a discrete space, continuous motion disappears and probability moves through jumps. These constructions look separate because their coordinate descriptions are separate.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s04-domain-flow-matching.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow matching paths on Euclidean, Riemannian, and discrete state spaces." caption="The three flow-matching domains developed earlier in the lecture series. Euclidean and manifold states move continuously, whereas a discrete state changes by jumps. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The common object is not a velocity. It is a continuous-time Markov process: a law whose next infinitesimal move depends only on its present state. Every velocity field defines such a process, but so do stochastic diffusions and jump mechanisms. The infinitesimal generator records all three without pretending that they share the same kind of state update.

This change of viewpoint leads to two distinct questions. The first is how to train a general Markov process by conditional regression, which is the purpose of generator matching. The second is how to choose conditional paths when many endpoint pairings are valid, which leads from multisample couplings to equivariant flow matching.

## One Markov language for several data modalities

The motivating examples already span the relevant process families. A flow follows a smooth deterministic trajectory. A diffusion adds continuous random motion. A jump process stays still between discontinuous events. A continuous-time Markov chain, or CTMC, is the corresponding construction on a discrete state space.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s05-markov-process-families.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Four continuous-time Markov process families: flow, diffusion, jump process, and continuous-time Markov chain." caption="Flow, diffusion, jump, and CTMC trajectories require different local update rules. Generator matching treats them as members of one continuous-time Markov family. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The state space may also mix modalities. A protein model can carry a continuous structure together with a discrete amino-acid sequence. An image-caption model can carry an image in a Euclidean pixel or latent space and text in a discrete token space. There is no reason to force the two components through one artificial coordinate system if their natural Markov mechanisms can instead be combined.

Generator matching starts from a representation theorem used throughout the lecture: under mild assumptions, specifying a Markov process is equivalent to specifying its generator. The process describes complete random trajectories. The generator describes how the conditional expectation of every test function changes over an infinitesimal time step.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s06-process-generator-correspondence.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="One-to-one correspondence between a continuous-time Markov process and its infinitesimal generator under mild assumptions." caption="The generator is an infinitesimal description of a Markov process. Under the regularity assumptions used in the lecture, either side determines the other. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

This is the same strategic move that makes flow matching tractable. We will define a conditional probability path through a conditional generator, average those conditional objects to obtain a marginal generator, and learn its finite-dimensional coefficients from conditional targets. The operator language is new; the conditional-to-marginal architecture is inherited from flow matching.

## A generator differentiates the transition law

Let $$\mathcal S$$ be a general state space and let $$X_t\in\mathcal S$$ for $$t\in[0,1]$$. For ordered times $$t_0<\cdots<t_n<t_{n+1}$$, the Markov property is

$$
\Pr\!\left(X_{t_{n+1}}\in A
\mid X_{t_0},\ldots,X_{t_n}\right)
=
\Pr\!\left(X_{t_{n+1}}\in A
\mid X_{t_n}\right)
$$

for every measurable set $$A\subseteq\mathcal S$$. The present state is sufficient for predicting the next state. This does not mean that the path has no temporal structure, or that successive states are independent. It means that any relevant history has already been compressed into $$X_{t_n}$$.

For an increment $$h>0$$, the transition kernel is

$$
p_{t+h\mid t}(A\mid x)
:=
\Pr(X_{t+h}\in A\mid X_t=x).
$$

The notation separates three pieces: the current state $$x$$, the elapsed time from $$t$$ to $$t+h$$, and the set of possible destinations $$A$$. Acting on a test function $$f:\mathcal S\to\mathbb R$$, the same kernel gives a conditional expectation,

$$
[p_{t+h\mid t}f](x)
:=
\mathbb E[f(X_{t+h})\mid X_t=x].
$$

The initial law $$p_0$$ and the collection of transition kernels determine the finite-dimensional distributions of the process. The Markov property supplies the composition rule that connects several time intervals. This is the finite-time description.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s08-transition-kernel-correspondence.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="One-to-one correspondence between a Markov process and its initial distribution plus transition kernels." caption="The initial distribution tells us where the process begins, and the transition kernels propagate every current state through finite time. Together they determine the Markov process. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The generator takes the derivative at $$h=0$$. For a suitable test function $$f$$,

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
:=
\lim_{h\downarrow0}
\frac{[p_{t+h\mid t}f](x)-f(x)}{h}
=
\lim_{h\downarrow0}
\frac{\mathbb E[f(X_{t+h})\mid X_t=x]-f(x)}{h}.
$$

Equivalently, the short-time expansion is

$$
\mathbb E[f(X_{t+h})\mid X_t=x]
=f(x)+h[\mathcal L_t f](x)+o(h).
$$

The generator is an operator on observables. It does not directly say “add this vector to $$x$$.” Instead, it says how quickly the expected value of every probe $$f$$ changes when the process starts from $$x$$. Choosing different probes exposes different aspects of the same local dynamics.

That distinction is essential once random motion or jumps enter. A deterministic velocity can be read as a state derivative. A diffusion increment is of order $$\sqrt h$$, not $$h$$, and its mean may vanish even while its variance changes the distribution. A jump may travel a finite distance during an interval whose probability is only of order $$h$$. The generator sees the correct first-order effect in all three cases.

## Flow, diffusion, and jump generators

The lecture derives the three terms separately before combining them. The derivations all begin with the same test-function definition, but the small-time increment behaves differently in each process family.

### A deterministic flow gives a first-order operator

Consider the ordinary differential equation

$$
\frac{dX_t}{dt}=u_t(X_t).
$$

Starting from $$X_t=x$$, one small ODE step gives

$$
X_{t+h}=x+h u_t(x)+o(h).
$$

Insert this update into the generator definition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
&=\lim_{h\downarrow0}
\frac{f(x+h u_t(x)+o(h))-f(x)}{h}\\
&=\lim_{h\downarrow0}
\frac{f(x)+h\nabla f(x)^\top u_t(x)+o(h)-f(x)}{h}\\
&=\nabla f(x)^\top u_t(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The learnable object is the velocity $$u_t(x)$$. The operator converts that vector into a directional derivative of $$f$$. This is why ordinary flow matching can regress a vector field rather than a general operator: the operator family is already fixed, and only its first-order coefficient remains unknown.

The formula also identifies the level at which two flows are compared. Generator matching does not directly compare two state increments. It compares the coefficient that determines how those increments act on every test function. For deterministic flows, the coefficient happens to be exactly the familiar velocity.

### A diffusion survives through quadratic variation

Now consider the drift-free Itô stochastic differential equation used in the slides,

$$
dX_t=\sigma_t(X_t)dW_t.
$$

For a short interval, write $$\Delta W=W_{t+h}-W_t$$. Conditional on $$X_t=x$$,

$$
X_{t+h}=x+\sigma_t(x)\Delta W+o(\sqrt h),
$$

with

$$
\mathbb E[\Delta W]=0,
\qquad
\mathbb E[\Delta W\Delta W^\top]=hI.
$$

A second-order Taylor expansion is required because the random increment has size $$\sqrt h$$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x+\sigma_t\Delta W)
&=f(x)
+\nabla f(x)^\top\sigma_t\Delta W\\
&\quad+
\frac12(\sigma_t\Delta W)^\top
\nabla^2f(x)
(\sigma_t\Delta W)
+o(h).
\end{aligned}
$$

The linear term vanishes after expectation. The quadratic term contributes

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[
(\sigma_t\Delta W)^\top\nabla^2f(x)(\sigma_t\Delta W)
\right]
=h\operatorname{tr}\!\left(
\sigma_t(x)\sigma_t(x)^\top\nabla^2f(x)
\right).
$$

Therefore

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\frac12
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(
\sigma_t(x)\sigma_t(x)^\top\nabla^2f(x)
\right).
$$

In the scalar isotropic notation used on the lecture slide, this becomes

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\frac12\sigma_t(x)^2\nabla^2 f(x).
$$

The stochastic increment has zero conditional mean, so a first-order expansion would incorrectly suggest no dynamics. Its covariance is order $$h$$, and that covariance becomes a second-order differential operator. The generator keeps the distributional effect that the mean increment misses.

### A jump process gives a nonlocal operator

Let $$Q_t(dy,x)$$ be a jump-rate kernel. Its total rate out of $$x$$ is

$$
\lambda_t(x)=\int_{\mathcal S}Q_t(dy,x).
$$

Over a sufficiently small interval $$h$$, the process remains at its current state with probability $$1-h\lambda_t(x)+o(h)$$. With probability $$h\lambda_t(x)+o(h)$$, it jumps to a destination distributed according to the normalized kernel

$$
\frac{Q_t(dy,x)}{\lambda_t(x)}.
$$

The short-time conditional expectation is thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[f(X_{t+h})\mid X_t=x]
&=(1-h\lambda_t(x))f(x)\\
&\quad+h\lambda_t(x)
\int f(y)\frac{Q_t(dy,x)}{\lambda_t(x)}
+o(h)\\
&=f(x)+h\int\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)Q_t(dy,x)+o(h).
\end{aligned}
$$

The generator is therefore

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=
\int_{\mathcal S}
\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)Q_t(dy,x).
$$

No derivative of $$f$$ is needed. A jump compares the observable at two separated states, so the operator is nonlocal. On a finite discrete state space, write $$Q_t(y,x)$$ for the rate from $$x$$ to $$y$$. Then

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\sum_{y\ne x}Q_t(y,x)\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr),
$$

which is the generator of a continuous-time Markov chain. The diagonal rate is fixed by conservation,

$$
Q_t(x,x)=-\sum_{y\ne x}Q_t(y,x).
$$

### The general Euclidean generator combines all three

Under the regularity assumptions invoked in the lecture, a continuous-time Markov process on Euclidean space has a generator with drift, diffusion, and jump components:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
&=\nabla f(x)^\top u_t(x)\\
&\quad+
\frac12\operatorname{tr}\!\left(
a_t(x)\nabla^2f(x)
\right)\\
&\quad+
\int\bigl(f(y)-f(x)\bigr)Q_t(dy,x),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $$a_t(x)=\sigma_t(x)\sigma_t(x)^\top$$. The first term moves mass deterministically. The second spreads mass continuously. The third relocates mass through discrete events of finite size.

On a discrete state space, only the jump term remains, so every continuous-time Markov process is a CTMC. On a mixed state space, several components can act together. A protein sequence can jump between residue identities while its coordinates follow a continuous flow; the total generator is the sum of the component generators when their infinitesimal mechanisms are superposed.

The unification is precise because it preserves the differences between the components. Calling everything a “velocity” would hide the Hessian in a diffusion and the nonlocal difference in a jump. Calling everything a generator exposes exactly which coefficient the model must learn in each case.

The coefficient must still define a valid process. A diffusion matrix $$a_t(x)$$ must be positive semidefinite because it is a covariance. Off-diagonal jump rates must be nonnegative, while the diagonal term is determined by the requirement that total probability remains one. A drift has no corresponding sign constraint, but its regularity controls whether the ODE has a well-defined solution. Generator matching supplies one training template across these cases; it does not remove the structural constraints of the selected generator family.

Superposition is easiest to see at this level. If $$\mathcal L_t^{(1)}$$ and $$\mathcal L_t^{(2)}$$ are compatible generators, then

$$
\mathcal L_t
=
\mathcal L_t^{(1)}+\mathcal L_t^{(2)}
$$

describes both infinitesimal mechanisms acting together. A drift and a diffusion produce the usual drift-diffusion process. Independent coordinate-wise jump mechanisms add their rates. A continuous structural flow and a discrete sequence jump process can therefore share one joint clock without treating residues as Euclidean coordinates or turning positions into categories. The sum is an operator statement, while the corresponding neural model may share representations across the component coefficients.

## Conditional generator matching

A generator is an infinite-dimensional operator, so directly asking a neural network to output $$\mathcal L_t$$ is not useful. The lecture instead fixes an operator family and learns its finite-dimensional coefficient. Write

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=\mathcal K\bigl(f,x,F_t(x)\bigr),
$$

where $$\mathcal K$$ is known and linear in $$F_t(x)$$. The three examples identify the relevant coefficient:

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
\text{flow:}
&F_t(x)=u_t(x),
&[\mathcal L_t f](x)=\nabla f(x)^\top F_t(x),\\[4pt]
\text{diffusion:}
&F_t(x)=a_t(x),
&[\mathcal L_t f](x)=\frac12\operatorname{tr}(F_t(x)\nabla^2f(x)),\\[4pt]
\text{jump:}
&F_t(x)=Q_t(\cdot,x),
&[\mathcal L_t f](x)=\int(f(y)-f(x))F_t(dy,x).
\end{array}
$$

For diffusion, the natural pairing is the Frobenius inner product between the diffusion matrix and the Hessian. For jumps, it is the function-space pairing between the jump-rate kernel and the finite difference $$f(y)-f(x)$$. The parameter is not the generator itself; it is the coefficient that becomes a generator after $$\mathcal K$$ acts on a test function.

### Conditional paths define tractable targets

Let $$Z$$ denote a conditioning variable, often a data sample or an endpoint pair. Suppose we can construct a conditional probability path $$p_t(x\mid z)$$ and a conditional generator $$\mathcal L_t^z$$ that generates that path. The marginal path is

$$
p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid z)p(z)dz.
$$

The generator-matching recipe follows three steps from the lecture:

1. Define $$\mathcal L_t^z$$ for the tractable conditional path $$p_t(\cdot\mid z)$$.
2. Express the generator $$\mathcal L_t$$ of the marginal path through conditional generators.
3. Replace an intractable marginal loss by a conditional loss whose gradient is available from samples.

The additional issue, compared with ordinary flow matching, is the operator parameterization. The marginalization identity first holds at the level of generators. It must then descend to the chosen coefficients $$F_t$$ before a neural network can regress them.

### Marginalization happens at the operator level

Let

$$
p_t(z\mid x)
=\frac{p_t(x\mid z)p(z)}{p_t(x)}
$$

be the posterior distribution of the condition after observing the current state. Then the marginal generator is

$$
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
=
\mathbb E_{Z\sim p_t(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[[\mathcal L_t^Z f](x)\right].
$$

This posterior weighting is not optional. A condition $$z$$ whose path assigns negligible density near $$x$$ should contribute negligibly to the local dynamics at $$x$$. The prior $$p(z)$$ tells us how often a condition was sampled; the posterior $$p_t(z\mid x)$$ tells us which conditions could plausibly have produced the observed intermediate state.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s17-conditional-marginal-generators.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A marginal generator transporting one distribution to another beside a conditional generator transporting the source to a single endpoint." caption="The marginal generator combines many conditional paths, while one conditional generator targets a fixed endpoint. Their connection is a posterior expectation over conditions. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The identity can be checked by differentiating expectations. For a conditional path,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}
\mathbb E_{X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid z)}[f(X_t)]
=
\mathbb E_{X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid z)}
\left[[\mathcal L_t^z f](X_t)\right].
$$

Average both sides over $$Z\sim p(z)$$. The left side becomes the derivative under the marginal path. On the right, the tower property gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E_{Z}
\mathbb E_{X_t\mid Z}
\left[[\mathcal L_t^Zf](X_t)\right]\\
&\qquad=
\mathbb E_{X_t}
\mathbb E_{Z\mid X_t}
\left[[\mathcal L_t^Zf](X_t)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The inner posterior expectation must therefore act as the marginal generator on every test function.

### Linearity transfers the identity to learnable coefficients

Suppose the conditional generators use the same linear operator family,

$$
[\mathcal L_t^z f](x)
=\mathcal K\bigl(f,x,F_t^z(x)\bigr).
$$

Linearity in the last argument gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathcal L_t f](x)
&=
\mathbb E_{Z\mid X_t=x}
\left[
\mathcal K(f,x,F_t^Z(x))
\right]\\
&=
\mathcal K\!\left(
f,x,
\mathbb E_{Z\mid X_t=x}[F_t^Z(x)]
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence the marginal coefficient is

$$
F_t(x)
=
\mathbb E_{Z\sim p_t(\cdot\mid x)}
[F_t^Z(x)].
$$

This one equation recovers the familiar conditional-velocity identity for a flow, the conditional diffusion-coefficient identity for a diffusion parameterization, and the posterior average of conditional jump rates for a jump model. The generator family changes, but the conditional-to-marginal averaging rule does not.

### The conditional loss has the correct gradient

Let $$F_t^\theta(x)$$ be the neural coefficient. The inaccessible marginal squared loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{GM}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{t,X_t}
\left[
\left\lVert
F_t^\theta(X_t)-F_t(X_t)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

The tractable conditional objective samples $$Z$$ first and then $$X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid Z)$$:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CGM}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{t,Z,X_t\mid Z}
\left[
\left\lVert
F_t^\theta(X_t)-F_t^Z(X_t)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

Condition on $$X_t=x$$ and expand the square. The term involving the prediction is

$$
-2F_t^\theta(x)^\top
\mathbb E[F_t^Z(x)\mid X_t=x]
=-2F_t^\theta(x)^\top F_t(x).
$$

The remaining conditional-target norm does not depend on $$\theta$$. Consequently,

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CGM}}(\theta)
=
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{GM}}(\theta).
$$

The conditional targets can be noisy estimators of the marginal coefficient, but they produce the same population gradient. Other discrepancies $$D(F_t^Z,F_t^\theta)$$ may be used when they preserve the required conditional-mean property; the slides write the objective in this general form. Squared regression makes the mechanism explicit.

## What the generator framework enables

The applications slide uses three examples to show why an operator-level framework is more than common notation. First, a discrete sequence trajectory can be modeled directly by a jump process rather than by embedding categorical states into a continuous space. The state remains discrete at every time, while the probability mass over possible states changes continuously.

Second, a CTMC contains a genuine stochasticity knob. Two chains can share the same one-time probability path while using different jump activity: one may switch states frequently and another may make only a few jumps. A deterministic velocity field does not expose this degree of freedom because every initial state has one trajectory. A jump generator specifies both where mass moves and how random individual paths remain.

Third, separate generators can be combined for multimodal protein generation. A structural component generates geometry; a sequence component generates residue identities; inverse folding predicts sequence from structure; forward folding maps sequence back to structure; and a co-generation model evolves both modalities together.

Generator matching therefore separates two design choices. The conditional probability path says what marginal distributions should be visited. The generator family says what kinds of trajectories may realize those marginals: deterministic flow, diffusion, jumps, or a sum of mechanisms. The next half of the lecture returns to deterministic flow matching and changes a different choice—the coupling between endpoints.

## Flow matching leaves the coupling open

The flow-matching recap begins with a target data law $$p_1$$ and a source law $$p_0$$. We seek a parameterized ODE

$$
dX_t=u_t^\theta(X_t)dt
$$

whose time-one distribution is $$p_1$$. A conditioning variable $$Z$$ defines a tractable conditional path $$p_t(x\mid z)$$ and conditional velocity $$u_t(x\mid z)$$. Posterior averaging gives

$$
u_t(x)
=
\mathbb E[u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x].
$$

The marginal velocity is intractable, but the conditional objective

$$
\mathbb E_{t,Z,X_t\mid Z}
\left[
\lVert u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid Z)\rVert^2
\right]
$$

has the same parameter gradient as regression against $$u_t(x)$$. This is exactly the flow member of the generator-matching construction.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s21-flow-matching-recap.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Conditional and marginal probability paths together with their conditional and marginal velocity fields." caption="The four objects in conditional flow matching: a conditional path, its mixture, a conditional velocity, and the posterior-averaged marginal velocity. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The condition does not have to be the target endpoint alone. It only has to define conditional paths whose averaged endpoints equal the desired boundary laws:

$$
\int p_0(x\mid z)p(z)dz=p_0(x),
\qquad
\int p_1(x\mid z)p(z)dz=p_1(x).
$$

Three choices in the lecture satisfy this contract:

- $$Z=X_1$$: sample a data endpoint and build a conditional path toward it.
- $$Z=X_0$$: sample a source endpoint and build a conditional path away from it.
- $$Z=(X_0,X_1)$$: sample a paired source and target endpoint and connect the pair.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s22-conditioning-choices.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three flow-matching constructions conditioned on the target endpoint, source endpoint, or an endpoint pair." caption="Target, source, and endpoint-pair conditioning all recover the same required boundary marginals when their conditional paths are averaged. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The source distribution need not have a tractable density when endpoint pairs can be sampled. Conditional flow matching uses samples and conditional velocities, not an evaluation of $$\log p_0(x)$$. The lecture makes this point with a corrupted-image source: even if its density is unavailable, samples from that source can still be paired with clean targets.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s22-intractable-prior.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A corrupted dog-image source distribution paired with a clean dog-image target distribution." caption="Flow matching can pair samples from a source whose density is intractable. The conditional path needs endpoint samples and a tractable local construction, not a closed-form prior density. Lecture illustration by the author." %}

Once $$Z=(X_0,X_1)$$, we must choose a joint law

$$
q(x_0,x_1)
$$

with marginals

$$
p_0(x_0)=\int q(x_0,x_1)dx_1,
\qquad
p_1(x_1)=\int q(x_0,x_1)dx_0.
$$

Any such joint law is a coupling. Independent pairing, $$q=p_0\otimes p_1$$, is valid but only one possibility. It ignores the geometry of the two distributions: a source point may be paired with a distant target even when a closer target exists.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s23-endpoint-couplings.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Several possible pairings between samples from a source density and a target density." caption="A coupling decides which source sample travels to which target sample. The endpoint marginals constrain the number of samples on each side but do not uniquely determine the connecting lines. Lecture diagram by the author." %}

The conditional loss for paired endpoints is

$$
\mathbb E_{t,(X_0,X_1)\sim q,
X_t\sim p_t(\cdot\mid X_0,X_1)}
\left[
\lVert
u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid X_0,X_1)
\rVert^2
\right].
$$

Changing $$q$$ changes the conditional paths and regression targets. It may also change the intermediate marginal path and the learned velocity, even though $$p_0$$ and $$p_1$$ stay fixed. The coupling is therefore part of the generative construction, not merely a data-loader detail.

## Multisample couplings shorten conditional transport

The lecture's design principle is to find a coupling that induces straight paths. A smooth marginal velocity has kinetic action

$$
\int_0^1
\mathbb E_{X_t\sim p_t}
\left[
\lVert u_t(X_t)\rVert^2
\right]dt.
$$

Using the posterior-average identity and Jensen's inequality,

$$
\left\lVert
\mathbb E[u_t(X_t\mid X_0,X_1)\mid X_t]
\right\rVert^2
\le
\mathbb E[
\lVert u_t(X_t\mid X_0,X_1)\rVert^2
\mid X_t].
$$

After averaging over $$X_t$$ and time, the marginal action is bounded above by the expected conditional action. Straight conditional interpolation between paired endpoints has constant velocity $$X_1-X_0$$, so its conditional action is

$$
\int_0^1
\lVert X_1-X_0\rVert^2dt
=
\lVert X_1-X_0\rVert^2.
$$

This reduces the surrogate objective to transport cost under the coupling:

$$
\min_q
\mathbb E_{(X_0,X_1)\sim q}
[\lVert X_1-X_0\rVert^2].
$$

Short endpoint pairings reduce an upper bound on the marginal kinetic action. They also make the learned paths closer to straight conditional paths. A straighter velocity field usually tolerates coarser numerical integration because it bends less between solver evaluations. This is the lecture's reason for connecting coupling quality to discretization error.

The right-hand transport removes many avoidable crossings. From <a href='https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/pooladian23a.html'>Pooladian et al.</a>" %}

### A minibatch turns coupling into perfect matching

The population optimal-transport coupling is usually unavailable. Multisample flow matching approximates it inside a minibatch. Sample

$$
X_0^{(1)},\ldots,X_0^{(k)}\sim p_0,
\qquad
X_1^{(1)},\ldots,X_1^{(k)}\sim p_1.
$$

Let $$B_k$$ be the set of permutations of $$k$$ items. Solve

$$
\pi^k
=
\arg\min_{\pi\in B_k}
\sum_{i=1}^{k}
\left\lVert
X_0^{(i)}-X_1^{(\pi(i))}
\right\rVert^2.
$$

Then sample matched pairs $$(X_0^{(i)},X_1^{(\pi^k(i))})$$ for conditional flow matching. Because each source and target sample appears exactly once, the empirical marginals remain the two minibatches. The permutation changes only the joint pairing.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s25-minibatch-perfect-matching.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three stages of multisample coupling: draw two minibatches, solve a minimum-cost permutation, and sample matched pairs." caption="Minibatch optimal transport keeps the empirical source and target marginals while replacing independent pairing with a minimum-cost permutation. Lecture figure following <a href='https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/pooladian23a.html'>Pooladian et al.</a>" %}

Batch size controls how many alternative partners the matching algorithm can consider. At $$k=1$$, the procedure is independent pairing because there is no choice. As $$k$$ increases, each point can be paired against a larger candidate set, and the empirical coupling can better reflect local geometry.

The improvement is not free. The matching problem adds computation, and a finite minibatch is not the population optimal coupling. The benchmark retained in the lecture shows a sharp initial decrease in cost followed by diminishing returns. It also states the empirical boundary directly: in high-dimensional problems, the sampling-speed improvement over tailored samplers can be minor, whereas lower-dimensional scientific problems such as protein-backbone generation appear more promising.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s26-minibatch-coupling-cost.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Coupling cost decreases with minibatch size for a baseline coupling and a flow-matching coupling, with diminishing returns." caption="Larger batches lower the finite matching cost, but most of the reduction occurs early. The lecture relates this behavior to <a href='https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/pooladian23a.html'>Pooladian et al.</a>, <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00482'>Tong et al.</a>, and the protein application of <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02391'>Bose et al.</a>" %}

The key claim is not that minibatch matching recovers exact optimal transport. It is that the coupling is a controllable part of conditional flow matching, and even a finite matching can remove obviously long, crossing pairings while preserving the endpoint marginals.

## Equivariant flow matching compares symmetry orbits

Molecular coordinates add another ambiguity. Rotating a molecule changes its coordinate matrix but not the physical structure. Permuting identical atoms changes the ordering of rows but not the molecule. A distance computed between stored coordinate arrays can therefore be large even when the two arrays represent the same point in physical configuration space.

The lecture illustrates this with two coordinate representatives and their symmetry copies. A raw coupling sees every rotated or permuted copy as a separate candidate. A geometric coupling should instead compare the orbits generated by the symmetry group.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s27-noise-orbit-molecule.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A molecular coordinate representative connected by arrows to several rotated copies." caption="One molecular endpoint has an orbit of rotated coordinate representatives. The group preserves the repeated molecular image and the transformation arrows from the lecture." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-17/s27-data-orbit-molecule.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Another molecular coordinate representative connected by arrows to several rotated copies." caption="The target molecule has its own orbit of equivalent representatives. Comparing the two orbits avoids making an arbitrary stored orientation part of the transport problem. " %}

Let a group $$G$$ act on coordinates through $$\rho(g)$$. For a sampled source $$x_0$$ and target $$x_1$$, choose the representative of the target orbit that is closest to the source:

$$
g^*
=
\arg\min_{g\in G}
\left\lVert
x_0-\rho(g)x_1
\right\rVert^2.
$$

Then treat

$$
(x_0,\rho(g^*)x_1)
$$

as the endpoint pair in the conditional flow-matching loss. This is the orbit-level counterpart of minibatch perfect matching. Minibatch matching searches over assignments between samples; symmetry alignment searches over equivalent representatives of one sample. The two searches can also be combined when both ambiguities are present.

For atom permutations, the discrete minimization is an assignment problem and can be solved with the Hungarian algorithm once a pairwise cost is defined. For global rotations, the Kabsch algorithm solves the least-squares alignment after centering corresponding points. In a molecule with both permutation and rotation symmetry, the operations may need to be coordinated because the optimal rotation depends on the correspondence and the optimal correspondence depends on the rotation.

The conditional objective becomes

$$
\min_\theta
\mathbb E
\left[
\left\lVert
u_t^\theta(X_t)
-u_t\!\left(
X_t\mid x_0,\rho(g^*)x_1
\right)
\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

Alignment does not change the target distribution when that distribution is invariant under the group action. It selects a representative from the same orbit and removes motion caused only by coordinate convention. The conditional path is shorter because it no longer spends time rotating the whole molecule or swapping indistinguishable indices merely to reconcile two arbitrary encodings.

The order of operations matters. First sample $$x_0$$ from the source and $$x_1$$ from the target. Next solve the group-alignment problem for that pair. Only then sample an intermediate state from the conditional path between $$x_0$$ and $$\rho(g^*)x_1$$ and evaluate its conditional velocity. Aligning an already sampled intermediate state would define a different path and generally a different training target. The group search belongs to coupling construction, before conditional interpolation.

This construction also clarifies what equivariance contributes. An equivariant velocity model guarantees that transforming the input transforms the predicted velocity consistently. Orbit-aware coupling addresses a different question: which transformed endpoint should define the training path in the first place. An equivariant architecture can still be trained on needlessly long paths if its coupling ignores equivalent representatives. Conversely, alignment alone does not make a non-equivariant network equivariant.

The lecture ends with that division of labor. Generator matching chooses a valid infinitesimal mechanism for the state space: flow, diffusion, jumps, or a composition. Conditional flow matching chooses tractable paths whose posterior average produces the desired marginal dynamics. Multisample and equivariant couplings choose endpoint pairs that avoid unnecessary transport. These are separate choices, and each changes a different part of the learned generative process.
