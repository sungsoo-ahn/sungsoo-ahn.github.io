## Flow matching before geometry

Flow matching begins with a time-dependent velocity field and the ODE

$$
\frac{dX_t}{dt}=u_t(X_t).
$$

Its solution defines a one-to-one flow map $$X_t=\psi_t(X_0)$$. If $$X_0$$ follows the base density $$p_0$$, then the push-forward $$(\psi_t)_\#p_0$$ is the marginal density $$p_t$$. This is the deterministic analogue of specifying an SDE and following its probability law, but the endpoint convention used here takes $$t=0$$ as noise and $$t=1$$ as data.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s05-flow-map.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow matching ODE, flow map, and push-forward probability path." caption="A velocity field generates a flow map, and the flow map pushes the base density along a probability path." %}

The marginal path is usually introduced through tractable conditional paths. For a data endpoint $$x_1$$,

$$
p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid x_1)p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_1)\,dx_1,
$$

where every $$p_t(\cdot\mid x_1)$$ is generated by a conditional velocity $$u_t(\cdot\mid x_1)$$. A familiar Euclidean choice is the Gaussian path induced by $$X_t=(1-t)X_0+t x_1$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s06-conditional-marginal-paths.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Conditional probability paths marginalized into a flow matching probability path." caption="Tractable endpoint-conditioned paths marginalize to the target probability path." %}

Four objects must remain distinct. Conditional velocities generate conditional paths. Marginalizing the conditional paths gives the marginal path. The marginal velocity that generates this path is a posterior average,

$$
u_t(x)=\int u_t(x\mid x_1)p(x_1\mid X_t=x)\,dx_1,
$$

and is generally intractable even when its conditional ingredients are easy to sample.


Direct regression to the marginal velocity is therefore unavailable. Conditional flow matching replaces it with

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert u_\theta(X_t,t)-u_t(X_t\mid X_1)\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

The conditional and marginal squared-error losses have the same parameter gradient. Sampling the condition makes the target tractable while preserving the optimization problem that matters.

The identity is worth writing once because it is the bridge that survives the move to manifolds. Let

$$
U=u_t(X_tmid X_1),
\qquad
\bar U=\mathbb E[Umid X_t].
$$

For any predictor $$v_	heta(X_t,t)$$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\lVert v_	heta-U\rVert^2
&=\mathbb E\lVert v_	heta-\bar U\rVert^2
 +\mathbb E\lVert U-\bar U\rVert^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The cross term vanishes after conditioning on $$X_t$$, and the second term does not depend on $$\theta$$. Conditional regression therefore learns the posterior-averaged marginal velocity without evaluating that conditional expectation explicitly. Geometry will change the space in which the vectors live and the norm used to compare them, but not this conditional-expectation argument.


The condition need not be only the data endpoint. It can include the source and endpoint together, or any variable $$z$$ that indexes tractable conditional paths whose mixture is the desired marginal path. This flexibility becomes useful once a geometry admits several ways to define a conditional transport.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s09-conditioning-choices.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow matching conditioned on an endpoint, source, or source endpoint pair." caption="Flow matching permits richer conditions than a single endpoint, provided their conditional paths marginalize correctly." %}

For a concrete Euclidean path, take $$X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$$ and pair it with one endpoint $$X_1=x_1$$. The affine interpolation

$$
X_t=(1-t)X_0+t x_1
$$

has conditional law $$\mathcal N(t x_1,(1-t)^2I)$$ and constant pathwise velocity $$x_1-X_0$$. Eliminating $$X_0=(X_t-tx_1)/(1-t)$$ gives the velocity at the current state,

$$
u_t(X_t\mid x_1)=\frac{x_1-X_t}{1-t}.
$$

The apparent singularity as $$t\to1$$ is canceled along an exact conditional path because $$x_1-X_t=(1-t)(x_1-X_0)$$. A numerical solver or imperfect network need not enjoy that exact cancellation, which is one reason endpoint parameterizations, time schedules, and terminal cutoffs matter in practice. The manifold formula later in the lecture keeps the same structure and replaces the coordinate difference by a logarithmic map.

## Why generative models need manifolds

Many scientific objects do not naturally inhabit one unconstrained Euclidean vector space. Protein conformations have bond and rotational constraints; molecular conformers mix Cartesian and periodic variables; probability distributions lie on a simplex; Earth coordinates lie on a sphere; rotations form $$\mathrm{SO}(3)$$. The lecture places these examples side by side because “non-Euclidean” is not one data type but a recurring structural condition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s11-manifold-domains.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Scientific examples of non Euclidean data including proteins, conformations, probability distributions, and Earth coordinates." caption="Scientific data often obey geometric constraints that ordinary vector addition ignores." %}

The same issue appears in three-dimensional surfaces, latent manifolds, and datasets whose meaningful variations occupy a lower-dimensional subset of the ambient representation. A model can store such an object in Euclidean coordinates without making its valid state space Euclidean.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s12-embedded-manifolds.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Rabbit surface, latent imaging manifold, and data driven manifold examples." caption="Extrinsic coordinates may contain the data while the admissible configurations remain a curved manifold." %}

This breaks the most convenient Euclidean flow-matching recipe in two places. There is no universal standard Gaussian intrinsic to an arbitrary manifold, and the affine interpolation

$$
(1-t)x_0+t x_1
$$

uses addition and scalar multiplication that may leave the state space. A learned velocity must also be a legitimate direction at its current point rather than an arbitrary ambient vector.

The unit sphere makes both failures visible with one line of arithmetic. Let $$x_0=(1,0,0)$$ and $$x_1=(0,1,0)$$. Their Euclidean midpoint is $$(1/2,1/2,0)$$, whose norm is $$1/\sqrt2$$ rather than one. Renormalizing the midpoint returns a valid state, but renormalization silently changes the path and its speed. It also becomes unstable when the interpolated vector approaches zero, as it does for antipodal endpoints. A manifold construction must specify the path rather than repair an invalid Euclidean path afterward.

Rotations expose a related algebraic failure. A state $$R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$$ must satisfy $$R^\top R=I$$ and $$\det R=1$$. The arithmetic mean of two rotation matrices generally satisfies neither condition. Projecting it back with an SVD gives a rotation, but that projection is an algorithmic choice, not the straight line intrinsic to the rotation group. On a torus, the problem appears as periodicity: angles $$\theta=-\pi+0.01$$ and $$\phi=\pi-0.01$$ are close on the circle although their stored real numbers differ by almost $$2\pi$$.


The replacement starts by adding geometric structure in stages. A smooth manifold supplies a global differentiable space. A Riemannian metric equips each tangent space with an inner product. A velocity field assigns a tangent vector to every point, and integrating it produces a flow that remains on the manifold.




Straight lines are replaced by geodesics. In [Riemannian Flow Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03660), this replacement makes conditional flow matching intrinsic to a Riemannian manifold. On manifolds with tractable geometry—Euclidean space, spheres, tori, and some hyperbolic spaces—the conditional flow can be written with the exponential and logarithmic maps. For endpoints $$x_0,x_1\in\mathcal M$$,

$$
\psi_t(x_0\mid x_1)
=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}\!\left(t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(x_1)\right).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s17-geodesic-conditional-flow.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Geodesic conditional path replacing a Euclidean straight line." caption="Geodesics provide the manifold analogue of straight conditional paths." %}

The Euclidean norm in the regression loss must change as well. If the model and target velocities lie in $$T_{X_t}\mathcal M$$, their discrepancy is measured with the metric at $$X_t$$:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid X_1)\right\rVert_{g}^2
\right].
$$

The marginal and conditional Riemannian flow-matching losses retain the same gradient-equivalence principle.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s18-riemannian-flow-losses.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Riemannian flow matching and conditional flow matching losses." caption="The regression target is tangent-valued and its norm is evaluated with the Riemannian metric." %}

This replacement has a strict division of labor. The manifold specifies valid states and tangent directions. The metric specifies vector lengths and shortest paths. The chosen conditional coupling specifies which source is paired with which target. The schedule specifies how quickly the pair is traversed. The network approximates the resulting marginal field. Changing any one of these ingredients changes the generative model even when the architecture is held fixed. “Use a manifold” is therefore not a complete recipe; it is a demand that every later operation declare which geometry it respects.

The source distribution illustrates the issue. On a compact homogeneous space such as a sphere or rotation group, a uniform distribution with respect to the invariant volume can be a natural analogue of isotropic noise. On a noncompact manifold, a normal distribution centered at a reference point can be defined by sampling a tangent Gaussian and applying Exp, but its density is not obtained by simply reusing the Euclidean Gaussian formula: the exponential map changes volume and may cover some endpoints more than once. On a product space, the source may factor across components only after physical units and relative metric weights are fixed.

Suppose a molecular state combines a translation $$r\in\mathbb R^3$$, a rotation $$R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$$, and torsions $$\theta\in\mathbb T^k$$. A product metric might be written schematically as

$$
\lVert(v_r,v_R,v_\theta)\rVert_g^2
=w_r\lVert v_r\rVert^2
+w_R\lVert v_R\rVert^2
+w_\theta\lVert v_\theta\rVert^2.
$$

The weights compare quantities with different units and scales. They affect the geodesic, loss, and effective learning rate of every component. Choosing them by convention is still a modeling decision. Similarly, an independent product coupling is not the same as a physically correlated coupling between orientation and torsion. Geometry makes the operations legal; it does not decide which statistical dependencies the generative path should preserve.

## Smooth manifolds and coordinate representations

A smooth manifold $$\mathcal M$$ is globally allowed to curve or wrap, but locally resembles $$\mathbb R^d$$. A point belongs to the abstract space itself, not automatically to one privileged coordinate system.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s19-local-euclidean-patches.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Curved manifold locally mapped to Euclidean patches." caption="A manifold can be globally non-Euclidean while every sufficiently small patch looks Euclidean." %}

A chart $$(U_i,\phi_i)$$ maps a patch $$U_i\subset\mathcal M$$ to coordinates in $$\mathbb R^d$$. An atlas is a collection of such charts that covers the manifold. Smoothness means that overlapping charts agree through smooth transition maps rather than through identical coordinates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s20-chart-atlas.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Coordinate chart mapping a manifold patch into Euclidean space." caption="Charts create local Euclidean coordinates without flattening the entire manifold at once." %}

If $$U_i\cap U_j$$ is nonempty, the coordinate change

$$
\phi_j\circ\phi_i^{-1}
$$

must be smooth on the overlap. This compatibility is what makes differentiation independent of the chosen chart.

The point is not that coordinates are forbidden. It is that a coordinate formula must transform consistently. If $$x=\phi_i(p)$$ and $$y=\phi_j(p)$$ describe the same point, then a tangent with coefficients $$v_x$$ in the first chart has coefficients

$$
v_y
=D(\phi_j\circ\phi_i^{-1})(x),v_x
$$

in the second. A neural field that predicts unrelated coefficient vectors in two overlapping charts does not define one geometric velocity. Either the architecture, its features, or an explicit transformation rule must make these two predictions agree. This is the chart analogue of requiring a rotation-equivariant vector field in an ambient representation.


There are two common computational representations. An extrinsic representation embeds $$\mathcal M$$ in a higher-dimensional ambient space through an inclusion $$\iota:\mathcal M\hookrightarrow\mathbb R^n$$. The unit sphere, for example, is a two-dimensional manifold embedded in $$\mathbb R^3$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s22-extrinsic-sphere.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sphere represented extrinsically as a manifold embedded in three dimensions." caption="An extrinsic representation stores manifold points in an ambient Euclidean space with constraints." %}

An intrinsic representation instead works in chart coordinates. Stereographic projection maps most of a sphere to a plane, letting ordinary coordinate computations represent local manifold operations. The convenience is local: the excluded pole and chart transitions still matter.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s23-stereographic-projection.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Stereographic projection as intrinsic local coordinates on a sphere." caption="Intrinsic coordinates move computation into a chart, but no single chart must cover every point." %}

Some manifolds do admit familiar global coordinates over almost all of the space. Spherical coordinates use radius, azimuth, and polar angle, although their singularities and periodic identifications show why a coordinate tuple is not the same thing as the geometry itself.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s24-spherical-coordinates.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Spherical coordinate system as a global coordinate choice with singularities." caption="Global coordinates can be useful while still carrying singularities and identifications." %}

For the unit sphere, longitude--latitude coordinates give

$$
x(\lambda,\varphi)
=\bigl(\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,
       \cos\varphi\sin\lambda,
       \sin\varphi\bigr).
$$

At the poles, every longitude represents the same point, so a model that treats $$\lambda$$ as an ordinary real coordinate invents distinctions that the state space does not contain. At the antimeridian, nearby points can have coordinate values near $$-\pi$$ and $$\pi$$. These are not rare numerical curiosities: a learned flow can cross a seam or approach a pole during sampling. Extrinsic coordinates avoid chart seams but require constraints and tangent projection; intrinsic coordinates reduce dimension but require chart management. Neither representation is universally superior.

## Tangent vectors and velocity fields

A curve $$\gamma:\mathbb R\to\mathcal M$$ gives a valid motion through the manifold. In Euclidean space its tangent is the difference quotient of coordinates. On an abstract manifold, however, subtraction such as $$\gamma(h)-\gamma(0)$$ is not intrinsically defined.


The coordinate-free definition looks at how the curve differentiates every smooth test function $$f:\mathcal M\to\mathbb R$$:

$$
X_{\gamma,p}f
=\left.\frac{d}{dt}(f\circ\gamma)(t)\right|_{t=0},
\qquad p=\gamma(0).
$$

Two curves define the same tangent vector when they produce the same directional derivative on every smooth test function. All such tangent vectors at $$p$$ form the tangent space $$T_p\mathcal M$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s27-tangent-derivation.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent vector defined as a directional derivative operator." caption="A tangent vector records infinitesimal change through its action on smooth functions." %}

Charts provide a basis. If $$x^1,\ldots,x^d$$ are local coordinates, then every tangent vector can be written

$$
v=\sum_{i=1}^d v^i\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\bigg|_p.
$$

The coefficients depend on the chart, while the tangent vector does not. The derivation follows by inserting $$\phi^{-1}\circ\phi$$ into the curve and applying the ordinary chain rule in $$\mathbb R^d$$.


With an extrinsic representation, the differential of the inclusion maps intrinsic tangent coordinates into the ambient space. For the sphere embedded in $$\mathbb R^3$$, the result is the two-dimensional plane orthogonal to the radius vector.


This yields three model-design choices. A network can output coordinates in a local tangent basis. It can output an ambient vector already constrained to the tangent space. Or it can output an unconstrained ambient vector $$\nu(x)$$ and project it:

$$
u(x)=\Pi_x\nu(x)\in T_x\mathcal M.
$$

For the unit sphere, $$\Pi_x=I-xx^\top$$. The projection is not decorative; without it, a numerical step generally leaves the manifold.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s30-velocity-designs.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Local coordinate, ambient, and projected designs for manifold velocity fields." caption="Velocity parameterizations differ in where they enforce membership in the tangent space." %}

Take $$x=(0,0,1)$$ and suppose an ambient network returns $$\nu=(2,-1,3)$$. Because

$$
xx^\top=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&1
\end{bmatrix},
$$

the projected velocity is $$\Pi_x\nu=(2,-1,0)$$. Its dot product with $$x$$ is zero, so it lies in $$T_x\mathbb S^2$$. A naive step $$x+h\Pi_x\nu$$ is still only first-order valid: its norm is $$\sqrt{1+5h^2}$$. The tangent constraint makes the instantaneous ODE valid, while a retraction or exponential map is still needed to place a finite numerical update back on the sphere.

This separates two checks that are often conflated. A **field check** asks whether $$u(x)\in T_x\mathcal M$$. A **solver check** asks whether the discrete map from $$x_k$$ to $$x_{k+1}$$ remains on $$\mathcal M$$ and approximates the intended continuous trajectory. An equivariant or tangent-valued network can pass the first and fail the second if it is paired with an unconstrained Euclidean integrator.

## Riemannian metrics, distances, and geodesics

A tangent space supplies directions but not their lengths. A Riemannian metric assigns a smoothly varying inner product $$g_p$$ to each $$T_p\mathcal M$$. On a sphere, the inherited ambient dot product is one natural choice, but a metric is extra structure rather than a consequence of smoothness alone.


Different manifolds carry different natural metrics. Euclidean space uses the identity matrix, the sphere uses the ambient inner product restricted to its tangent plane, and hyperbolic space uses a position-dependent metric. A smooth manifold equipped with this choice is a Riemannian manifold $$(\mathcal M,g)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s33-metric-geometries.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean sphere and hyperbolic examples of Riemannian metrics." caption="The metric, not merely the point set, determines lengths and angles on a Riemannian manifold." %}

In local coordinates, the metric is represented by a positive-definite matrix $$G(p)$$:

$$
\langle u,v\rangle_{g,p}=u^\top G(p)v.
$$

This “Riemannian metric” should not be confused with a metric-space distance function. It is a local inner product from which lengths, angles, and eventually distances are derived.


The corresponding norm and angle are

$$
\lVert v\rVert_{g,p}=\sqrt{v^\top G(p)v},
\qquad
\cos\theta=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle_{g,p}}
{\lVert u\rVert_{g,p}\lVert v\rVert_{g,p}}.
$$

Unlike a fixed Euclidean dot product, these quantities may change with the base point even when the coordinate coefficients do not.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s35-norm-angle-loss.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Vector lengths and angles computed by a Riemannian metric." caption="The metric is the computational device for tangent norms and angles." %}

To compare two points $$p,q\in\mathcal M$$, first consider a smooth curve $$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal M$$ joining them. Its instantaneous speed is the metric norm of $$\dot\gamma(t)$$.


The curve length and induced geodesic distance are

$$
L_g(\gamma)=\int_0^1
\sqrt{\dot\gamma(t)^\top G(\gamma(t))\dot\gamma(t)}\,dt,
$$

$$
d_g(p,q)=\inf_{\gamma:p\to q}L_g(\gamma).
$$

A shortest path is a geodesic. Geodesics can also be characterized as stationary curves of the energy functional, which is why they replace straight lines in conditional transport.


Changing the metric changes the shortest path even when the underlying point set is unchanged. On a probability simplex, a Euclidean metric produces linear segments, while the Fisher--Rao metric measures motion according to the information carried by changes in the distribution.

On the unit sphere with the inherited metric, two points satisfy

$$
d_{\mathbb S^2}(p,q)=\arccos(p^\top q).
$$

For the quarter-turn pair $$(1,0,0)$$ and $$(0,1,0)$$, the geodesic distance is $$\pi/2$$. Their ambient chord length is only $$\sqrt2$$. Both numbers are valid measurements, but they answer different questions: one measures travel constrained to the sphere, the other measures a straight displacement through the ambient space. A loss that uses the chord may be a useful approximation for nearby points, yet it is not the intrinsic metric claimed by a Riemannian formulation.

The probability simplex provides an even sharper contrast. Under the square-root map $$p\mapsto 2\sqrt p$$, the Fisher--Rao metric becomes spherical. For categorical distributions $$p$$ and $$q$$, its geodesic distance can be written

$$
d_{\mathrm{FR}}(p,q)
=2\arccos\left(\sum_i\sqrt{p_iq_i}\right).
$$

Moving a probability mass near the boundary can therefore have a very different length from moving the same coordinate amount near the center. The example explains why the metric belongs in the learning objective rather than only in a visualization of the manifold.


Distances make geometric training objectives possible. Flow matching compares tangent velocities; diffusion objectives compare tangent scores; both can use geodesic distance to construct conditional paths. But an objective is only half of a generative model: inference still needs a way to take numerical steps that stay on $$\mathcal M$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s39-flow-diffusion-losses.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow and diffusion losses expressed with manifold distances." caption="A Riemannian distance turns Euclidean flow and diffusion losses into geometry-aware objectives." %}

An Euler update $$x_{t+\Delta t}=x_t+u_t(x_t)\Delta t$$ assumes that points and tangent vectors can be added. Brownian diffusion additionally assumes Euclidean Gaussian increments. Neither operation is intrinsic to a manifold, so ODE and SDE solvers need geometric update maps and manifold-valued noise.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s40-inference-constraints.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean ODE and SDE updates contrasted with manifold inference requirements." caption="Manifold inference cannot use unconstrained coordinate addition as its state update." %}

“Gaussian noise in the tangent space” also needs a basis convention. If $$e_1(x),\ldots,e_d(x)$$ is an orthonormal frame under $$g_x$$ and $$\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$$, then

$$
\eta_x=\sum_{a=1}^d\xi_a e_a(x)
$$

is isotropic in $$T_x\mathcal M$$. Sampling independent ambient coordinates and projecting them can produce the same law for an embedded sphere with the inherited metric, but not for every embedding or metric. In local coordinates, the covariance must involve $$G(x)^{-1}$$. The phrase “add Gaussian noise” is therefore incomplete until it names the measure and the tangent covariance.

Continuous-time SDE notation brings one further choice. An intrinsic Brownian motion can be written with a moving orthonormal frame in Stratonovich form, where ordinary chain rules respect coordinate changes. Converting that equation to Itô form adds a geometry-dependent drift correction. Alternatively, one can define the process through the Laplace--Beltrami generator. These descriptions agree when their correction terms and conventions are matched; omitting them changes the marginal law. The deterministic flow-matching part of the lecture avoids this stochastic subtlety, while the comparison figure keeps it visible because geometric diffusion models must address it explicitly.

## Exponential, logarithmic, and transport maps

Three operators replace the missing vector-space primitives. The exponential map moves from a tangent space to the manifold, the logarithmic map moves a nearby manifold point back to a tangent space, and parallel transport compares vectors based at different points.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s42-manifold-operators.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Questions answered by exponential logarithmic maps and parallel transport." caption="Exp, Log, and parallel transport provide the basic movement operations absent from a generic manifold." %}

For $$v\in T_p\mathcal M$$, let $$\gamma_v$$ be the geodesic satisfying $$\gamma_v(0)=p$$ and $$\dot\gamma_v(0)=v$$. The exponential map is

$$
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=\gamma_v(1).
$$

In Euclidean space it reduces exactly to $$p+v$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s43-exponential-map.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential map following a geodesic from a tangent vector to a manifold point." caption="The exponential map is the geometry-aware replacement for adding a displacement to a point." %}

Locally, the logarithmic map inverts the exponential map:

$$
\operatorname{Log}_p(q)=v
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=q.
$$

It returns the initial tangent of the geodesic from $$p$$ to $$q$$. The inverse is only guaranteed where $$\operatorname{Exp}_p$$ is a diffeomorphism; beyond that neighborhood, several geodesics may connect the same endpoints.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s44-logarithmic-map.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Logarithmic map from a manifold point to the tangent space at a base point." caption="The logarithmic map expresses a nearby endpoint as a tangent displacement from its base point." %}

Parallel transport moves $$v\in T_p\mathcal M$$ along a specified curve to a vector in $$T_q\mathcal M$$ while preserving the metric inner product. Along that curve it is reversible and preserves lengths and angles. The curve matters: tangent spaces at different points are distinct vector spaces, so there is no canonical subtraction of their vectors without additional structure.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s45-parallel-transport.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Parallel transport of a tangent vector along a curve on a sphere." caption="Parallel transport compares tangent vectors at different base points without leaving the geometry." %}

These replacements reproduce Euclidean conditional flow matching in geometric form. A straight-line displacement $$(x_1-x_t)/(1-t)$$ becomes

$$
u_t(x_t\mid x_1)
=\frac{\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_1)}{1-t}.
$$

For $$\mathrm{SO}(3)$$, Exp and Log are matrix exponential and logarithm operations, with closed forms available through the rotation angle and skew-symmetric generator.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s46-euclidean-so3-operations.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean and SO3 logarithmic map velocities for conditional flow matching." caption="Replacing subtraction by the logarithmic map gives the target velocity on Euclidean space and on rotations." %}

The sphere supplies a closed-form version of the same operators. For unit vectors $$p,q\in\mathbb S^2$$ with $$\theta=\arccos(p^\top q)$$ and $$0<\theta<\pi$$,

$$
\operatorname{Log}_p(q)
=\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}
\left(q-(p^\top q)p\right),
$$

while for $$v\in T_p\mathbb S^2$$,

$$
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)
=\cos\lVert v\rVert\,p
 +\sin\lVert v\rVert\frac{v}{\lVert v\rVert}.
$$

For $$p=(1,0,0)$$ and $$q=(0,1,0)$$, the logarithmic displacement is $$(0,\pi/2,0)$$. Applying Exp at half of that vector gives $$(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2,0)$$, the intrinsic midpoint that the earlier affine example failed to produce. At antipodal points $$\sin\theta=0$$ and the shortest geodesic is not unique; this is the cut-locus limitation hidden by the compact formula.

On $$\mathrm{SO}(3)$$, let $$R_0$$ and $$R_1$$ be endpoint rotations. A common geodesic interpolation is

$$
R_t=R_0\exp\!\left(t\log(R_0^\top R_1)\right).
$$

Every intermediate matrix remains a rotation. The logarithm chooses a tangent generator for the relative rotation, so its branch convention matters near an angle of $$\pi$$ just as the spherical logarithm becomes ambiguous at antipodes. Closed form does not eliminate global geometry; it makes the region of validity explicit.

Inference replaces coordinate addition with an exponential-map step:

$$
x_{k+1}=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_k}
\left(\Delta t\,u_{t_k}(x_k)\right).
$$

For diffusion, a tangent Gaussian increment is combined with the drift in $$T_{x_k}\mathcal M$$ and then mapped back by Exp. Thus the state remains on the manifold after every step.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s47-manifold-inference.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential map updates for manifold ODE and diffusion simulation." caption="Manifold solvers take tangent-space increments and return to the state space with the exponential map." %}

In implementations, Exp is sometimes replaced by a cheaper retraction $$\mathcal R_x(v)$$ satisfying $$\mathcal R_x(0)=x$$ and $$D\mathcal R_x(0)=I$$ on the tangent space. Normalizing $$x+v$$ is a standard sphere retraction. It agrees with Exp to first order but follows a different finite path. This can be a good engineering tradeoff, provided the article or experiment states the actual update. Calling every retraction step an “exponential-map solver” hides an approximation that can matter at large step sizes.

The quarter-circle example quantifies that approximation. Start at $$p=(1,0,0)$$ with tangent velocity $$v=(0,1,0)$$ and take a step of size $$h=0.5$$. The exponential update is

$$
x_{\mathrm{Exp}}
=(\cos0.5,\sin0.5,0)
\approx(0.8776,0.4794,0),
$$

whereas normalized Euler gives

$$
x_{\mathrm{ret}}
=\frac{(1,0.5,0)}{\sqrt{1.25}}
\approx(0.8944,0.4472,0).
$$

Both points lie on the sphere, but their angular positions are $$0.5$$ and $$\arctan(0.5)\approx0.4636$$ radians. The local error is small for a small step and accumulates across a long integration. Halving the step usually reduces it; changing the learned field, metric, or terminal time can change it in a different way. Solver tolerance should therefore be varied independently of model quality when reporting sampling error.

Parallel transport creates a related numerical obligation whenever a method compares velocities at different states. On a curved manifold, $$u(x_k)$$ and $$u(x_{k+1})$$ live in different tangent spaces. Adding or averaging their coordinate vectors without transport is chart-dependent. First-order Euler avoids that comparison, but higher-order integrators, momentum methods, and multistep solvers must specify how tangent information is moved between base points. The geometric operator dictionary is thus not only for deriving a loss; it determines which standard numerical algorithms remain meaningful.

## Scores and likelihoods on manifolds

A score-based model needs the gradient of a log density, but an ordinary coordinate gradient does not automatically transform as the desired tangent direction. The manifold score is the Riemannian gradient $$\operatorname{grad}_g\log p_t(x)$$.


It is characterized by

$$
g_p(\operatorname{grad}_g f,v)=df_p(v)
\qquad\text{for every }v\in T_p\mathcal M.
$$

In local coordinates with metric matrix $$G(p)$$,

$$
\operatorname{grad}_g f(p)=G(p)^{-1}\nabla f(p).
$$

The inverse metric converts the coordinate differential into the tangent vector representing steepest ascent under $$g$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s50-riemannian-gradient.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Definition and coordinate expression of the Riemannian gradient." caption="The Riemannian gradient is metric-dual to the differential and equals the inverse metric times the coordinate gradient." %}

Continuous normalizing flows also require density evolution along the ODE. In Euclidean space the instantaneous log-density change is the negative divergence of the velocity. On a manifold, the same statement uses Riemannian divergence.


For local coordinates $$x^1,\ldots,x^d$$ and $$|G|=\det G$$,

$$
\operatorname{div}_g u
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}
\sum_{i=1}^d
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}
\left(\sqrt{|G|}\,u^i\right).
$$

The volume factor $$\sqrt{|G|}$$ corrects coordinate expansion for the Riemannian volume element.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s52-riemannian-divergence.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Local coordinate formula for Riemannian divergence." caption="The determinant of the metric accounts for how coordinate volume changes across the manifold." %}

On the unit sphere in longitude--latitude coordinates, the metric matrix is

$$
G(\lambda,\varphi)
=\begin{bmatrix}
\cos^2\varphi&0\\
0&1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\sqrt{|G|}=|\cos\varphi|.
$$

Away from the poles, a coordinate field $$u=(u^\lambda,u^\varphi)$$ therefore has

$$
\operatorname{div}_g u
=\frac{1}{\cos\varphi}
\left[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}
  (\cos\varphi\,u^\lambda)
+\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}
  (\cos\varphi\,u^\varphi)
\right].
$$

Using only $$\partial_\lambda u^\lambda+\partial_\varphi u^\varphi$$ would ignore how equal coordinate rectangles represent different surface areas at different latitudes. The divergence correction is exactly what turns coordinate density change into density change with respect to the sphere's area measure.

The lecture's final comparison makes the Euclidean-to-manifold replacement dictionary explicit. Euclidean conditional flow matching draws $$x_0$$ from a Gaussian, uses a straight interpolation, and targets $$x_1-x_0$$. Geometric flow matching chooses a base distribution on $$\mathcal M$$, follows a geodesic,

$$
x_t=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}
\left(t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(x_1)\right),
$$

and targets the tangent displacement $$\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_1)/(1-t)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s53-euclidean-geometric-summary.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Summary comparison of Euclidean and geometric conditional flow matching." caption="Straight interpolation and subtraction become a geodesic path and a logarithmic-map velocity." %}

Likelihood follows the same flow while accumulating Riemannian divergence:

$$
\log p_1(x_1)
=\log p_0(x_0)-\int_0^1\operatorname{div}_g u_t(x_t)\,dt.
$$

The full algorithm is therefore not Euclidean flow matching with renamed symbols. Its base distribution, velocities, loss norm, solver, score, and density evolution must all respect the same manifold and metric.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/s54-likelihood-summary.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Geometric flow matching likelihood and Riemannian divergence summary." caption="Geometric inference integrates a tangent ODE and a Riemannian divergence correction along the same path." %}

A reproducible geometric-flow model should consequently state a compact but complete contract:

1. the state manifold and whether it is represented intrinsically or extrinsically;
2. the Riemannian metric and base measure used for norms and densities;
3. the source distribution and source--target coupling;
4. the conditional path, including Exp/Log branch or cut-locus conventions;
5. how the network output is made tangent;
6. the numerical update—exact Exp, a named retraction, or a chart-based solver;
7. the divergence estimator and volume measure if likelihood is reported.

These choices are not implementation footnotes. They define the path law, the regression target, and the probability density being learned. Once they are explicit, Euclidean flow matching reappears as the special case $$\mathcal M=\mathbb R^d$$, $$G=I$$, $$\operatorname{Exp}_x(v)=x+v$$, and $$\operatorname{Log}_x(y)=y-x$$. The geometric formulation is valuable precisely because it shows which parts of that familiar recipe were structural assumptions all along.

The resulting perspective is deliberately conservative. A manifold does not guarantee a better generator, and an exact geometric operator does not compensate for a poor coupling or an inaccurate field. Geometry removes invalid states and coordinate artifacts from the formulation; statistics and optimization still determine whether the learned transport matches the data. The right comparison therefore holds the data and task fixed, varies the geometric choices explicitly, and reports both distributional quality and numerical error. That is the practical conclusion of the lecture: replace every Euclidean primitive that the state space does not support, but do not attribute to geometry improvements that actually come from a different model, coupling, dataset, or solver budget.
