## Flow matching before geometry

Flow matching begins with a time-dependent velocity field and the ODE

$$
\frac{dX_t}{dt}=u_t(X_t).
$$

Its solution defines a one-to-one flow map $$X_t=\psi_t(X_0)$$. If $$X_0$$ follows the base density $$p_0$$, then the push-forward $$(\psi_t)_\#p_0$$ is the marginal density $$p_t$$. This is the deterministic analogue of specifying an SDE and following its probability law, but the endpoint convention used here takes $$t=0$$ as noise and $$t=1$$ as data.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-5.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow matching ODE, flow map, and push-forward probability path." caption="A velocity field generates a flow map, and the flow map pushes the base density along a probability path. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 5." %}

The marginal path is usually introduced through tractable conditional paths. For a data endpoint $$x_1$$,

$$
p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid x_1)p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_1)\,dx_1,
$$

where every $$p_t(\cdot\mid x_1)$$ is generated by a conditional velocity $$u_t(\cdot\mid x_1)$$. A familiar Euclidean choice is the Gaussian path induced by $$X_t=(1-t)X_0+t x_1$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-6.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Conditional probability paths marginalized into a flow matching probability path." caption="Tractable endpoint-conditioned paths marginalize to the target probability path. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 6." %}

Four objects must remain distinct. Conditional velocities generate conditional paths. Marginalizing the conditional paths gives the marginal path. The marginal velocity that generates this path is a posterior average,

$$
u_t(x)=\int u_t(x\mid x_1)p(x_1\mid X_t=x)\,dx_1,
$$

and is generally intractable even when its conditional ingredients are easy to sample.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Relationship between conditional and marginal velocity fields and probability paths." caption="The tractable conditional path and velocity sit below their intractable marginal counterparts. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 7." %}

Direct regression to the marginal velocity is therefore unavailable. Conditional flow matching replaces it with

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert u_\theta(X_t,t)-u_t(X_t\mid X_1)\right\rVert^2
\right].
$$

The conditional and marginal squared-error losses have the same parameter gradient. Sampling the condition makes the target tractable while preserving the optimization problem that matters.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Marginal flow matching and conditional flow matching losses." caption="Conditional velocity regression avoids evaluating the posterior-averaged marginal velocity. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 8." %}

The condition need not be only the data endpoint. It can include the source and endpoint together, or any variable $$z$$ that indexes tractable conditional paths whose mixture is the desired marginal path. This flexibility becomes useful once a geometry admits several ways to define a conditional transport.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-9.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow matching conditioned on an endpoint, source, or source endpoint pair." caption="Flow matching permits richer conditions than a single endpoint, provided their conditional paths marginalize correctly. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 9." %}

## Why generative models need manifolds

Many scientific objects do not naturally inhabit one unconstrained Euclidean vector space. Protein conformations have bond and rotational constraints; molecular conformers mix Cartesian and periodic variables; probability distributions lie on a simplex; Earth coordinates lie on a sphere; rotations form $$\mathrm{SO}(3)$$. The lecture places these examples side by side because “non-Euclidean” is not one data type but a recurring structural condition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Scientific examples of non Euclidean data including proteins, conformations, probability distributions, and Earth coordinates." caption="Scientific data often obey geometric constraints that ordinary vector addition ignores. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 11." %}

The same issue appears in three-dimensional surfaces, latent manifolds, and datasets whose meaningful variations occupy a lower-dimensional subset of the ambient representation. A model can store such an object in Euclidean coordinates without making its valid state space Euclidean.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Rabbit surface, latent imaging manifold, and data driven manifold examples." caption="Extrinsic coordinates may contain the data while the admissible configurations remain a curved manifold. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 12." %}

This breaks the most convenient Euclidean flow-matching recipe in two places. There is no universal standard Gaussian intrinsic to an arbitrary manifold, and the affine interpolation

$$
(1-t)x_0+t x_1
$$

uses addition and scalar multiplication that may leave the state space. A learned velocity must also be a legitimate direction at its current point rather than an arbitrary ambient vector.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean flow matching ingredients that fail on a manifold." caption="Both the conditional path and the notion of velocity must be rebuilt when the state space lacks vector-space operations. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 13." %}

The replacement starts by adding geometric structure in stages. A smooth manifold supplies a global differentiable space. A Riemannian metric equips each tangent space with an inner product. A velocity field assigns a tangent vector to every point, and integrating it produces a flow that remains on the manifold.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Smooth manifold as the first geometric structure." caption="A smooth manifold supplies the global differentiable state space. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 14." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Riemannian metric added to a smooth manifold." caption="A smoothly varying inner product turns the smooth manifold into a Riemannian manifold. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 15." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-16.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent velocity field and flow on a Riemannian manifold." caption="Differentiating a flow gives a tangent velocity, while solving the ODE recovers the flow. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 16." %}

Straight lines are replaced by geodesics. On manifolds with tractable geometry—Euclidean space, spheres, tori, and some hyperbolic spaces—the conditional flow can be written with the exponential and logarithmic maps. For endpoints $$x_0,x_1\in\mathcal M$$,

$$
\psi_t(x_0\mid x_1)
=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}\!\left(t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(x_1)\right).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-17.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Geodesic conditional path replacing a Euclidean straight line." caption="Geodesics provide the manifold analogue of straight conditional paths. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 17." %}

The Euclidean norm in the regression loss must change as well. If the model and target velocities lie in $$T_{X_t}\mathcal M$$, their discrepancy is measured with the metric at $$X_t$$:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RCFM}}(\theta)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\lVert u_t^\theta(X_t)-u_t(X_t\mid X_1)\right\rVert_{g}^2
\right].
$$

The marginal and conditional Riemannian flow-matching losses retain the same gradient-equivalence principle.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-18.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Riemannian flow matching and conditional flow matching losses." caption="The regression target is tangent-valued and its norm is evaluated with the Riemannian metric. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 18." %}

## Smooth manifolds and coordinate representations

A smooth manifold $$\mathcal M$$ is globally allowed to curve or wrap, but locally resembles $$\mathbb R^d$$. A point belongs to the abstract space itself, not automatically to one privileged coordinate system.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Curved manifold locally mapped to Euclidean patches." caption="A manifold can be globally non-Euclidean while every sufficiently small patch looks Euclidean. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 19." %}

A chart $$(U_i,\phi_i)$$ maps a patch $$U_i\subset\mathcal M$$ to coordinates in $$\mathbb R^d$$. An atlas is a collection of such charts that covers the manifold. Smoothness means that overlapping charts agree through smooth transition maps rather than through identical coordinates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Coordinate chart mapping a manifold patch into Euclidean space." caption="Charts create local Euclidean coordinates without flattening the entire manifold at once. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 20." %}

If $$U_i\cap U_j$$ is nonempty, the coordinate change

$$
\phi_j\circ\phi_i^{-1}
$$

must be smooth on the overlap. This compatibility is what makes differentiation independent of the chosen chart.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-21.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Compatible transition map between overlapping manifold charts." caption="Smooth transition functions stitch local coordinate patches into one differentiable space. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 21." %}

There are two common computational representations. An extrinsic representation embeds $$\mathcal M$$ in a higher-dimensional ambient space through an inclusion $$\iota:\mathcal M\hookrightarrow\mathbb R^n$$. The unit sphere, for example, is a two-dimensional manifold embedded in $$\mathbb R^3$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-22.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sphere represented extrinsically as a manifold embedded in three dimensions." caption="An extrinsic representation stores manifold points in an ambient Euclidean space with constraints. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 22." %}

An intrinsic representation instead works in chart coordinates. Stereographic projection maps most of a sphere to a plane, letting ordinary coordinate computations represent local manifold operations. The convenience is local: the excluded pole and chart transitions still matter.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Stereographic projection as intrinsic local coordinates on a sphere." caption="Intrinsic coordinates move computation into a chart, but no single chart must cover every point. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 23." %}

Some manifolds do admit familiar global coordinates over almost all of the space. Spherical coordinates use radius, azimuth, and polar angle, although their singularities and periodic identifications show why a coordinate tuple is not the same thing as the geometry itself.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Spherical coordinate system as a global coordinate choice with singularities." caption="Global coordinates can be useful while still carrying singularities and identifications. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 24." %}

## Tangent vectors and velocity fields

A curve $$\gamma:\mathbb R\to\mathcal M$$ gives a valid motion through the manifold. In Euclidean space its tangent is the difference quotient of coordinates. On an abstract manifold, however, subtraction such as $$\gamma(h)-\gamma(0)$$ is not intrinsically defined.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Curve on a manifold and tangent direction at a point." caption="A curve supplies the intuition for direction even when coordinate subtraction is unavailable. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 26." %}

The coordinate-free definition looks at how the curve differentiates every smooth test function $$f:\mathcal M\to\mathbb R$$:

$$
X_{\gamma,p}f
=\left.\frac{d}{dt}(f\circ\gamma)(t)\right|_{t=0},
\qquad p=\gamma(0).
$$

Two curves define the same tangent vector when they produce the same directional derivative on every smooth test function. All such tangent vectors at $$p$$ form the tangent space $$T_p\mathcal M$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent vector defined as a directional derivative operator." caption="A tangent vector records infinitesimal change through its action on smooth functions. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 27." %}

Charts provide a basis. If $$x^1,\ldots,x^d$$ are local coordinates, then every tangent vector can be written

$$
v=\sum_{i=1}^d v^i\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\bigg|_p.
$$

The coefficients depend on the chart, while the tangent vector does not. The derivation follows by inserting $$\phi^{-1}\circ\phi$$ into the curve and applying the ordinary chain rule in $$\mathbb R^d$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-28.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Coordinate tangent basis obtained from manifold chart directions." caption="Coordinate curves induce a tangent basis, and the chain rule decomposes any tangent into that basis. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 28." %}

With an extrinsic representation, the differential of the inclusion maps intrinsic tangent coordinates into the ambient space. For the sphere embedded in $$\mathbb R^3$$, the result is the two-dimensional plane orthogonal to the radius vector.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-29.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent plane of an embedded sphere in ambient coordinates." caption="The inclusion map represents intrinsic tangent vectors as constrained ambient vectors. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 29." %}

This yields three model-design choices. A network can output coordinates in a local tangent basis. It can output an ambient vector already constrained to the tangent space. Or it can output an unconstrained ambient vector $$\nu(x)$$ and project it:

$$
u(x)=\Pi_x\nu(x)\in T_x\mathcal M.
$$

For the unit sphere, $$\Pi_x=I-xx^\top$$. The projection is not decorative; without it, a numerical step generally leaves the manifold.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-30.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Local coordinate, ambient, and projected designs for manifold velocity fields." caption="Velocity parameterizations differ in where they enforce membership in the tangent space. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 30." %}

## Riemannian metrics, distances, and geodesics

A tangent space supplies directions but not their lengths. A Riemannian metric assigns a smoothly varying inner product $$g_p$$ to each $$T_p\mathcal M$$. On a sphere, the inherited ambient dot product is one natural choice, but a metric is extra structure rather than a consequence of smoothness alone.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-32.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tangent space and Riemannian metric on a sphere." caption="Choosing an inner product on every tangent space adds measurable geometry to the manifold. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 32." %}

Different manifolds carry different natural metrics. Euclidean space uses the identity matrix, the sphere uses the ambient inner product restricted to its tangent plane, and hyperbolic space uses a position-dependent metric. A smooth manifold equipped with this choice is a Riemannian manifold $$(\mathcal M,g)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-33.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean sphere and hyperbolic examples of Riemannian metrics." caption="The metric, not merely the point set, determines lengths and angles on a Riemannian manifold. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 33." %}

In local coordinates, the metric is represented by a positive-definite matrix $$G(p)$$:

$$
\langle u,v\rangle_{g,p}=u^\top G(p)v.
$$

This “Riemannian metric” should not be confused with a metric-space distance function. It is a local inner product from which lengths, angles, and eventually distances are derived.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-34.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Metric tensor matrix defining tangent vector inner products." caption="A positive-definite metric tensor converts tangent coordinates into geometric inner products. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 34." %}

The corresponding norm and angle are

$$
\lVert v\rVert_{g,p}=\sqrt{v^\top G(p)v},
\qquad
\cos\theta=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle_{g,p}}
{\lVert u\rVert_{g,p}\lVert v\rVert_{g,p}}.
$$

Unlike a fixed Euclidean dot product, these quantities may change with the base point even when the coordinate coefficients do not.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-35.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Vector lengths and angles computed by a Riemannian metric." caption="The metric is the computational device for tangent norms and angles. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 35." %}

To compare two points $$p,q\in\mathcal M$$, first consider a smooth curve $$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal M$$ joining them. Its instantaneous speed is the metric norm of $$\dot\gamma(t)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Curve connecting two points on a sphere with tangent velocity." caption="Distance begins with the length of a valid curve connecting the two manifold points. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 36." %}

The curve length and induced geodesic distance are

$$
L_g(\gamma)=\int_0^1
\sqrt{\dot\gamma(t)^\top G(\gamma(t))\dot\gamma(t)}\,dt,
$$

$$
d_g(p,q)=\inf_{\gamma:p\to q}L_g(\gamma).
$$

A shortest path is a geodesic. Geodesics can also be characterized as stationary curves of the energy functional, which is why they replace straight lines in conditional transport.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-37.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Curve length integral and shortest geodesic distance." caption="Integrating tangent speed gives curve length; minimizing over curves gives geodesic distance. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 37." %}

Changing the metric changes the shortest path even when the underlying point set is unchanged. On a probability simplex, a Euclidean metric produces linear segments, while the Fisher--Rao metric measures motion according to the information carried by changes in the distribution.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-38.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Different geodesics on a probability simplex under Euclidean and Fisher Rao metrics." caption="Geodesics belong to the pair of manifold and metric, not to the manifold alone. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 38." %}

Distances make geometric training objectives possible. Flow matching compares tangent velocities; diffusion objectives compare tangent scores; both can use geodesic distance to construct conditional paths. But an objective is only half of a generative model: inference still needs a way to take numerical steps that stay on $$\mathcal M$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-39.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow and diffusion losses expressed with manifold distances." caption="A Riemannian distance turns Euclidean flow and diffusion losses into geometry-aware objectives. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 39." %}

An Euler update $$x_{t+\Delta t}=x_t+u_t(x_t)\Delta t$$ assumes that points and tangent vectors can be added. Brownian diffusion additionally assumes Euclidean Gaussian increments. Neither operation is intrinsic to a manifold, so ODE and SDE solvers need geometric update maps and manifold-valued noise.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-40.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean ODE and SDE updates contrasted with manifold inference requirements." caption="Manifold inference cannot use unconstrained coordinate addition as its state update. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 40." %}

## Exponential, logarithmic, and transport maps

Three operators replace the missing vector-space primitives. The exponential map moves from a tangent space to the manifold, the logarithmic map moves a nearby manifold point back to a tangent space, and parallel transport compares vectors based at different points.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-42.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Questions answered by exponential logarithmic maps and parallel transport." caption="Exp, Log, and parallel transport provide the basic movement operations absent from a generic manifold. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 42." %}

For $$v\in T_p\mathcal M$$, let $$\gamma_v$$ be the geodesic satisfying $$\gamma_v(0)=p$$ and $$\dot\gamma_v(0)=v$$. The exponential map is

$$
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=\gamma_v(1).
$$

In Euclidean space it reduces exactly to $$p+v$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-43.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential map following a geodesic from a tangent vector to a manifold point." caption="The exponential map is the geometry-aware replacement for adding a displacement to a point. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 43." %}

Locally, the logarithmic map inverts the exponential map:

$$
\operatorname{Log}_p(q)=v
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Exp}_p(v)=q.
$$

It returns the initial tangent of the geodesic from $$p$$ to $$q$$. The inverse is only guaranteed where $$\operatorname{Exp}_p$$ is a diffeomorphism; beyond that neighborhood, several geodesics may connect the same endpoints.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-44.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Logarithmic map from a manifold point to the tangent space at a base point." caption="The logarithmic map expresses a nearby endpoint as a tangent displacement from its base point. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 44." %}

Parallel transport moves $$v\in T_p\mathcal M$$ along a specified curve to a vector in $$T_q\mathcal M$$ while preserving the metric inner product. Along that curve it is reversible and preserves lengths and angles. The curve matters: tangent spaces at different points are distinct vector spaces, so there is no canonical subtraction of their vectors without additional structure.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-45.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Parallel transport of a tangent vector along a curve on a sphere." caption="Parallel transport compares tangent vectors at different base points without leaving the geometry. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 45." %}

These replacements reproduce Euclidean conditional flow matching in geometric form. A straight-line displacement $$(x_1-x_t)/(1-t)$$ becomes

$$
u_t(x_t\mid x_1)
=\frac{\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_1)}{1-t}.
$$

For $$\mathrm{SO}(3)$$, Exp and Log are matrix exponential and logarithm operations, with closed forms available through the rotation angle and skew-symmetric generator.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-46.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Euclidean and SO3 logarithmic map velocities for conditional flow matching." caption="Replacing subtraction by the logarithmic map gives the target velocity on Euclidean space and on rotations. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 46." %}

Inference replaces coordinate addition with an exponential-map step:

$$
x_{k+1}=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_k}
\left(\Delta t\,u_{t_k}(x_k)\right).
$$

For diffusion, a tangent Gaussian increment is combined with the drift in $$T_{x_k}\mathcal M$$ and then mapped back by Exp. Thus the state remains on the manifold after every step.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-47.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential map updates for manifold ODE and diffusion simulation." caption="Manifold solvers take tangent-space increments and return to the state space with the exponential map. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 47." %}

## Scores and likelihoods on manifolds

A score-based model needs the gradient of a log density, but an ordinary coordinate gradient does not automatically transform as the desired tangent direction. The manifold score is the Riemannian gradient $$\operatorname{grad}_g\log p_t(x)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-49.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Need for a Riemannian gradient to compute a manifold score." caption="The score must be defined as a tangent vector using the geometry. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 49." %}

It is characterized by

$$
g_p(\operatorname{grad}_g f,v)=df_p(v)
\qquad\text{for every }v\in T_p\mathcal M.
$$

In local coordinates with metric matrix $$G(p)$$,

$$
\operatorname{grad}_g f(p)=G(p)^{-1}\nabla f(p).
$$

The inverse metric converts the coordinate differential into the tangent vector representing steepest ascent under $$g$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-50.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Definition and coordinate expression of the Riemannian gradient." caption="The Riemannian gradient is metric-dual to the differential and equals the inverse metric times the coordinate gradient. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 50." %}

Continuous normalizing flows also require density evolution along the ODE. In Euclidean space the instantaneous log-density change is the negative divergence of the velocity. On a manifold, the same statement uses Riemannian divergence.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-15/slide-51.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Need for Riemannian divergence to compute manifold log density." caption="Likelihood computation replaces Euclidean divergence with its Riemannian counterpart. Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 51." %}

For local coordinates $$x^1,\ldots,x^d$$ and $$|G|=\det G$$,

$$
\operatorname{div}_g u
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}
\sum_{i=1}^d
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}
\left(\sqrt{|G|}\,u^i\right).
$$

The volume factor $$\sqrt{|G|}$$ corrects coordinate expansion for the Riemannian volume element.

The lecture's final comparison makes the Euclidean-to-manifold replacement dictionary explicit. Euclidean conditional flow matching draws $$x_0$$ from a Gaussian, uses a straight interpolation, and targets $$x_1-x_0$$. Geometric flow matching chooses a base distribution on $$\mathcal M$$, follows a geodesic,

$$
x_t=\operatorname{Exp}_{x_0}
\left(t\operatorname{Log}_{x_0}(x_1)\right),
$$

and targets the tangent displacement $$\operatorname{Log}_{x_t}(x_1)/(1-t)$$.

Cropped from the 2025 GDL geometric-flow-matching lecture, slide 53." %}

Likelihood follows the same flow while accumulating Riemannian divergence:

$$
\log p_1(x_1)
=\log p_0(x_0)-\int_0^1\operatorname{div}_g u_t(x_t)\,dt.
$$

The full algorithm is therefore not Euclidean flow matching with renamed symbols. Its base distribution, velocities, loss norm, solver, score, and density evolution must all respect the same manifold and metric.
