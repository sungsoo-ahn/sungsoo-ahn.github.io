Scalar- and vector-based geometric GNNs already manipulate features with known transformation laws. A scalar does not change when the input is rotated. A vector is multiplied by the same rotation matrix as the coordinates. The next step is not to abandon those features, but to recognize them as the first two members of a larger type system.

That type system is the subject of this lecture. It lets a network carry type-$$0$$, type-$$1$$, up to type-$$L$$ features at every point, and it tells us exactly which linear maps, products, and nonlinearities preserve equivariance. The progression is cumulative: representations define steerability; irreducible representations define types; spherical harmonics realize those types on directions; Clebsch--Gordan coefficients couple them; Tensor Field Networks turn the couplings into message passing; and NequIP extends the bookkeeping from rotations to reflections.

## From scalars and vectors to steerable feature types

Suppose a three-dimensional rotation $$g$$ is represented on coordinates by $$R(g)\in SO(3)$$. A scalar feature $$s$$ and vector feature $$v$$ transform as

$$
s\longmapsto s,
\qquad
v\longmapsto R(g)v.
$$

Both are **steerable**: once we know how the input has moved, a fixed linear rule tells us how to move the feature. Scalars and ordinary vectors are therefore called type-$$0$$ and type-$$1$$ steerable features. A steerable network generalizes this idea by maintaining channels of type $$l=0,1,\ldots,L$$.

This immediately creates the three questions that organize the lecture:

1. What is a steerable feature? We need the language of **group representations**.
2. What distinguishes type $$l$$ from type $$l'$$? We need **irreducible representations** and the Wigner $$D$$ matrices.
3. How can two typed features be combined into another typed feature? We need the **Clebsch--Gordan tensor product**.

The lecture drew its representation-theory perspective from Cohen and Welling's [Steerable CNNs](https://openreview.net/forum?id=rJQKYt5ll), Duval et al.'s [Hitchhiker's Guide to Geometric GNNs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511), Sophia Tang's [guide to spherical-equivariant graph transformers](https://alchemybio.substack.com/p/spherical-equivariant-graph-transformer), and Erik Bekkers's [group-equivariant deep-learning lectures](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB4zsnLnZfo). We will use only as much group theory as is needed to reconstruct the network.

## Groups, actions, and representations

A **group** $$(G,\cdot)$$ is a set of transformations equipped with a product that satisfies four axioms:

- **Closure:** $$g\cdot h\in G$$ for every $$g,h\in G$$.
- **Associativity:** $$(g\cdot h)\cdot i=g\cdot(h\cdot i)$$.
- **Identity:** there is an element $$e$$ such that $$e\cdot g=g\cdot e=g$$.
- **Inverse:** every $$g$$ has an element $$g^{-1}$$ such that $$g^{-1}g=gg^{-1}=e$$.

These rules describe how transformations compose before specifying what they transform. For geometric learning, the central examples are matrix groups. The orthogonal group is

$$
O(n)=\{R\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}:R^\top R=I\}.
$$

Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles. Their determinants are $$+1$$ or $$-1$$, so $$O(n)$$ contains both rotations and reflections. The **special orthogonal group**

$$
SO(n)=\{R\in O(n):\det R=1\}
$$

contains only orientation-preserving rotations. In two and three dimensions we therefore speak of the planar and spatial rotation groups $$SO(2)$$ and $$SO(3)$$.

### A group must act on something

An abstract group becomes geometrically meaningful through a **group action**. A left action on a domain $$X$$ maps $$(g,x)$$ to $$g\cdot x$$ and satisfies

$$
g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x,
\qquad
e\cdot x=x.
$$

For a point cloud $$X=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$$, a rotation acts on every point with the same matrix:

$$
g\cdot X=(R(g)x_1,\ldots,R(g)x_N).
$$

The compatibility condition matters. Rotating by $$h$$ and then by $$g$$ must produce exactly the same configuration as applying the composed group element $$gh$$ once.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s11-group-action-composition.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A point x acted on successively by group elements g prime and g, ending at the same point as the product action." caption="The two paths express the compatibility condition <i>g</i> · (<i>g</i>' · <i>x</i>) = (<i>gg</i>') · <i>x</i>. The exact illustration source is unresolved." %}

For a numerical check, take $$x=(1,0)^\top$$ and let $$g_{\pi/2}$$ be a counterclockwise quarter turn. One action gives

$$
g_{\pi/2}\cdot x=
\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Applying the same action again gives $$(-1,0)^\top$$. The product $$g_{\pi/2}g_{\pi/2}=g_\pi$$ also sends $$(1,0)^\top$$ directly to $$(-1,0)^\top$$. The equality is elementary, but it distinguishes a genuine action from an arbitrary family of transformations: the data transformation must respect the multiplication table of the group.

### Representations are linear actions on features

A **group representation** $$\rho$$ maps each group element to an invertible linear operator on a vector space $$V$$:

$$
\rho:G\longrightarrow GL(V),
\qquad
v\longmapsto \rho(g)v.
$$

It must preserve the group product and identity,

$$
\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h),
\qquad
\rho(e)=I.
$$

A vector $$v\in V$$ with this known transformation rule is a **steerable feature vector**. The word “vector” here refers to an element of a representation space, not necessarily an ordinary Cartesian arrow. Its dimension and transformation matrix depend on the representation.

### Circular harmonics: the first concrete example

For $$SO(2)$$, write a rotation by angle $$\alpha$$ as $$g_\alpha$$. Degree-$$k$$ circular harmonics form the two-component vector

$$
v_k(\theta)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos(k\theta)\\
\sin(k\theta)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Shifting the input angle by $$\alpha$$ gives

$$
v_k(\theta+\alpha)=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos(k\alpha)&-\sin(k\alpha)\\
\sin(k\alpha)&\cos(k\alpha)
\end{bmatrix}}_{\rho_k(g_\alpha)}
v_k(\theta).
$$

The spatial frequency and feature transformation are tied together: a degree-$$k$$ pattern completes $$k$$ oscillations around the circle and its coefficient vector rotates through angle $$k\alpha$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s14-circular-harmonics.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Constant, degree one, degree two, and degree three circular harmonic curves." caption="The lecture's native circular-harmonic plot. Increasing the degree increases angular frequency; each pair of sine and cosine curves is closed under planar rotations." %}

The lecture posed a small diagnostic question: how does the area spanned by the two components change under rotation? It does not. Since

$$
\det \rho_k(g_\alpha)
=\cos^2(k\alpha)+\sin^2(k\alpha)=1,
$$

the representation is itself a rotation matrix. It preserves inner products, lengths, oriented area, and the two-dimensional subspace generated by the harmonic pair. This closure under the group action is the property we will generalize to three dimensions.

## Spherical harmonics as steerable vectors

Spherical harmonics are the three-dimensional counterpart of circular harmonics. They form an orthonormal basis for square-integrable functions on the unit sphere. Each basis function is indexed by a nonnegative degree $$l$$ and an order $$m\in\{-l,\ldots,l\}$$. In a complex convention,

$$
Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)
=N_{lm}e^{im\phi}P_l^m(\cos\theta),
$$

where $$N_{lm}$$ is a normalization constant and $$P_l^m$$ is an associated Legendre polynomial. For fixed $$l$$ there are $$2l+1$$ orders. Larger $$l$$ means finer angular variation.

The same functions appear as angular eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. On the unit sphere,

$$
\Delta_{S^2}Y_l^m=-l(l+1)Y_l^m.
$$

This is why they are the natural Fourier basis for directional information.

The exact origin of this basis chart is unresolved." %}

### One degree is one steerable vector

Collect all orders of one degree into

$$
Y^{(l)}(\hat r)=
\begin{bmatrix}
Y_l^{-l}(\hat r)&\cdots&Y_l^{l}(\hat r)
\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}.
$$

When the direction is rotated by $$g\in SO(3)$$, the components mix through the degree-$$l$$ Wigner matrix:

$$
Y^{(l)}(g\cdot\hat r)
=D^{(l)}(g)Y^{(l)}(\hat r).
$$

The matrix $$D^{(l)}(g)$$ is a $$(2l+1)\times(2l+1)$$ representation of $$SO(3)$$. Rotation may change every individual $$m$$ component, but it never mixes degree $$l$$ with a different degree. The complete degree-$$l$$ vector is therefore a closed steerable feature.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s16-spherical-harmonic-steerability.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Spherical harmonic coefficient vectors before and after rotation, related by a Wigner D matrix." caption="Rotating the direction changes the whole coefficient vector through a fixed Wigner <i>D</i> matrix. See the steerable-filter construction in <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Thomas et al.</a>" %}

For example, the degree-two vector has five entries,

$$
Y^{(2)}(\hat r)=
\begin{bmatrix}
Y_2^{-2}(\hat r)&Y_2^{-1}(\hat r)&Y_2^0(\hat r)&Y_2^1(\hat r)&Y_2^2(\hat r)
\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}.
$$

A generic rotation mixes all five entries through $$D^{(2)}(g)$$. Selecting only $$Y_2^0$$ would not produce a closed feature type: after rotation, the result generally contains the other four orders. This is why a steerable network must store the complete $$m=-l,\ldots,l$$ block and apply channel operations uniformly across its representation indices.

At $$l=0$$, there is one constant component: a scalar. At $$l=1$$, the real spherical harmonics are proportional to the Cartesian coordinate functions on the unit sphere. Up to normalization and basis ordering,

$$
Y^{(1)}(\hat r)\propto
\begin{bmatrix}
x/r\\y/r\\z/r
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The ordinary three-dimensional vector representation is thus the degree-one member of the same family. Higher degrees extend the angular resolution without losing a known transformation law.

### Radial information and angular information separate

For an edge $$i\leftarrow j$$ in a geometric graph, write

$$
r_{ij}=x_j-x_i,
\qquad
r_{ij}=\lVert r_{ij}\rVert,
\qquad
\hat r_{ij}=r_{ij}/r_{ij}.
$$

A radial function can depend on the invariant distance $$r_{ij}$$. A spherical harmonic encodes the direction $$\hat r_{ij}$$ as a type-$$l$$ feature. Their product therefore has learned radial dependence and exact angular steerability. This factorization is the central filter construction in [Tensor Field Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219).

## Cartesian tensors and irreducible representations

The lecture next compares two routes to higher-order steerable features. We may take ordinary outer products of Cartesian vectors, or reorganize the same transformation content into irreducible spherical types. Both routes are valid, but only the second exposes the smallest independently transforming pieces.

Abstractly, a tensor product is the space that turns bilinear maps of two input spaces into ordinary linear maps. Concretely, for vectors $$u,v,w\in\mathbb R^3$$, the first few Cartesian tensors are

$$
u_i,
\qquad
M_{ij}=u_i v_j,
\qquad
T_{ijk}=u_i v_j w_k.
$$

These are outer products, not cross products. Their number of components grows as $$3^k$$ with tensor order $$k$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s20-cartesian-tensor-orders.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A scalar, vector, matrix, and higher tensor represented as arrays with increasing tensor order." caption="The lecture's native Cartesian-tensor illustration, following the organization used in Duval et al.'s <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511'>Hitchhiker's Guide</a>. Tensor order counts Cartesian indices, not irreducible rotation type." %}

Under a rotation $$R$$, one matrix and one rank-three tensor transform as

$$
M'_{ij}=\sum_{a,b}R_{ia}R_{jb}M_{ab},
\qquad
T'_{ijk}=\sum_{a,b,c}R_{ia}R_{jb}R_{kc}T_{abc}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
M'=RMR^\top,
\qquad
\operatorname{vec}(M')=(R\otimes R)\operatorname{vec}(M).
$$

The raw Cartesian tensor is steerable, but it is not irreducible.

### A rank-two tensor already mixes three types

Any $$3\times3$$ matrix decomposes uniquely into its trace, antisymmetric part, and symmetric traceless part:

$$
M=
\underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{tr}M}{3}I}_{\text{one scalar component}}
+\underbrace{\frac{M-M^\top}{2}}_{\text{three antisymmetric components}}
+\underbrace{\left(\frac{M+M^\top}{2}-\frac{\operatorname{tr}M}{3}I\right)}_{\text{five symmetric-traceless components}}.
$$

The dimensions add up as $$9=1+3+5$$. Under rotation, these pieces behave as type $$0$$, type $$1$$, and type $$2$$ features. The antisymmetric matrix is equivalent to a pseudovector; the symmetric traceless tensor is the genuinely new type.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s22-rank-two-decomposition.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A rank-two Cartesian tensor split into trace, antisymmetric, and traceless symmetric parts and rearranged by a basis change." caption="The nine Cartesian entries reorganize into scalar, antisymmetric, and symmetric-traceless components of dimensions 1, 3, and 5. See Duval et al.'s <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511'>Cartesian and spherical tensor discussion</a>." %}

Take the concrete outer product $$u=e_x$$ and $$v=e_y$$. Then

$$
M=uv^\top=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its trace is zero. The remaining entries split equally between

$$
\frac{M-M^\top}{2}=
\frac12
\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\frac{M+M^\top}{2}=
\frac12
\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The first matrix is equivalent to an axial vector along $$z$$; the second is a type-$$2$$ symmetric-traceless feature. Even this sparse outer product therefore contains two different rotational responses. A layer that calls all nine entries simply “rank two” hides information that the irrep basis makes explicit.

### Equivalent and reducible representations

Two representations $$\rho_A$$ and $$\rho_B$$ are **equivalent** when an invertible change of basis $$Q$$ relates them:

$$
\rho_B(g)=Q^{-1}\rho_A(g)Q.
$$

They contain the same symmetry information in different coordinates. A representation is **reducible** if one basis makes every representation matrix block diagonal,

$$
\rho(g)=Q^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}
\rho_1(g)&0\\
0&\rho_2(g)
\end{bmatrix}Q.
$$

An **irreducible representation**, or irrep, has no nontrivial invariant subspace and cannot be decomposed further. Irreps therefore provide the compact labels for steerable feature types.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s24-representation-bases.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="The same three-dimensional rotation expressed in a Cartesian basis and in a spherical basis." caption="Similar matrices describe one representation in different coordinates; a suitable basis also exposes irreducible blocks. The exact illustration source is unresolved." %}

For $$SO(2)$$, the circular-harmonic frequency blocks are the irreducible building blocks. For $$SO(3)$$, the irreps are the Wigner matrices $$D^{(l)}$$, one for every nonnegative integer $$l$$. A finite representation can be written as a direct sum

$$
\rho(g)\simeq
\bigoplus_{l=0}^{L}
\left(I_{C_l}\otimes D^{(l)}(g)\right),
$$

where $$C_l$$ is the number of learnable channels, or copies, of type $$l$$. Each copy has $$2l+1$$ representation components indexed by $$m=-l,\ldots,l$$.

The practical vocabulary now becomes precise:

- type $$0$$: one-dimensional scalars;
- type $$1$$: three-dimensional Cartesian vectors, up to basis convention;
- type $$2$$: five-dimensional symmetric-traceless content;
- type $$l$$: a $$(2l+1)$$-component feature transformed by $$D^{(l)}$$.

Spherical harmonics are concrete functions realizing these abstract blocks:

$$
Y^{(l)}(g\hat r)=D^{(l)}(g)Y^{(l)}(\hat r).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s29-wigner-harmonic-equivariance.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A spherical harmonic rotated in input space and in coefficient space through a Wigner D matrix." caption="The lecture's native Wigner--harmonic commuting diagram. Transforming the direction first or applying the Wigner matrix to its coefficients gives the same result." %}

## Clebsch--Gordan tensor products

Irreps categorize features, but a network must also let features interact. The ordinary tensor product of a type-$$l_1$$ vector and a type-$$l_2$$ vector transforms with the Kronecker-product representation

$$
v^{(l_1)}\otimes v^{(l_2)}
\longmapsto
\left(D^{(l_1)}(g)\otimes D^{(l_2)}(g)\right)
\left(v^{(l_1)}\otimes v^{(l_2)}\right).
$$

That representation is usually reducible. For $$SO(3)$$ it decomposes as

$$
D^{(l_1)}\otimes D^{(l_2)}
\simeq
\bigoplus_{l=|l_1-l_2|}^{l_1+l_2}D^{(l)}.
$$

The Clebsch--Gordan coefficients are the fixed change of basis that performs this block decomposition.

### The indexed coupling rule

For input features $$v^{(l_1)}$$ and $$w^{(l_2)}$$, the type-$$l$$ output has components

$$
\left(v^{(l_1)}\otimes_{\mathrm{CG}}w^{(l_2)}\right)_{m}^{(l)}
=\sum_{m_1=-l_1}^{l_1}
\sum_{m_2=-l_2}^{l_2}
C_{(l_1,m_1)(l_2,m_2)}^{(l,m)}
v_{m_1}^{(l_1)}w_{m_2}^{(l_2)}.
$$

The coefficient tensor $$C$$ is fixed by representation theory. The network learns how channels are weighted before and after this map; it does not learn an arbitrary mixing of the representation indices $$m$$.

Two selection rules make the product sparse. First, only degrees satisfying

$$
|l_1-l_2|\le l\le l_1+l_2
$$

can appear. Second, in the usual complex basis, a coefficient can be nonzero only when $$m=m_1+m_2$$.

The familiar low-order operations are already Clebsch--Gordan products:

<div class="table-responsive" markdown="1">

| Input types | Allowed output | Familiar operation |
|---|---:|---|
| $$0\otimes0$$ | $$0$$ | scalar multiplication |
| $$0\otimes1$$ | $$1$$ | scalar--vector multiplication |
| $$1\otimes1$$ | $$0$$ | dot-product component |
| $$1\otimes1$$ | $$1$$ | cross-product component |
| $$1\otimes1$$ | $$2$$ | symmetric-traceless component |

</div>

Thus the rank-two decomposition $$1+3+5$$ is the same statement as

$$
1\otimes1=0\oplus1\oplus2,
$$

where the symbols now denote irrep types rather than dimensions.

For two ordinary vectors $$u$$ and $$v$$, the three output types can be written directly, up to basis-dependent normalization constants:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{type }0 &: \quad u\cdot v,\\
\text{type }1 &: \quad u\times v,\\
\text{type }2 &: \quad
\frac{uv^\top+vu^\top}{2}
-\frac{u\cdot v}{3}I.
\end{aligned}
$$

The dimensions are again $$1$$, $$3$$, and $$5$$. Clebsch--Gordan coupling is not an exotic operation added on top of familiar vector algebra; it is the systematic version of the dot, cross, and symmetric-traceless decomposition for arbitrary input types.

The visual pattern is useful. Multiplying two angular basis functions creates a more complicated function, but that function decomposes into a finite set of allowed degrees. Clebsch--Gordan coefficients state exactly how much of each output basis element is present.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s38-clebsch-gordan-index-map.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two input representation vectors connected through a Clebsch Gordan coefficient tensor to an output vector." caption="The fixed Clebsch--Gordan tensor links input indices (<i>l</i><sub>1</sub>, <i>m</i><sub>1</sub>) and (<i>l</i><sub>2</sub>, <i>m</i><sub>2</sub>) to an admissible output (<i>l</i>, <i>m</i>). See <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Thomas et al.</a>" %}

This is the decisive architectural move. A layer may combine nonlinear information from two steerable features while guaranteeing that every output is still assigned a valid representation type.

## Tensor Field Networks

[Tensor Field Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219) were among the first neural architectures to operate directly on scalar, vector, and higher-order steerable feature fields. A tensor field associates typed features with each geometric point. The network builds an angular filter from a spherical harmonic, couples it to a hidden feature with a Clebsch--Gordan product, aggregates over neighboring points, and then applies a type-aware nonlinearity.

### The hidden-feature index ledger

Write one hidden feature as

$$
V_{acm}^{(l)}.
$$

Its indices have distinct roles:

- $$a$$ identifies a point or atom;
- $$c$$ identifies a learnable channel among repeated copies of type $$l$$;
- $$l$$ identifies the Wigner representation;
- $$m=-l,\ldots,l$$ indexes the components that must rotate together.

Ordinary neural-network weights may mix the channel index $$c$$. They may not arbitrarily mix the $$m$$ entries, because that would destroy the prescribed Wigner action.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s42-tfn-feature-indices.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tensor Field Network feature dictionary showing point, channel, rotation order, and representation indices." caption="Point, channel, type, and representation indices play different computational roles in a Tensor Field Network. See <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Thomas et al.</a>" %}

### A radial function times a spherical harmonic

For relative position $$r_{ab}=x_b-x_a$$, a filter of angular type $$l_f$$ is factorized as

$$
F_{c m_f m_i}^{(l_f,l_i)}(r_{ab})
=R_{c m_f m_i}^{(l_f,l_i)}(\lVert r_{ab}\rVert)
Y_{m_f}^{(l_f)}(\hat r_{ab}).
$$

The learned radial network $$R$$ depends only on distance and is invariant. The spherical harmonic supplies the exact directional transformation. The filter is therefore a type-$$l_f$$ steerable vector for every pair of channels.

### Typed message passing

To produce an output type $$l_o$$, TFN combines the filter and an input feature with the appropriate Clebsch--Gordan coefficients and sums over neighbors:

$$
\mathcal L_{a c m_o}^{(l_o)}
=
\sum_{b\in\mathcal N(a)}
\sum_{m_f,m_i}
C_{(l_f,m_f)(l_i,m_i)}^{(l_o,m_o)}
F_{c m_f m_i}^{(l_f,l_i)}(r_{ab})
V_{b c m_i}^{(l_i)}.
$$

The triangle rule

$$
|l_f-l_i|\le l_o\le l_f+l_i
$$

determines which filter--input--output triples exist. Aggregation over neighbors is safe because addition commutes with the linear group action.

The equivariance of the layer follows by chaining the three transformation laws. Under a rotation $$g$$,

$$
F^{(l_f)}(g r)=D^{(l_f)}(g)F^{(l_f)}(r),
\qquad
V^{(l_i)}\longmapsto D^{(l_i)}(g)V^{(l_i)}.
$$

The Clebsch--Gordan map is an **intertwiner**. If $$C_{l_f,l_i\to l_o}$$ denotes the projection to output type $$l_o$$, then

$$
C_{l_f,l_i\to l_o}
\left(D^{(l_f)}(g)\otimes D^{(l_i)}(g)\right)
=D^{(l_o)}(g)C_{l_f,l_i\to l_o}.
$$

Therefore each edge message transforms by $$D^{(l_o)}(g)$$. Summing those messages over $$b\in\mathcal N(a)$$ leaves the same matrix outside the sum, so the aggregated output has exactly the requested type. The radial network can be arbitrarily expressive without affecting this proof because its input $$\lVert r_{ab}\rVert$$ is invariant.

### Nonlinearity without losing the type

For $$l=0$$, an ordinary scalar activation is valid:

$$
V_{ac}^{(0)}\longmapsto
\eta^{(0)}\!\left(V_{ac}^{(0)}+b_c^{(0)}\right).
$$

For $$l>0$$, applying an elementwise nonlinearity to the $$m$$ components would generally break equivariance. TFN instead computes the invariant norm

$$
\lVert V_{ac}^{(l)}\rVert
=\left(\sum_{m=-l}^{l}|V_{acm}^{(l)}|^2\right)^{1/2}
$$

and uses a scalar function of that norm to gate the whole irrep vector:

$$
V_{acm}^{(l)}
\longmapsto
\eta^{(l)}\!\left(\lVert V_{ac}^{(l)}\rVert+b_c^{(l)}\right)
\frac{V_{acm}^{(l)}}{\lVert V_{ac}^{(l)}\rVert}.
$$

The magnitude changes, but the direction in representation space follows the same Wigner matrix. Type-wise self-interaction layers provide the analogue of $$1\times1$$ convolutions: they mix channels with weights shared across all $$m$$ components of a given type.

## NequIP and reflection parity

[NequIP](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-29939-5) applies a TFN-like construction to interatomic potentials. Given atomic types and coordinates, it predicts an atomic energy for every atom, sums them into a total potential energy, and obtains conservative forces by differentiation:

$$
E_{\mathrm{pot}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{atoms}}}E_{i,\mathrm{atomic}},
\qquad
F_i=-\nabla_{x_i}E_{\mathrm{pot}}.
$$

The energy must be invariant to translation, rotation, and reflection. The force must transform equivariantly. Rotation degree $$l$$ handles only the proper-rotation subgroup $$SO(3)$$, so NequIP adds a parity label to represent the full orthogonal group $$O(3)$$.

### Parity extends the feature type

A NequIP feature is labeled by $$(l,p)$$, where $$p\in\{+1,-1\}$$ records its response to inversion. An even feature remains unchanged and an odd feature changes sign:

$$
V^{(l,+1)}\longmapsto V^{(l,+1)},
\qquad
V^{(l,-1)}\longmapsto- V^{(l,-1)}.
$$

Rotation degree and parity are independent pieces of metadata. For example, polar and axial vectors both have three components under proper rotations, but behave differently under reflection.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-09/s49-parity-reflection.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A magnetic field and current reflected across a vertical mirror, illustrating odd and even parity." caption="Rotation type alone cannot distinguish polar and axial behavior; parity records the missing reflection law. The exact illustration source is unresolved." %}

The distinction is visible in elementary vector algebra. Under spatial inversion, a displacement is polar and changes sign: $$r\mapsto-r$$. The cross product of two polar vectors does not:

$$
(-u)\times(-v)=u\times v.
$$

It is an axial vector. Both a polar vector and an axial vector look like type $$l=1$$ under proper rotations, yet their parities differ. Spherical harmonics obey

$$
Y^{(l)}(-\hat r)=(-1)^lY^{(l)}(\hat r),
$$

so the angular filter also carries a definite inversion parity.

### The radial--angular filter

NequIP retains the spherical filter

$$
S_m^{(l)}(r_{ij})=R(r_{ij})Y_m^{(l)}(\hat r_{ij}).
$$

The radial function is produced by a neural network acting on a basis of Bessel functions multiplied by a smooth cutoff envelope. In the lecture's notation,

$$
B_n(r)
=\frac{2}{r_c}\frac{\sin(n\pi r/r_c)}{r}
f_{\mathrm{env}}(r,r_c),
\qquad
R(r)=W_N\!\left(\cdots W_2(W_1B(r))\right).
$$

The envelope sends the interaction smoothly to zero at cutoff $$r_c$$. The fixed harmonic supplies angular type; the learned radial network controls how strongly atoms at different separations interact.

The energy construction then turns hidden equivariance into physical output laws. If $$E(Rx)=E(x)$$ for an orthogonal matrix $$R$$, the chain rule gives

$$
\nabla_{Rx}E(Rx)=R\nabla_xE(x),
$$

and hence $$F(Rx)=RF(x)$$. Translation invariance similarly implies

$$
0=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}E(x_1+t,\ldots,x_N+t)
=-\sum_i F_i,
$$

so the predicted internal forces sum to zero. Predicting a scalar energy first therefore enforces equivariant, conservative forces and a global momentum-balance identity that a direct unconstrained vector output would not automatically satisfy.

### Degree and parity selection rules

A schematic NequIP update can be written as

$$
\mathcal L_{a c m_o}^{(l_o,p_o)}
=\sum_{b\in\mathcal N(a)}
\sum_{m_f,m_i}
C_{(l_f,m_f)(l_i,m_i)}^{(l_o,m_o)}
R_{c}^{l_o,p_o,l_f,p_f,l_i,p_i}(r_{ab})
Y_{m_f}^{(l_f)}(\hat r_{ab})
V_{b c m_i}^{(l_i,p_i)}.
$$

An interaction is allowed only when both

$$
|l_f-l_i|\le l_o\le l_f+l_i,
\qquad
p_fp_i=p_o.
$$

The first condition is angular-momentum coupling. The second is parity multiplication. Together they make every hidden update $$O(3)$$-equivariant.

The $$l=0$$ network uses only invariant scalar hidden features and is often dramatically worse. Moving to $$l=1$$ produces the largest improvement; $$l=2$$ and $$l=3$$ usually reduce the error further, but not monotonically in every cell. The uracil force result, for example, is lower at $$l=2$$ than at $$l=3$$. The table supports a precise conclusion: equivariant internal features are responsible for much of NequIP's data efficiency, while ever-higher maximum degree is not an unconditional guarantee of improvement.

The lecture ends by locating TFN and NequIP within a larger family. [Cormorant](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04015) uses related covariant molecular features with a more expressive scalar interaction; [3D Steerable CNNs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02547) apply steerable filters to volumetric data; and [Clebsch--Gordan Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.09231) use Clebsch--Gordan products as nonlinear operations in spherical Fourier space.

The chain is now complete. A group says how transformations compose. An action says how they move the data. A representation says how they move feature vectors. Irreps split those vectors into types. Spherical harmonics encode directions in those types. Clebsch--Gordan coefficients couple them. TFN turns the coupling into an equivariant convolution, and NequIP adds reflection parity to obtain an interatomic potential with invariant energy and equivariant conservative forces.
