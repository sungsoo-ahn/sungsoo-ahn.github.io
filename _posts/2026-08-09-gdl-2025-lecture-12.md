There is no single task called “materials prediction.” A model trained on QM9 maps one small molecule to invariant scalar properties. A force field trained on MD17 must recover an energy surface and a vector field. A universal materials potential must also handle periodic cells, many elements, off-equilibrium structures, pressure, temperature, and derived observables produced by long simulations.

That changing contract is the thread connecting the source lecture. Its model survey—GNoME, MatterSim, Orb, SevenNet, and eSEN—only makes sense after specifying what each dataset exposes and what each benchmark measures. Scale can mean more chemical elements, more thermodynamic conditions, more first-principles labels, more atoms in one simulation, or more downstream properties. Those axes are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## The datasets change the scientific contract

### QM9 predicts invariant molecular properties

[QM9](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201422) contains about 134,000 stable organic molecules with at most nine heavy atoms. Each molecule is associated with nineteen quantities computed from quantum chemistry. The usual prediction target is a molecular scalar, so translating or rotating the coordinates must not change the answer.

### MD17 makes forces part of the target

MD17 changes both the sample unit and the output. It contains snapshots from <i>ab initio</i> molecular-dynamics trajectories for ten organic molecules, including benzene and aspirin. Every snapshot provides atomic positions, one total potential energy, and a three-dimensional force vector for every atom. Revised MD17 selects 100,000 structures from the original collection and recalculates their energies and forces more consistently.

For a configuration $$X=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$$, an energy model defines

$$
E_\theta(X)\in\mathbb R,
\qquad
F_{i,\theta}(X)=-\nabla_{x_i}E_\theta(X).
$$

Energy is invariant to a global rotation, while force is equivariant:

$$
E_\theta(RX+t)=E_\theta(X),
\qquad
F_{i,\theta}(RX+t)=R F_{i,\theta}(X).
$$

A small energy error does not automatically imply an accurate gradient. MD17 therefore tests a stronger object than QM9: a local approximation to a potential-energy surface together with its derivatives.

### MPTrj and Alexandria broaden chemical and geometric coverage

The Materials Project trajectory dataset, MPTrj, contains 1,580,395 crystal structures collected from relaxation trajectories. It spans 89 elements and supplies energies, forces, stresses, and, when available, magnetic moments. Its purpose is no longer to fit one molecule or one narrow family. It trains a potential intended to transfer across much of inorganic chemical space.

The scale is uneven across elements and labels. The figure records 145,923 compounds, 49,295,660 force vectors, 14,223,555 stress labels, and 7,944,833 magnetic-moment labels. A “universal” dataset is therefore broad, but it is not uniformly sampled.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s06-mptraj-element-coverage.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Periodic table showing element and magnetic-moment counts in the Materials Project trajectory dataset." caption="MPTrj covers most of the periodic table, with strongly nonuniform element and magnetic-moment counts. The counts and label totals come from the <a href='https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Materials_Project_Trjectory_MPtrj_Dataset/23713842'>released MPTrj dataset</a>." %}

[Alexandria](https://alexandria.icams.rub.de/) is an open repository containing millions of molecular and periodic quantum-chemistry calculations. The feature emphasized in the lecture is not only its size. Alexandria retains slightly off-equilibrium structures and forces from geometry relaxation, so it records part of the route to a local minimum rather than only the final optimized structure.

That distinction matters for a force field. Relaxed crystals densely supervise low-energy basins but say little about the gradients needed to reach them. Relaxation paths add configurations with nonzero forces, broadening the local neighborhoods that a simulator encounters.

### Open Catalyst separates structure-to-force from initial-to-relaxed prediction

[OC20](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.09990) targets adsorption on catalyst surfaces. An adsorbate is an atom or molecule attached to a surface, the setting in which many energy-conversion reactions take place. The dataset contains 1,281,040 DFT relaxation trajectories and roughly 264 million individual energy-and-force evaluations across surfaces, adsorbates, and configurations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s08-oc20-dataset.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Open Catalyst 2020 dataset overview with adsorbates, surfaces, relaxation data, and applications." caption="OC20 couples diverse adsorbates and catalyst surfaces to relaxation, off-path, molecular-dynamics, and electronic-structure data. Source: the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.09990'>OC20 dataset paper</a>." %}

The source highlights two tasks with different outputs:

- **S2EF**, structure to energy and forces, receives a geometry and predicts its energy and atom-wise forces.
- **IS2RE**, initial structure to relaxed energy, receives the initial adsorbate--surface configuration and predicts the energy after relaxation.

S2EF approximates the local potential surface. IS2RE compresses an entire relaxation procedure into one endpoint estimate. Equal error units do not make those contracts equivalent.

[OC22](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08917) adds oxide electrocatalysts. It contains about 62,000 new DFT relaxation trajectories and 9.85 million evaluated structures across oxide compositions, adsorbate coverages, spin states, vacancies, and binary oxides. The benchmark predicts total energies for many states rather than limiting the target to adsorption energies. This encourages a model to represent the broader oxide energy landscape.

The overview comes from the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08917'>OC22 paper</a>." %}

[Open DAC 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00341) specializes the same machinery to direct air capture. It places carbon dioxide and water in porous sorbents such as metal--organic frameworks. Its targets include energy and forces for force-field development, adsorption energy, and relaxed geometry. The released ODAC23 dataset contains more than 38 million DFT calculations across more than 8,400 MOFs.

OC20, OC22, and ODAC23 do not merely add rows to one dataset. They change the surface chemistry, the adsorbate population, and the downstream decision. Generalization across adsorbates, catalyst compositions, oxide states, and MOF defects must be reported separately.

### OMat24, Matbench, and phonons add further evaluation layers

[OMat24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12771) contains more than 110 million periodic DFT calculations selected for structural and compositional diversity. Its structures include high-temperature ab initio molecular dynamics and randomly rattled versions of relaxed bulk materials from Alexandria. The point is to expose the model to non-equilibrium periodic configurations instead of concentrating every label near a relaxed minimum.

## Periodic crystals need a periodic representation

### One stored cell represents infinitely many atoms

A molecular point cloud is finite. A crystal is an infinite periodic set represented by a finite unit cell. Let the lattice matrix be

$$
L=[\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3],
$$

where $$\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3\in\mathbb R^3$$ are lattice vectors. If atom $$i$$ has position $$p_i$$ and type $$a_i$$ in the stored cell, the full crystal is

$$
\widehat{\mathcal P}
=\left\{p_i+Lk:
i=1,\ldots,N,\ k\in\mathbb Z^3\right\},
$$

$$
\widehat{\mathcal A}
=\left\{a_i:
i=1,\ldots,N,\ k\in\mathbb Z^3\right\}.
$$

The index $$k=(k_1,k_2,k_3)$$ chooses a periodic image. A graph constructed from only the coordinates written inside one cell can cut chemically adjacent atoms apart and can change when the cell boundary is shifted. The representation must therefore be invariant to which periodic image is chosen as the representative.

### Matformer encodes periodic images as multiple edges

[Matformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11807) builds a radius graph over the periodically repeated structure. The same pair of atom identities may be connected through several lattice translations. Those geometrically distinct relations become multiple edges, each with the distance to a particular periodic image.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s16-matformer-multiedge-graph.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Periodic lattice and a radius neighborhood represented as a multi-edge graph." caption="Matformer's periodic graph retains several image-dependent edges between the same atom identities. The diagram comes from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11807'>Yan et al.</a>" %}

Now shift the artificial unit-cell boundary. An atom may move from one stored image to another, but the infinite set of relative displacements is unchanged:

$$
(p_j+Lk_j)-(p_i+Lk_i)
=p_j-p_i+L(k_j-k_i).
$$

Because Matformer constructs features from these periodic relations, its graph representation is invariant to the choice of boundary.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s16-matformer-boundary-invariance.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two equivalent crystal unit cells producing the same periodic triangular motif." caption="Changing the stored cell moves representatives but preserves the periodic relation among atoms. This is the periodic-invariance condition enforced by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11807'>Matformer</a>." %}

Matformer also adds a periodic pattern encoding built from distances among repeated images. The lattice is not merely bookkeeping for neighbor search: its size and orientation help distinguish materials whose local atom types look similar.

### Crystal GNNs alter different bottlenecks

The lecture next surveys several crystal-specific architectures while expressing a deliberate reservation: adding more crystal machinery does not automatically imply a practical improvement.

- [ALIGNN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01829) performs message passing on the atomistic bond graph and on its line graph, making bond angles explicit.
- [PotNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10045) approximates complete Coulomb, dispersion, and repulsion potential sums rather than restricting every interaction to a local cutoff.
- [Crystalformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11686) turns the infinitely connected periodic attention problem into a tractable neural potential summation.
- [ComFormer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11857) constructs complete crystal representations and provides invariant and equivariant Transformer variants.
- [ReGNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02748) combines real-space geometric message passing for short-range effects with reciprocal-space blocks for long-range effects.

### Real-space and reciprocal-space cutoffs lose different information

A local message-passing layer can be written as a sum of messages at neighbor locations:

$$
h_i'
=\sum_j m(x_j)
=\int_{\mathbb R^3}m(x)
\sum_j\delta(x-x_j)\,dx.
$$

A real-space cutoff keeps only atoms with $$\lVert x_j-x_i\rVert\le r_c$$. This works well for fast-decaying interactions but removes every contribution beyond the radius.

The same field has a Fourier transform

$$
\widehat m(k)=\int_{\mathbb R^3}m(x)e^{i k\cdot x}\,dx.
$$

Slow spatial variation concentrates at low frequency. A reciprocal-space approximation keeps low-frequency components and truncates large $$\lVert k\rVert$$. It can therefore retain global, slowly decaying structure while discarding fine spatial detail. Neither truncation is universally correct; they approximate different parts of the interaction.

## GNoME turns energy prediction into an active discovery loop

[GNoME](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06735-9) is a collection of graph networks that predict crystal energy and stability. Its contribution is not only a lower test error. It connects model prediction to repeated candidate generation, DFT evaluation, and retraining.

The structural pipeline generates candidate crystals, converts them to graphs, and filters them by predicted stability. The compositional pipeline begins from chemical compositions, predicts stability, and applies ab initio random structure search. Candidates from both routes are evaluated with DFT. The validated structures return to the training database for another active-learning round.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s21-gnome-active-learning.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="GNoME structural and compositional candidate pipelines connected to DFT and active learning." caption="GNoME filters structural and compositional proposals, sends selected candidates to DFT, and feeds the results back into training. The pipeline from <a href='https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06735-9'>Merchant et al.</a> produced 2.2 million candidate structures, including 381,000 new entries on the convex hull, through this computational loop." %}

Stability is judged relative to competing compositions, commonly through energy above the convex hull. A model can rank millions of structures cheaply, but DFT remains the higher-fidelity evaluator inside the loop. Candidate generation, learned ranking, and first-principles validation are three distinct stages.

The lecture also draws a boundary around the autonomous-laboratory narrative. GNoME itself demonstrates a computational discovery pipeline. The robotic [A-Lab](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06734-w) is a separate experimental system that uses computed candidates, literature-derived recipes, robotics, characterization, and active learning. Collaboration between the projects does not make every GNoME prediction an experimentally closed-loop discovery. The computational output is a candidate set; synthesis and structural validation remain additional evidence.

## MatterSim expands the thermodynamic domain

### One model spans elements, temperature, pressure, and tasks

[MatterSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04967) is trained across chemical elements, temperatures, pressures, datasets, and evaluation scenarios. The source contrasts an invariant M3GNet backbone with an equivariant Graphormer backbone and reports that M3GNet is preferred for speed. This is an early version of the lecture's later question: how much explicit equivariance is worth paying for when data and compute are large?

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s23-mattersim-overview.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="MatterSim connecting material tasks, candidate structures, thermodynamic conditions, and machine learning capabilities." caption="MatterSim spans structure optimization, phonons, mechanical properties, phase diagrams, and molecular dynamics across temperature and pressure. The overview comes from the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04967'>MatterSim paper</a>." %}

The figure separates three roles. Candidate materials and thermodynamic conditions define the domain. A deep graph network supplies the atomistic model. That model is then used as a zero-shot force field, an uncertainty estimator, a fine-tuning starting point, or a direct property predictor. Those outputs should not be collapsed into one notion of “foundation-model accuracy.”

### Training alternates exploration and first-principles supervision

MatterSim begins from open datasets such as MPF2021, MPTrj, and Alexandria, then adds structures generated by random structure search and MatterGen. An ensemble estimates uncertainty. Molecular dynamics explores off-equilibrium states across temperature and pressure. High-uncertainty, deduplicated structures are sent to a first-principles supervisor, added to the database, and used to retrain the model.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s24-mattersim-training-loop.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="MatterSim ground-state and off-equilibrium exploration with uncertainty selection and first-principles supervision." caption="Open and generated structures seed MatterSim; uncertainty selection connects both ground-state and off-equilibrium exploration to a first-principles supervisor. The workflow comes from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04967'>Yang et al.</a>" %}

This loop changes the meaning of dataset size. Ten million uniformly redundant structures may add less information than a smaller set selected at the boundary of the model's current competence. The relevant denominator is not only the number of calculations but also which regions of composition and phase space they cover.

### The force field is evaluated through derived observables

The lecture emphasizes that MatterSim is used for lattice dynamics and thermodynamic quantities, not merely static energies. Its evaluation includes maximum phonon frequency, the phonon dispersion of ZnSe, bulk modulus, the temperature dependence of AlN's modulus, free energy, and the B1--B2 phase boundary of MgO.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s25-mattersim-physical-properties.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="MatterSim comparisons for phonons, elastic moduli, free energy, and an MgO phase boundary." caption="MatterSim predictions are passed into phonon, elasticity, free-energy, and phase-boundary calculations. These panels from the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04967'>source paper</a> show the model-to-observable handoff that a static force MAE alone cannot test." %}

Each observable amplifies a different part of the learned energy surface. Phonons depend on second derivatives near equilibrium. Molecular dynamics repeatedly queries first derivatives along a trajectory. A phase boundary compares free energies across phases and thermodynamic conditions. Agreement on one quantity does not guarantee agreement on another.

### Heating tests expose off-equilibrium robustness

MatterSim's robustness experiment selects 118 systems spanning bulk inorganic materials, MOFs, two-dimensional materials, interfaces, molecular crystals, polymers, surfaces, and molecules. Each system is heated from 0 to 5000 K over a relatively short trajectory. The simulation traverses crystalline, liquid, disordered, and phase-transition regimes.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s26-mattersim-heating-tests.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="MatterSim heating trajectories showing potential energy, pressure or temperature, and structural change." caption="Rapid heating drives representative systems through large structural changes while MatterSim tracks energy and pressure or temperature. The panels from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04967'>the MatterSim robustness study</a> test behavior far from relaxed crystals." %}

A bounded trajectory is still not proof of an accurate physical observable. It does show whether the model remains numerically usable when local environments leave the low-temperature training regime. The experiment is therefore a stress test for extrapolation, not simply another random test split.

MatterSim also combines zero-shot transfer with active learning on a new trajectory. In the displayed example, a small number of labeled structures reduces the maximum force error below a 0.2 eV/Å DFT-error threshold. Fine-tuning on 30 selected structures recovers radial and angular distributions close to experiment, while the zero-shot model deviates more strongly.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s27-mattersim-active-learning.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="MatterSim active learning curves, uncertainty, radial distribution, and angular distribution." caption="Targeted first-principles labels reduce force error and improve structural distributions along a new trajectory. The active-learning result comes from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04967'>MatterSim</a>." %}

## Orb asks whether scale can replace explicit geometric priors

### A scalable MPNN without explicit rotation symmetry

[Orb](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22570) deliberately prioritizes scalability and does not build rotation invariance or equivariance into the architecture in the way steerable force fields do. The lecture reports a model seven times smaller than MatterSim, training examples containing as many as 5,000 atoms, and leading Matbench Discovery results at the time of the source.

Orb uses an attention-augmented Graph Network-based Simulator. A graph encoder initializes node and edge features; repeated graph-network blocks pass messages; a decoder predicts the desired dynamics information. Schematically,

$$
e_{ij}^{(m+1)}
=\phi_e\!\left(e_{ij}^{(m)},v_i^{(m)},v_j^{(m)}\right),
$$

$$
v_i^{(m+1)}
=\phi_v\!\left(v_i^{(m)},\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}e_{ij}^{(m+1)}\right).
$$

The functions are shared over nodes and edges, which preserves graph-permutation equivariance, but the features do not carry a prescribed three-dimensional rotation law.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s29-orb-gns-architecture.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph Network-based Simulator with encoder, repeated message-passing processor, and decoder." caption="Orb adopts an attention-augmented Graph Network-based Simulator rather than a steerable tensor-product network. The architecture panel is retained from the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22570'>Orb paper</a>." %}

Orb pretrains through diffusion-based generation of material structures. A corrupted atomic configuration is iteratively transformed toward a structured material. The generative task makes a large pool of unlabeled structures useful before supervised force-field fitting.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s29-orb-diffusion-pretraining.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sequence of atomic configurations becoming an ordered porous material during Orb diffusion pretraining." caption="Orb's diffusion pretraining turns a disordered atomic configuration into a structured material across denoising steps. The source figure comes from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22570'>Neumann et al.</a>" %}

### Discovery score and runtime support different claims

The lecture reproduces an Orb comparison with Matbench Discovery F1 values of roughly 0.67 for MACE, 0.81 for GNoME, 0.83 for MatterSim, and 0.85 for Orb. The claim is about the complete released systems, not an architecture-only comparison.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s30-orb-discovery-f1.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Bar chart comparing Matbench Discovery F1 for MACE, GNoME, MatterSim, and Orb." caption="The source lecture's native Orb chart compares system-level Matbench Discovery F1. Orb is highest among the four displayed releases; the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22570'>paper</a> also reports MPTrj-controlled comparisons." %}

The runtime panel plots milliseconds on one A100 GPU as system size grows. Although the chart title says “throughput,” the vertical quantity is runtime, so lower curves are faster. Orb-xs, Orb-sm, and Orb remain far below MACE across the displayed range up to 10,000 atoms.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s30-orb-throughput.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Runtime versus atom count for MACE and several Orb model sizes on one A100 GPU." caption="Runtime grows with system size for every model, but the three Orb variants remain below MACE in the displayed one-A100 test. The chart comes from the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22570'>Orb evaluation</a>." %}

The two panels answer different questions. F1 measures a discovery ranking under one benchmark protocol. Runtime measures the cost of evaluating a system as atom count grows. A model can improve one axis while regressing on the other.

### Controlled scaling still finds a benefit from equivariance

Orb motivates the broad question, but one successful non-equivariant model does not settle it. [Does equivariance matter at scale?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23179) studies rigid-body interactions with equivariant and non-equivariant Transformers while varying model size, training steps, data, and compute.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s31-equivariance-scaling.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Scaling curves comparing equivariant, non-equivariant, and augmented Transformers with compute and data." caption="In the controlled rigid-body study, both models follow compute scaling laws, but the equivariant Transformer has lower loss at every tested compute budget. Data augmentation closes much of the non-equivariant model's data-efficiency gap. Panels from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23179'>Brehmer et al.</a>" %}

The compute plot fits power laws to both architectures and finds a similar multiplicative advantage for the equivariant model across the tested budgets. The data plot finds a large sample-efficiency advantage over the unaugmented baseline. Rotation augmentation can close that gap when the non-equivariant model receives sufficient training.

This does not contradict Orb. The controlled study fixes a rigid-body task and compares matched Transformers. Orb compares complete interatomic-potential systems with different architectures, objectives, training corpora, and runtime profiles. The lecture's conclusion remains appropriately open: more controlled studies are needed before calling equivariance either unnecessary at scale or universally worth its computational cost.

## SevenNet scales equivariant molecular dynamics through communication

[SevenNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03789) starts from a NequIP-like equivariant potential, incorporates modifications associated with the GNoME potential, and asks how to run message-passing molecular dynamics across many processors. The bottleneck is not only floating-point computation. Each processor needs neighbor information owned by other processors.

Spatial decomposition divides the simulation cell into subdomains. Each processor owns the atoms in one subdomain and creates ghost atoms for neighboring atoms across the boundary. Coordinates $$r$$ and atomic types $$Z$$ are communicated before graph construction. Hidden features $$h$$ must then be exchanged after message-passing layers because later layers depend on updated representations from remote neighbors.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s33-sevennet-spatial-decomposition.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="SevenNet spatial decomposition with forward communication of positions, types, and hidden features and reverse communication of energy gradients and forces." caption="SevenNet communicates coordinates and atom types, then hidden features between message-passing stages. Reverse communication combines partial energy gradients and forces across subdomains. The algorithm is from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03789'>Park et al.</a>" %}

The energy is accumulated from processor-local contributions. Force evaluation differentiates that total energy, producing partial gradients on owned and ghost atoms. Reverse communication sends those contributions back to the owning processor. The sequence is therefore:

1. decompose the cell and exchange $$r,Z$$;
2. construct local subdomain graphs;
3. exchange hidden states as message passing expands the receptive field;
4. sum atomic energies;
5. backpropagate energy gradients; and
6. reverse-communicate partial forces to their owners.

SevenNet also removes tensor products from selected network components to reduce cost. With the distributed algorithm and engineering changes, the source reports training and inference on a single system containing as many as 112,000 atoms.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s34-sevennet-large-system.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Large amorphous silicon nitride system with radial and angular distribution functions." caption="SevenNet simulates a 10.6 nm amorphous silicon-nitride cell and recovers its radial and angular distributions. This result from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03789'>the SevenNet paper</a> connects parallel execution to a system larger than the training structures." %}

The large-system result is not just a throughput number. The radial distribution functions $$g(r)$$ compare Si--N, Si--Si, and N--N distances, while the angular distributions compare Si--N--Si and N--Si--N motifs. The simulation must preserve meaningful structure after scaling to more than 100,000 atoms.

## eSEN makes smoothness part of model selection

### Conservative forces are necessary but not sufficient

[eSEN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12147) builds on the eSCN architecture and asks why low held-out energy and force errors may still produce poor molecular dynamics or physical properties. Its answer is that the learned potential must also be conservative and sufficiently smooth.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s35-esen-architecture.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="eSEN energy model and its edgewise SO2 convolution block." caption="eSEN predicts a scalar energy through stacked edgewise and nodewise blocks; forces and stress are obtained by differentiation. The architecture panel comes from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12147'>Fu et al.</a>" %}

A conservative force field satisfies

$$
\oint_\gamma F\cdot dr=0
$$

for every closed path $$\gamma$$. Predicting an invariant scalar energy and differentiating it,

$$
F_i=-\nabla_{r_i}E,
$$

enforces this condition. Directly predicting force vectors does not guarantee that they integrate to one globally consistent potential, even when the force MAE is low.

Numerical energy conservation also depends on derivatives of the potential and on the time integrator. The source writes an error bound of the form

$$
\left\lvert E(r_T,a)-E(r_0,a)\right\rvert
\le C\Delta t^2+C_N\Delta t^N T,
$$

where $$\Delta t$$ is the time step, $$T$$ is the simulated duration, and the constants depend on derivatives of the learned energy and the solver. Making $$\Delta t$$ small does not repair a potential with unbounded or discontinuous derivatives near neighbor cutoffs.

The lecture extracts three practical rules:

- do not predict forces independently when a conservative energy-derived field is required;
- multiply radial interactions by a smooth envelope that reaches zero at the neighbor cutoff; and
- prefer a Bessel radial basis over a Gaussian basis when the latter makes the representation more sensitive to small coordinate shifts.

### Energy drift diagnoses architectural roughness

The eSEN tests perturb trajectories under three failure mechanisms. Direct-force prediction produces enormous energy drift because the force need not be conservative. Discretizing the spherical representation introduces smaller but systematic drift. Removing a smooth envelope or imposing a hard neighbor limit makes the potential change discontinuously when an atom crosses the cutoff. Increasing basis count does not by itself repair that discontinuity.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-12/s37-esen-smoothness-tests.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Energy-drift curves for direct force prediction, representation discretization, cutoff choices, and radial bases." caption="eSEN's energy-conservation tests separate non-conservative direct forces, representation discretization, and nonsmooth potential choices. The plots from <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12147'>the source paper</a> show that test-set MAE alone misses these failures." %}

The diagnostic is operational: run molecular dynamics and measure energy drift. A model that fails is not rejected because its architecture sounds physically implausible; it is rejected because the downstream solver exposes a defect in the learned surface.

eSEN closes the lecture's progression by changing the success criterion again. QM9 began with invariant scalar error. MD17 added forces. Materials datasets added chemistry, periodicity, and off-equilibrium coverage. MatterSim and SevenNet added thermodynamic conditions and system size. eSEN finally requires the learned energy surface to remain smooth enough that a numerical trajectory produces meaningful physical properties.

No one leaderboard captures that progression. Dataset coverage, periodic representation, active selection, explicit equivariance, distributed communication, and energy smoothness control different failure modes. The next part of the course turns from predicting energies and properties to geometric generation; protein representation learning is deferred to that later task map.
