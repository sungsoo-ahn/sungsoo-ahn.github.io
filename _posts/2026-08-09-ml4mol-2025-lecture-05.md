## Geometric graphs and Euclidean symmetry

A geometric graph couples ordinary graph structure to points in Euclidean space. For $$N$$ vertices, write

$$
\mathcal G=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E),
\qquad
X=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)^\top\in\mathbb R^{N\times 3},
\qquad
H\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}.
$$

The edge set may come from chemical bonds, a distance cutoff, or a nearest-neighbor rule. It is often derived from the coordinates rather than supplied independently. A geometric GNN must therefore account for both permutation of vertices and transformations of the coordinate system.

Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 4." %}

The relevant transformations are translations, rotations, and sometimes reflections. A scalar molecular property such as energy should remain unchanged. A vector output such as force should rotate or reflect with the molecule. These requirements are called invariance and equivariance.

The Euclidean group $$E(n)$$ contains every distance-preserving transformation in $$n$$ dimensions. The special Euclidean group $$SE(n)$$ contains rotations and translations but excludes reflections. Likewise, $$O(n)$$ contains rotations and reflections fixing the origin, while $$SO(n)$$ keeps only rotations with determinant $$+1$$.

A molecular-dynamics model supplies a potential energy and its atom-wise derivatives. Classical force fields often decompose the energy into bond-length, angle, torsion, and nonbonded terms,

$$
E=E_{\mathrm{bond}}+E_{\mathrm{angle}}+E_{\mathrm{torsion}}+E_{\mathrm{nonbonded}},
\qquad
F_i=-\nabla_{x_i}E.
$$

The decomposition motivates geometric features at increasing order: distances describe pairs, angles describe triples, and torsions describe quadruples.

## Groups, actions, and representations

A group $$(G,\cdot)$$ is a set with a product satisfying closure, associativity, an identity element, and inverses. These axioms allow transformations to compose without leaving the family. The group is abstract: to say how it changes a molecule, graph, image, or feature vector, one must also define an action.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s09-image13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Examples of closure, identity, and inverse operations in a symmetry group." caption="The group axioms formalize a set of transformations that can be composed and undone. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 9." %}

An element of $$SE(n)$$ can be represented by a rotation–translation pair $$(R,t)$$. Acting on a point gives $$x\mapsto Rx+t$$. Composing $$(R,t)$$ with $$(R',t')$$ yields

$$
(R,t)(R',t')=(RR',Rt'+t).
$$

The translation in the second transformation must first be rotated by the first rotation. In homogeneous coordinates, the pair becomes the matrix

$$
\begin{pmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
$$

so ordinary matrix multiplication reproduces the group product.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s10-image110.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Rotation and translation transformations in Euclidean space." caption="Euclidean motions combine rotations with translations while preserving distances. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 10." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s11-image15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Homogeneous matrices for composing Euclidean rotations and translations." caption="Homogeneous matrices turn the semidirect rotation–translation composition into matrix multiplication. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 11." %}

### Group actions

A left group action maps $$(g,x)$$ to $$g\cdot x$$ and obeys

$$
g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x,
\qquad
e\cdot x=x.
$$

The same abstract group can act differently on different spaces. A rotation acts on positions, functions over space, vector fields, and tensor features through different formulas. The action is therefore part of the model specification.

### Group representations

A group representation is a linear action on a vector space. It assigns each $$g\in G$$ an invertible matrix $$\rho(g)$$ such that

$$
\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h),
\qquad
\rho(e)=I.
$$

A feature vector then transforms as $$v\mapsto\rho(g)v$$. Different feature types correspond to different representations of the same group.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s14-group-representation.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Definition of a group representation as an invertible matrix action on vectors." caption="Representations convert group composition into compatible linear transformations of features. Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 14." %}

For a graph with node-feature matrix $$X$$ and adjacency $$A$$, a permutation matrix $$P$$ acts by

$$
X\mapsto PX,
\qquad
A\mapsto PAP^\top.
$$

The node feature and adjacency use different representations. After vectorizing the adjacency, the second action becomes $$\operatorname{vec}(PAP^\top)=(P\otimes P)\operatorname{vec}(A)$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s15-image29.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Permutation acting once on node features and twice on an adjacency matrix." caption="Node-wise and pair-wise graph features transform under different permutation representations. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 15." %}

### Circular and spherical harmonics

Circular harmonics provide steerable features for planar rotations. The degree-$$m$$ pair $$(\cos m\theta,\sin m\theta)$$ mixes by a two-dimensional rotation matrix when the input angle is shifted. Its phase changes, while its squared amplitude—and therefore the area or norm associated with a full period—remains invariant.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s16-image30.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Circular harmonic basis functions and their two-dimensional rotation representation." caption="A planar rotation steers the sine and cosine components of a circular harmonic into one another. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 16." %}

Spherical harmonics $$Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)$$ are an orthonormal basis for functions on the sphere. The degree $$\ell$$ sets angular frequency, and $$m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell$$ indexes the $$2\ell+1$$ components. Degree zero is scalar. Degree one transforms like an ordinary three-dimensional vector after a change of basis.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s18-image32.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Grid of spherical harmonics for several degrees and orders." caption="Spherical harmonics organize angular functions by degree and order. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 18." %}

Under a rotation $$g\in SO(3)$$, the components at fixed $$\ell$$ mix by a Wigner matrix,

$$
Y^{(\ell)}(g\cdot\hat r)=D^{(\ell)}(g)Y^{(\ell)}(\hat r).
$$

The function rotates, but it remains inside the same degree-$$\ell$$ subspace.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s19-image36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Spherical harmonic components before and after a three-dimensional rotation." caption="Wigner D matrices steer the components of a fixed spherical-harmonic degree. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 19." %}

An operator $$\Phi:X\to Y$$ is equivariant when the transformation can be applied before or after the operator with the same result:

$$
\Phi\!\left(\rho_X(g)x\right)=\rho_Y(g)\Phi(x).
$$

Invariance is the special case $$\rho_Y(g)=I$$. A molecular energy is invariant; a coordinate update or force field is equivariant.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s20-image38.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Equivariance commuting diagram for a rotated geometric graph and output." caption="An equivariant model commutes with the group action on its input and output spaces. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 20." %}

## Invariant neural networks by scalarization

The simplest geometric GNN converts coordinate-sensitive inputs into invariant scalars before learning. Distances, bond angles, and torsion angles are unchanged by translations and rotations. Once those values have been computed, an ordinary scalar MPNN can process them without violating the desired invariance.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s22-image40.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Distance, bond angle, and torsion angle derived from atomic coordinates." caption="Scalarization replaces coordinates with invariant pair, triple, and quadruple geometry. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 22." %}

### SchNet

SchNet uses interatomic distance as an edge feature. A radial network converts $$r_{ij}=\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert$$ into a continuous filter, and the filter modulates a message from atom $$j$$ to atom $$i$$. A typical interaction has the form

$$
h_i^{(\ell+1)}=h_i^{(\ell)}+
\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}
W^{(\ell)}(r_{ij})\odot V^{(\ell)}h_j^{(\ell)}.
$$

Because the learned geometric input is radial, the hidden states remain scalar and the predicted energy is invariant.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s23-image42.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="SchNet continuous-filter interaction blocks centered on an atom." caption="SchNet turns each interatomic distance into a continuous convolutional filter. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 23." %}

### DimeNet

Distances alone do not expose the angular terms in the usual force-field decomposition. DimeNet uses directional messages associated with ordered atom pairs. The message $$m_{ji}$$ is interpreted as information traveling from $$j$$ to $$i$$. Updating it uses neighboring directed edges $$k\to j$$ and the angle $$\angle kji$$, so triplets enter the computation explicitly.

Radial basis functions encode $$r_{ji}$$, while spherical basis functions combine radial information with the bond angle. The pair-message graph can be viewed as message passing on directed edges rather than on atoms. This increases cost because the relevant objects are triplets, but it makes local angular structure directly accessible.

Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 24." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s25-directed-edge-update.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Directed edge message updated from neighboring directed edges and their bond angles." caption="A DimeNet message lives on an ordered atom pair and aggregates angle-aware messages entering its source. Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 25." %}

### GemNet

GemNet extends directional message passing to torsion information. Distances and angles do not always identify a unique three-dimensional structure; a dihedral distinguishes rotations around a bond. GemNet introduces interactions among appropriate two-hop neighborhoods so that four-atom geometry can affect the message.

Its universal directional formulation also connects invariant scalar processing to equivariant outputs through scalar–vector products. The progression from SchNet to DimeNet to GemNet therefore mirrors the progression from pair distances to angles to torsions in molecular geometry.

## Vector-based equivariant networks

Scalarization is not the only route. A network can retain scalar channels $$s_i$$ and vector channels $$v_i\in\mathbb R^{3\times d_v}$$. Scalar nonlinearities can be arbitrary. Vector operations must be constrained so that rotating the input rotates each vector channel in the same way.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s29-scalar-vector-channels.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Network with separate scalar and three-dimensional vector feature channels." caption="Vector-based networks carry invariant scalar channels beside equivariant vector channels. Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 29." %}

Safe primitives include linear mixing across vector channels, scalar multiplication of vectors, inner products that return scalars, and sums of equivariant vectors. A coordinate difference is especially useful:

$$
x_i-x_j\mapsto R(x_i-x_j)
$$

under a Euclidean motion because translation cancels.

### EGNN

EGNN builds a simple $$E(n)$$-equivariant layer from invariant edge messages and coordinate differences. One standard form is

$$
m_{ij}=\phi_e\!\left(h_i,h_j,\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2,e_{ij}\right),
$$

$$
x_i'=x_i+sum_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)\phi_x(m_{ij}),
\qquad
h_i'=\phi_h\!\left(h_i,\sum_jm_{ij}\right).
$$

The scalar coefficient $$\phi_x(m_{ij})$$ is invariant. Multiplying it by the relative vector gives an equivariant displacement, while the node-feature update stays invariant.

Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 30." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s31-image54.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Equations for EGNN edge, coordinate, and node-feature updates." caption="The EGNN layer alternates invariant message computation with an equivariant coordinate update. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 31." %}

### PaiNN

PaiNN keeps explicit scalar and vector features rather than using the coordinates themselves as the only equivariant state. Its allowed operations include arbitrary functions of scalars, scalar products of vector channels, linear mixing of vector channels, multiplication of a vector by a scalar, and sums of vectors.

The interaction block uses a radial filter and the unit direction $$\hat r_{ij}$$ to exchange information between atoms. It produces scalar messages, vector messages aligned with existing vector features, and vector messages aligned with the interatomic direction. A mixing block then forms invariant vector norms and inner products, uses them to update the scalar channels, and gates linearly transformed vector channels. Residual updates preserve both information flow and transformation type.

PaiNN can therefore represent polarization-like directional responses while remaining much simpler than a full spherical-tensor network. The price is that its hidden geometry is restricted to scalar and ordinary vector types.

## Spherical tensor features

Modern steerable GNNs generalize the scalar–vector split to type $$0,1,\ldots,L$$ spherical tensors. Type zero is a scalar. Type one is vector-like. Higher types contain $$2\ell+1$$ components that transform together under $$D^{(\ell)}(R)$$. A layer may carry several channels of each type at every atom.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s36-spherical-feature-types.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Network layers containing type-zero, type-one, and higher spherical tensor channels." caption="A spherical-tensor network carries multiple irreducible feature types through every layer. Cropped from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 36." %}

The central operation is the Clebsch–Gordan tensor product. It combines two steerable inputs and decomposes their product into output types. This supplies the bilinear mixing that an ordinary MLP would perform freely, but with the output representation controlled exactly.

### Irreducible representations

A representation is reducible when a change of basis makes every representation matrix block diagonal. Each invariant block is itself a representation. Repeating this decomposition yields irreducible representations—pieces that cannot be decomposed further by a shared change of basis.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s38-image65.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A reducible representation decomposed into smaller invariant blocks." caption="Irreducible representations are the minimal feature types preserved by every group transformation. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 38." %}

For $$SO(2)$$, circular harmonics provide the irreducible types: the constant scalar and the two-component sine–cosine pairs at each nonzero frequency. For $$SO(3)$$, the irreducible representations are the Wigner matrices $$D^{(\ell)}$$ of size $$(2\ell+1)\times(2\ell+1)$$.

## Tensor products and Clebsch–Gordan decomposition

The ordinary tensor product of two representation vectors is steerable. If $$v_1\mapsto\rho_1(g)v_1$$ and $$v_2\mapsto\rho_2(g)v_2$$, then

$$
v_1\otimes v_2
\mapsto
(\rho_1(g)\otimes\rho_2(g))(v_1\otimes v_2).
$$

The product representation is usually reducible. A Clebsch–Gordan change of basis reorganizes it into irreducible output blocks.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s42-image71.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Kronecker-product representation acting on the tensor product of two steerable vectors." caption="The tensor product of two steerable vectors transforms under the Kronecker product representation. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 42." %}

For two spherical tensors of types $$\ell_1$$ and $$\ell_2$$, the allowed output degrees satisfy the triangle rule

$$
|\ell_1-\ell_2|\le \ell\le \ell_1+\ell_2.
$$

The $$m$$-th component of the output is

$$
(v^{(\ell_1)}\otimes_{\mathrm{CG}}v^{(\ell_2)})^{(\ell)}_m
=
\sum_{m_1,m_2}
C^{\ell m}_{\ell_1m_1,\ell_2m_2}
v^{(\ell_1)}_{m_1}v^{(\ell_2)}_{m_2}.
$$

The coefficients are fixed by representation theory rather than learned. Learnable channel weights determine which permitted couplings the network uses.

### Cartesian tensors as a comparison

A Cartesian vector $$x$$ transforms as $$x\mapsto Rx$$. Its outer product $$A=x\otimes x=xx^\top$$ transforms as

$$
A\mapsto RAR^\top,
\qquad
\operatorname{vec}(A)\mapsto(R\otimes R)\operatorname{vec}(A).
$$

Higher Cartesian tensors use repeated Kronecker products. Their dimension grows as $$3^k$$ for order $$k$$, and the representation is reducible.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s47-image76.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Vector, matrix, and order-three Cartesian tensor formed by repeated outer products." caption="Naive Cartesian tensor products generate higher-order features whose dimensions grow exponentially. {% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s48-image82.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Derivation of the rotation law for a rank-two Cartesian tensor." caption="A rank-two Cartesian tensor rotates on both indices, equivalently by a Kronecker-product matrix after vectorization. Spherical tensors use the same raw tensor-product principle followed by irreducible decomposition. For two type-one inputs, the nine-dimensional Cartesian product decomposes into $$\ell=0,1,2$$ blocks of dimensions $$1+3+5$$. The decomposition removes redundancy from the transformation law and labels each output by a definite type.

## Tensor Field Networks

Tensor Field Networks were among the first neural networks to operate systematically on steerable features at geometric points. Each hidden feature is indexed by atom or point $$a$$, channel $$c$$, degree $$\ell$$, and order $$m$$. The name *tensor field* reflects the assignment of tensors to spatial locations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s53-image81.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tensor Field Network mapping a point cloud with typed features to new tensor fields." caption="TFN associates irreducible tensor features with every geometric point. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol geometric GNN lecture, slide 53." %}

The filter on displacement $$r_{ab}=x_b-x_a$$ separates radius and direction,

$$
F_{c_fm_f}^{(\ell_f)}(r_{ab})
=R_{c_f}^{(\ell_f)}(\lVert r_{ab}\rVert)
Y_{m_f}^{(\ell_f)}(\widehat r_{ab}).
$$

The radial function is learned. The spherical harmonic supplies the exact angular transformation law.

The message couples an input feature with the filter through a Clebsch–Gordan product and then sums over neighbors, channels, and compatible input/filter degrees. In schematic form,

$$
V_{acm}^{(\ell),\mathrm{out}}
=
\sum_{b\in\mathcal N(a)}
\sum_{\ell_i,\ell_f,c_i,c_f}
W^{\ell}_{\ell_i\ell_f,c_ic_f,c}
\left(
V_{bc_i}^{(\ell_i)}
\otimes_{\mathrm{CG}}
F_{c_f}^{(\ell_f)}(r_{ab})
\right)_m^{(\ell)}.
$$

Nonlinearities must preserve type. Type-zero channels use ordinary scalar activations. For $$\ell>0$$, TFN applies a norm-based nonlinearity: transform the invariant norm and rescale the vector of components without changing its orientation in representation space. Self-interaction layers mix channels only within the same type, acting like point-wise $$1\times1$$ convolutions.

## NequIP for interatomic potentials

NequIP applies a TFN-like architecture to interatomic potentials. Local equivariant interaction blocks update atom-centered features. The final readout extracts invariant scalar contributions and sums them into a total energy,

$$
E=\sum_i E_i,
\qquad
F_i=-\nabla_{x_i}E.
$$

The energy is invariant, and differentiating it gives equivariant, conservative forces. Locality comes from the neighbor cutoff; the equivariant feature types determine what angular information can propagate inside that cutoff.

NequIP augments rotation degree with parity. A feature type $$(\ell,p)$$ records how it transforms under reflection in addition to how it rotates. Ordinary vectors and pseudovectors have different parity even when both have three components. The filter uses spherical harmonics of the displacement, radial networks, and parity-compatible Clebsch–Gordan paths.

For each edge $$j\to i$$, the learned radial coefficients multiply $$Y^{(\ell)}(\widehat r_{ij})$$. The interaction then couples filter and hidden features exactly as in TFN, sums over neighbors, and applies channel mixing and gated nonlinearities. Only degree and parity combinations allowed by the triangle and parity rules are instantiated.

The lecture closes with MD17 comparisons against learning-based and kernel baselines such as sGDML and FCHL. The reported force errors show the benefit of equivariant tensor features and strong data efficiency across the tested molecules. The result is not merely that NequIP has more geometric inputs: every intermediate feature has a declared transformation law, and the energy–force interface preserves it through differentiation.

This four-part progression is the main point of the lecture. Scalarization builds invariant models from distances, angles, and torsions. Vector networks add directional hidden state with a small menu of safe operations. Spherical-tensor networks generalize the feature types using irreducible representations and Clebsch–Gordan products. TFN and NequIP assemble those pieces into interatomic architectures whose outputs obey the Euclidean symmetries of molecular physics.
