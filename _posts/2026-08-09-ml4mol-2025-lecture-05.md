A molecule does not acquire a new energy when we rotate the coordinate axes. Its force vectors do rotate. That small distinction—what must stay fixed and what must move with the geometry—organizes almost every architecture in this chapter.

There are four increasingly expressive ways to respect it. First, one can discard orientation and learn only from invariant distances, angles, and torsions. Second, one can carry ordinary vectors alongside scalar channels. Third, one can generalize vectors to all irreducible spherical-tensor types. Finally, one can assemble those types into complete architectures such as Tensor Field Networks and NequIP. The mathematics of groups, actions, and representations is useful because it lets us state the transformation law once and then enforce it at every layer.

## Geometric graphs and Euclidean symmetry

A geometric graph is an ordinary graph together with points in Euclidean space. For $$N$$ vertices, write

$$
\mathcal G=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E),
\qquad
\mathcal V=\{1,\ldots,N\},
\qquad
X=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)^\top\in\mathbb R^{N\times 3},
\qquad
H\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}.
$$

The row $$x_i$$ is the position of vertex $$i$$ and the row $$h_i$$ contains its non-geometric features. For a molecule, these may encode atom type, charge, or an intermediate hidden state. The edge set can represent chemical bonds, but many atomistic networks instead construct it from a cutoff: $$i$$ and $$j$$ are neighbors when $$\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert<r_c$$. Connectivity is then itself induced by geometry.

A rigid change of pose alters those coordinates without altering the molecule; see the geometric-graph formalism reviewed by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511'>Duval et al.</a>" %}

This input has two symmetries. Relabeling atoms should only relabel node-wise outputs; that is permutation equivariance. Changing the Euclidean coordinate frame should leave scalar properties fixed and transform geometric outputs in a prescribed way. This lecture concentrates on the second symmetry.

### The language of invariance and equivariance

The Euclidean group $$E(n)$$ contains all distance-preserving transformations of $$n$$-dimensional Euclidean space. Every element acts as

$$
x\longmapsto Rx+t,
\qquad R\in O(n),\quad t\in\mathbb R^n,
$$

where $$R^\top R=I$$. The orthogonal group $$O(n)$$ includes proper rotations with determinant $$+1$$ and reflections with determinant $$-1$$. The special Euclidean group $$SE(n)$$ keeps translations and proper rotations but excludes reflections. In three dimensions, the distinction matters whenever chirality or parity matters.

For all atoms at once, translation, rotation, and reflection give

$$
X\mapsto X+\mathbf 1t^\top,
\qquad
X\mapsto XR^\top,
\qquad
X\mapsto XM^\top
$$

under a row-vector convention. A scalar energy should satisfy $$E(g\cdot X)=E(X)$$. A force matrix should satisfy $$F(g\cdot X)=F(X)R^\top$$ for a rotation. The first map is invariant; the second is equivariant. The right requirement comes from the physical type of the output, not from a blanket demand that everything be invariant.

### Molecular properties and molecular dynamics

It contains roughly 134,000 stable small organic molecules composed of C, H, O, N, and F, together with geometric, energetic, electronic, and thermodynamic targets. Atomization energy, orbital energy, heat capacity, and similar scalar targets do not depend on the pose of a molecule. A network may see three-dimensional coordinates, but its prediction must not reveal the coordinate frame.

A molecular-dynamics simulator repeatedly evaluates a potential energy and differentiates it:

$$
F_i=-\nabla_{x_i}E(X,H).
$$

Classical force fields make the geometry explicit by decomposing the potential into bond-length, bond-angle, torsion, and nonbonded terms,

$$
E=E_{\mathrm{bondlen}}+E_{\mathrm{angle}}+E_{\mathrm{torsion}}+E_{\mathrm{nonbonded}}.
$$

Pair distances, three-atom angles, and four-atom torsions therefore form a natural ladder of geometric descriptors. A learned potential need not keep this exact decomposition, but an architecture that cannot represent these relations is starting with a severe handicap.

## Groups, actions, and representations

A group $$(G,\cdot)$$ is a set equipped with a product satisfying four axioms:

1. **Closure:** $$g\cdot h\in G$$ for every $$g,h\in G$$.
2. **Associativity:** $$(g\cdot h)\cdot i=g\cdot(h\cdot i)$$.
3. **Identity:** an element $$e$$ satisfies $$e\cdot g=g\cdot e=g$$.
4. **Inverse:** every $$g$$ has $$g^{-1}$$ with $$g^{-1}g=gg^{-1}=e$$.

These axioms say that transformations can be composed and undone without leaving the family. They do not yet say what the transformations act on.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s09-group-composition.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A robot transformed first by g and then by g prime, compared with one composed transformation." caption="Compatibility of composition is the operational content of a group action: applying two rigid motions in sequence must equal applying their group product. This representation language is used systematically in <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.08498'>Cohen and Welling's steerable CNNs</a>." %}

### The Euclidean group as rotation–translation pairs

An element of $$SE(n)$$ can be represented by a pair $$(t,R)$$. Acting on a point gives $$x\mapsto Rx+t$$. If $$g=(t,R)$$ and $$g'=(t',R')$$, then

$$
g\cdot g'=(t,R)\cdot(t',R')=(Rt'+t,RR'),
$$

and

$$
g^{-1}=(-R^{-1}t,R^{-1}).
$$

The translation $$t'$$ is rotated before it is added because the second motion is expressed in the coordinate frame produced by the first. Homogeneous coordinates turn this semidirect product into ordinary matrix multiplication:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}R'&t'\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}RR'&Rt'+t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This is the concrete algebra behind "roto-translation." Reflections extend $$SE(n)$$ to $$E(n)$$ by allowing any $$R\in O(n)$$.

### A group action specifies the transformed object

A left group action on a set $$\mathcal X$$ is a map $$(g,x)\mapsto g\circ x$$ satisfying

$$
g\circ(g'\circ x)=(gg')\circ x,
\qquad
e\circ x=x.
$$

The same abstract rotation group can act on points, images, scalar fields, vector fields, and neural features in different ways. A rotation sends a point to $$Rx$$, a scalar field to $$[g\circ f](x)=f(R^{-1}x)$$, and a vector field to $$[g\circ v](x)=Rv(R^{-1}x)$$. Saying only "the group is $$SO(3)$$" is therefore incomplete; the input and output actions are part of the model definition.

See <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.08498'>Cohen and Welling</a> for the corresponding representation-based construction." %}

### Representations make actions linear

A group representation maps every $$g\in G$$ to an invertible matrix $$\rho(g)$$ such that

$$
\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h),
\qquad
\rho(e)=I.
$$

A feature vector transforms by $$v\mapsto\rho(g)v$$. Such a feature is often called a *steerable vector*: once its value is known in one frame, the representation tells us exactly how to steer it into another. Scalars use the trivial representation $$\rho(g)=1$$. Ordinary three-dimensional vectors use $$\rho(R)=R$$. Higher feature types use larger matrices.

Graphs already give a familiar example. If $$P$$ is a permutation matrix, node features and adjacency transform differently:

$$
X\mapsto PX,
\qquad
A\mapsto PAP^\top.
$$

After vectorization,

$$
\operatorname{vec}(PAP^\top)=(P\otimes P)\operatorname{vec}(A).
$$

The same permutation therefore has one representation on node-wise quantities and another on pair-wise quantities. Geometric networks work the same way: every feature type comes with its own representation.

### Circular harmonics: steering in two dimensions

Circular harmonics are the cleanest example. Start with the degree-one vector

$$
v(\theta_0)=
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_0\\\sin\theta_0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Adding an angle $$\theta$$ gives

$$
v(\theta_0+\theta)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}
v(\theta_0).
$$

The source slides use an equivalent sign convention for a left action. What matters is that the sine and cosine components mix by a $$2\times2$$ representation. For degree $$k$$,

$$
v_k(\theta_0)=
\begin{pmatrix}\cos(k\theta_0)\\\sin(k\theta_0)\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_k(\theta_0+\theta)=\rho_k(\theta)v_k(\theta_0),
$$

with a rotation through $$k\theta$$ in feature space.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s17-circular-harmonic-family.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Constant and sinusoidal circular harmonics with increasing frequency." caption="Circular harmonics separate angular signals into frequencies. A spatial rotation changes the phase within one frequency pair but does not mix different frequencies, as formalized in <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.08498'>steerable CNNs</a>." %}

The lecture asks what happens to the triangle area $$\tfrac12\cos\theta_0\sin\theta_0$$ under rotation. It is *not* invariant: it depends on the coordinate axes. The invariant is the squared norm,

$$
\lVert v_k(\theta_0)\rVert^2
=\cos^2(k\theta_0)+\sin^2(k\theta_0)=1.
$$

This distinction is worth keeping. Equivariance does not make every scalar expression invariant; only functions preserved by the representation, such as the norm of an orthogonal representation, are guaranteed to be invariant.

### Spherical harmonics: steering on the sphere

Spherical harmonics generalize Fourier modes from a circle to the sphere. In one standard convention,

$$
Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)
=N_{\ell m}P_\ell^m(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},
\qquad
m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell.
$$

Here $$P_\ell^m$$ is an associated Legendre polynomial and $$N_{\ell m}$$ is a normalization constant. The degree $$\ell$$ selects a $$(2\ell+1)$$-dimensional subspace; $$m$$ indexes a component inside it. Real spherical harmonics replace complex exponentials by sine/cosine combinations without changing the representation-theoretic picture.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s18-spherical-harmonics.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Grid of real spherical harmonics for degrees zero through three and orders minus three through three." caption="Spherical harmonics organize angular functions by degree ℓ and component m. Color records sign, while lobes reveal the increasing angular frequency used by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Tensor Field Networks</a>." %}

Under a rotation $$R\in SO(3)$$, all components at fixed degree mix by a Wigner matrix:

$$
Y^{(\ell)}(R\hat r)=D^{(\ell)}(R)Y^{(\ell)}(\hat r).
$$

The rotated function stays inside the same degree-$$\ell$$ subspace. That closure is exactly what a neural feature type needs.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s19-wigner-steering.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Angular function represented by spherical-harmonic coefficients before and after a three-dimensional rotation." caption="Rotating an angular function steers each fixed-ℓ coefficient vector by its Wigner D matrix. This is the angular transformation law used by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Thomas et al.</a>" %}

An operator $$\Phi:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y$$ is equivariant when

$$
\Phi\!\left(\rho_{\mathcal X}(g)x\right)
=\rho_{\mathcal Y}(g)\Phi(x).
$$

It is invariant when the output action is trivial. This commuting relation is the criterion that every architecture below is trying to satisfy.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s20-equivariance-commuting-diagram.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Geometric graph transformed before or after a neural map, producing the same transformed output." caption="Equivariance means that transforming the input before the model gives the same result as transforming the model's output afterward. <a href='https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/satorras21a.html'>EGNN</a> realizes this relation with invariant messages and equivariant coordinate differences." %}

## Invariant neural networks by scalarization

The first architectural strategy is to remove orientation before learning. Distances, bond angles, and suitable torsional encodings turn coordinate-sensitive inputs into scalars. An ordinary message-passing network can then operate on them without seeing an arbitrary coordinate frame.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s22-distance-angle-torsion.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Bond length, bond angle, and dihedral angle constructed from two, three, and four atoms." caption="Pair distances, three-body angles, and four-body dihedrals expose progressively richer local geometry while avoiding an arbitrary global orientation; <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08903'>GemNet</a> develops the corresponding two-hop construction." %}

There is a parity caveat hidden by the word *invariant*. Distances and ordinary bond angles are invariant under all of $$E(3)$$. A signed dihedral changes sign under reflection. An $$O(3)$$-invariant model must therefore use a reflection-even torsion encoding, such as cosine, or explicitly track parity. This becomes central again in NequIP.

### SchNet: continuous radial filters

SchNet uses distance as its edge-wise geometric feature. Hidden states are initialized from atom types, and radial basis functions encode $$r_{ij}=\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert$$. The interaction written in the source lecture is

$$
h_i^{(l+1)}
=(h^l*W^l)_i
=\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}h_j^l\odot W^l(r_{ij}).
$$

The name *continuous filter* comes from viewing $$W^l(r)$$ as a filter evaluated at continuous atomic locations rather than on a pixel grid. Formally, a continuous convolution can be written as

$$
\int_x W^l(x_i,x)\sum_{j\in\mathcal V}h_j\,\delta(x-x_j)\,dx.
$$

Replacing the integral over a point measure by a neighbor sum gives the practical layer above. Since the filter depends only on distance and the hidden features are scalars, every intermediate state is invariant.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s23-continuous-filter.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Discrete and continuous spatial filters evaluated as an atom moves." caption="A grid-bound filter produces stepwise changes as an atom crosses cells; SchNet's continuous radial filter changes smoothly with position. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08566'>Schütt et al.</a>" %}

The strength of this design is simplicity. The limitation is equally clear: a pair distance does not directly expose an angle or a torsion. Several layers may infer some higher-order structure indirectly, but the architecture does not make directional relations primitive.

### DimeNet: messages live on directed edges

DimeNet starts from the force-field decomposition and asks how to place angular information inside message passing. Potential energy can vary sharply with a bond angle or torsion even when individual bond lengths change little.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s24-torsional-energy.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Periodic torsional energy profile across conformations of a carbon chain." caption="A torsional potential is periodic and distinguishes conformations that pair distances alone may represent awkwardly. This directional motivation is explicit in <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03123'>DimeNet</a>." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s24-angular-energy.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Energy as a function of the H-O-H bond angle with molecular geometries along the curve." caption="Angular energy changes continuously with a three-atom geometry, motivating the explicit angle channel introduced by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03123'>DimeNet</a>." %}

The key change is that an embedding $$m_{ji}$$ belongs to the ordered atom pair $$(j,i)$$ and represents a message traveling from $$j$$ to $$i$$. Its update aggregates messages $$m_{kj}$$ entering $$j$$ from neighbors $$k$$ while using both a radial embedding and the angle $$\angle kji$$:

$$
m_{ji}^{(l+1)}
=f_{\mathrm{update}}\!\left(
m_{ji}^{(l)},
\sum_{k\in\mathcal N(j)\setminus\{i\}}
f_{\mathrm{int}}\!\left(
m_{kj}^{(l)},e_{\mathrm{RBF}}^{(ji)},a_{\mathrm{SBF}}^{(kj,ji)}
\right)
\right).
$$

The radial basis encodes distance and the spherical basis encodes the pair of distance and angle. This is message passing on the directed line graph: the computational objects are ordered bonds, and adjacency between them forms atom triplets. The construction is analogous to belief propagation because a message into $$j$$ is used to update a message leaving $$j$$ while excluding immediate backtracking through $$i$$.

### GemNet: torsions and two-hop geometry

Distances and bond angles do not always identify a unique three-dimensional arrangement. Four atoms can rotate around a central bond while keeping the adjacent bond lengths and angles fixed. A dihedral records the relative orientation of the two local planes.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s26-torsion-completeness.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Hydrogen peroxide geometry labeled with distances, two bond angles, and a torsion angle." caption="The torsion φ measures the rotation between the two bond planes of H₂O₂, completing the local description used by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08903'>GemNet</a> beyond distances and bond angles." %}

GemNet incorporates this information through two-hop neighborhoods. For a target directed bond $$a\to b$$, atoms $$c$$ neighboring $$a$$ and $$d$$ neighboring $$b$$ define angles such as $$\varphi_{cab}$$, $$\varphi_{abd}$$, and a torsional relation $$\theta_{cabd}$$. Learned radial, circular, and spherical basis embeddings are combined to update the directional message.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s27-gemnet-two-hop.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="GemNet two-hop directed bond neighborhood with two bond angles and a torsion." caption="GemNet couples the neighborhoods on both ends of a bond so that its message can depend on two bond angles and their relative rotation. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08903'>Gasteiger, Becker, and Günnemann</a>." %}

The lecture also highlights GemNet's universality argument. In nondegenerate geometry, an equivariant vector output at atom $$a$$ can be expanded in relative-position vectors,

$$
h_a(X,H)=\sum_{c\ne a}f_a^{(c)}(X,H)x_{ca},
$$

where each coefficient function is invariant to rotations and translations and equivariant to permutations. The geometric vectors carry the transformation law; invariant scalar functions supply the learnable coefficients. The nondegeneracy condition matters because the available relative vectors must span enough directions to express the output.

This scalar–vector product is the bridge to the next part. DimeNet and GemNet primarily learn invariant directional embeddings, but the same principle can build explicitly equivariant hidden states.

## Vector-based equivariant neural networks

A vector-based network keeps two channel families at layer $$l$$: invariant scalars $$s_i^l$$ and equivariant vectors $$v_i^l\in\mathbb R^{3\times d_v}$$. The scalar channels may pass through arbitrary nonlinear functions. The vector channels may only use operations that preserve their transformation law. Information can flow along four routes: scalar to scalar, vector to vector, scalar-gated vector, and vector-to-scalar through an invariant contraction.

The minimal safe operations include:

- any nonlinear function $$f(s)$$ of invariant scalars;
- scaling an equivariant vector by an invariant scalar, $$s\,v$$;
- linear mixing across vector channels, $$vW$$;
- invariant norms and inner products, $$\lVert v\rVert^2$$ and $$\langle v_1,v_2\rangle$$;
- sums of vectors;
- gradients of invariant scalar functions, such as $$-\nabla_XE$$.

A cross product also transforms correctly under proper rotations, but it is an axial vector: under reflection it picks up a different parity from an ordinary polar vector. A model advertised as $$E(3)$$- or $$O(3)$$-equivariant must keep that distinction rather than treating every three-component object as the same type.

### EGNN: invariant weights on relative vectors

EGNN uses three observations. First, translation cancels in a coordinate difference:

$$
(x_1+t)-(x_2+t)=x_1-x_2.
$$

Second, the difference rotates equivariantly. Third, multiplying it by an invariant weight preserves equivariance:

$$
x_1-x_2\longmapsto w(x_1-x_2),
\qquad
w=\phi(\lVert x_1-x_2\rVert).
$$

The layer then alternates invariant message computation with an equivariant coordinate update:

$$
m_{ij}=\phi_e\!\left(
h_i^l,h_j^l,\lVert x_i^l-x_j^l\rVert^2,a_{ij}
\right),
$$

$$
x_i^{l+1}
=x_i^l+C\sum_{j\ne i}(x_i^l-x_j^l)\phi_x(m_{ij}),
$$

$$
m_i=\sum_{j\ne i}m_{ij},
\qquad
h_i^{l+1}=\phi_h(h_i^l,m_i).
$$

The edge message and coefficient are invariant. Every term in the coordinate sum is an equivariant relative vector, so the update is equivariant. The node embedding remains invariant. EGNN is deliberately sparse in geometric types: it moves coordinates but does not maintain a general collection of vector channels.

### PaiNN: scalar and vector hidden channels

PaiNN keeps explicit scalar features $$s_i=[s_i^m,s_i^u]$$ and vector features $$\vec v_i=[\vec v_i^m,\vec v_i^u]$$. It uses residual interaction and mixing stages. In the interaction stage, the scalar update is a convolution with an invariant radial filter,

$$
\Delta s_i^m
=(\phi_s(s)*\mathcal W_s)_i
=\sum_j\phi_s(s_j)\odot\mathcal W_s(\lVert\vec r_{ij}\rVert).
$$

The vector update has two equivariant sources:

$$
\Delta\vec v_i^m
=\sum_j\vec v_j\odot\phi_{vv}(s_j)
\odot\mathcal W_{vv}(\lVert\vec r_{ij}\rVert)
$$

$$
\hspace{2.5cm}
+\sum_j\phi_{vs}(s_j)
\odot\nabla_{\vec r_{ij}}\mathcal W_{vs}(\lVert\vec r_{ij}\rVert).
$$

The first term filters existing vectors with invariant weights. The second differentiates an invariant radial function; its gradient points along the interatomic direction and is therefore equivariant. Residual updates give $$s_i^{m+1}=s_i^m+\Delta s_i^m$$ and $$\vec v_i^{m+1}=\vec v_i^m+\Delta\vec v_i^m$$.

The mixing stage first applies learned linear maps $$U$$ and $$V$$ across vector channels. Their norms and inner products are invariant, so they can modulate the scalar update:

$$
\Delta s_i^u
=a_{ss}\!\left(s_i,\lVert V\vec v_i\rVert\right)
+a_{sv}\!\left(s_i,\lVert V\vec v_i\rVert\right)
\left\langle U\vec v_i,V\vec v_i\right\rangle.
$$

The vector update gates an equivariant linear transform by an invariant coefficient:

$$
\Delta\vec v_i^u
=a_{vv}\!\left(s_i,\lVert V\vec v_i\rVert\right)U\vec v_i.
$$

PaiNN can propagate polarization-like directional responses much more directly than a scalar network. Its restriction is that every non-scalar channel is an ordinary vector. Spherical-tensor networks remove that restriction.

## Spherical tensor features

The fourth part of the lecture replaces the scalar–vector split by types $$\ell=0,1,\ldots,L$$. A type-$$\ell$$ feature has $$2\ell+1$$ components and transforms by $$D^{(\ell)}(R)$$. Type zero is scalar. Type one is vector-like after a basis change. Types two and above carry angular patterns that cannot be represented by a single ordinary vector.

A layer may keep several channels of every type at every atom. Linear maps may freely mix channels within the same type, but they cannot mix representation components arbitrarily. The central bilinear operation is the Clebsch–Gordan tensor product, which combines two input types and decomposes their product into valid output types.

### Irreducible representations are the feature types

The reason to use irreducible representations is compactness. A representation $$\rho$$ is reducible when one shared change of basis $$Q$$ makes every matrix block diagonal:

$$
\rho(g)=Q^{-1}
\begin{pmatrix}
\rho_1(g)&0\\
0&\rho_2(g)
\end{pmatrix}Q
=Q^{-1}(\rho_1(g)\oplus\rho_2(g))Q.
$$

Repeating the decomposition yields irreducible pieces that cannot be split further. A steerable feature can then be categorized by how it responds to rotation, and redundant transformation structure is removed.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s38-rank-two-decomposition.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Rank-two Cartesian tensor decomposed into trace, antisymmetric, and traceless symmetric blocks." caption="A nine-component rank-two Cartesian tensor decomposes into irreducible blocks of dimensions 1, 3, and 5: trace, antisymmetric, and symmetric traceless. See the tensor discussion in <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511'>Duval et al.</a>" %}

For $$SO(2)$$, the constant function is a one-dimensional irrep and each nonzero circular frequency is a two-dimensional real irrep. Collecting frequencies gives a block-diagonal representation,

$$
1\oplus\rho_1(\theta)\oplus\rho_2(\theta)\oplus\rho_3(\theta)\oplus\cdots.
$$

An arbitrary linear combination across frequencies is not itself one irrep. For example, combining $$\cos\alpha$$ with $$\sin2\alpha$$ mixes two blocks with different rotation speeds. Channels may combine copies of the same type; different types must remain identifiable.

For $$SO(3)$$, the irreducible representations are the Wigner matrices

$$
D^{(\ell)}:SO(3)\to\mathbb R^{(2\ell+1)\times(2\ell+1)},
\qquad
D^{(\ell)}(g)=\left[D_{mn}^{(\ell)}(g)\right]_{m,n=-\ell}^{\ell}.
$$

A general finite representation decomposes into a direct sum of these blocks. The first dimensions are $$1,3,5,7$$ for $$\ell=0,1,2,3$$. This is why a type label is more precise than merely saying that a feature is "geometric."

## Tensor products and Clebsch–Gordan decomposition

A tensor product multiplies two feature spaces. If $$a$$ and $$b$$ transform under $$\rho_a$$ and $$\rho_b$$, then their outer product is steerable:

$$
a\otimes b
\longmapsto
(\rho_a(g)\otimes\rho_b(g))(a\otimes b).
$$

The exact Kronecker order depends on the vectorization convention; the source slides write the corresponding action with $$D^{(\ell_b)}(g^{-1})\otimes D^{(\ell_a)}(g)$$. Either way, the product has a valid group representation. The problem is that this representation is usually reducible and therefore has no single type.

A Clebsch–Gordan change of basis block-diagonalizes the product into Wigner representations:

$$
Q(\rho_{\ell_1}(g)\otimes\rho_{\ell_2}(g))Q^{-1}
=\bigoplus_{\ell=|\ell_1-\ell_2|}^{\ell_1+\ell_2}D^{(\ell)}(g).
$$

The Clebsch–Gordan tensor product means "take the ordinary product, then express it in this irreducible basis."

### Component form

Let $$v^{(\ell_1)}\in V_{\ell_1}$$ and $$v^{(\ell_2)}\in V_{\ell_2}$$. The output component of degree $$\ell$$ and order $$m$$ is

$$
\left(v^{(\ell_1)}\otimes_{\mathrm{CG}}v^{(\ell_2)}\right)_m^{(\ell)}
=
\sum_{m_1=-\ell_1}^{\ell_1}
\sum_{m_2=-\ell_2}^{\ell_2}
C_{(\ell_1,m_1)(\ell_2,m_2)}^{(\ell,m)}
v_{m_1}^{(\ell_1)}v_{m_2}^{(\ell_2)}.
$$

Each coefficient maps one pair of representation components $$(\ell_1,m_1)$$ and $$(\ell_2,m_2)$$ into an allowed output component $$(\ell,m)$$. Nonzero couplings obey the triangle rule

$$
|\ell_1-\ell_2|\le\ell\le\ell_1+\ell_2
$$

and, in the usual basis, the order-selection rule $$m=m_1+m_2$$. The coefficients are fixed by representation theory. Learned weights mix channels and choose how much of each permitted path the network uses.

### Cartesian tensors as a comparison

The ordinary Cartesian construction is familiar. A vector $$x$$ is order one. Its outer product $$A=xx^\top=x\otimes x$$ is order two, and $$x\otimes x\otimes x$$ is order three. Flattening only changes storage; it does not change the transformation law.

Flattening makes matrix multiplication convenient but leaves the underlying representation reducible. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511'>Duval et al.</a>" %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s47-cartesian-block-representation.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Block sizes for scalar, vector, and rank-two Cartesian rotation representations." caption="Successive Cartesian orders occupy blocks whose dimensions grow as 1, 3, 9, and so on, before the irreducible decomposition reviewed by <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511'>Duval et al.</a>" %}

If $$x\mapsto R_gx$$, then

$$
g\cdot A
=(g\cdot x)(g\cdot x)^\top
=R_gxx^\top R_g^\top
=R_gAR_g^\top,
$$

and therefore

$$
\operatorname{vec}(g\cdot A)
=(R_g\otimes R_g)\operatorname{vec}(A).
$$

For order three,

$$
\operatorname{vec}(g\cdot T)
=(R_g\otimes R_g\otimes R_g)\operatorname{vec}(T).
$$

Tensor products can also lower order through contraction. With $$A=xx^\top$$, the product $$Ax=xx^\top x$$ is again a vector. "Tensor product" is thus the raw multiplication mechanism; the chosen contractions and decompositions determine the output type.

For two ordinary vectors, the nine-dimensional product decomposes as

$$
3\otimes3=1\oplus3\oplus5,
$$

corresponding to $$\ell=0,1,2$$: the trace, antisymmetric part, and symmetric traceless part. Some source-slide wording counts Cartesian tensor order as "type 1, 2, 3"; throughout this article, *type* means the spherical degree $$\ell$$, so the irreducible dimensions are unambiguously $$1,3,5$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s50-rank-two-irreps.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A rank-two tensor rearranged into trace, antisymmetric, and symmetric traceless irreducible parts." caption="A basis change reorganizes the same nine Cartesian entries into ℓ=0, ℓ=1, and ℓ=2 blocks. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07511'>Duval et al.</a>" %}

This returns us to the Clebsch–Gordan formula: it performs exactly this product-plus-decomposition for arbitrary spherical input degrees.

## Tensor Field Networks

Tensor Field Networks were among the first neural architectures to operate systematically on steerable features at geometric points. The word *field* means that a collection of tensors is attached to every point or atom. Convolutions couple hidden features to geometric filters through Clebsch–Gordan products; type-preserving nonlinearities and self-interactions complete the layer.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s53-harmonic-edge-featurization.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Relative atomic direction projected into spherical harmonics of degrees zero, one, and two." caption="An edge direction is normalized onto the sphere and expanded in spherical harmonics, producing one typed geometric feature for each degree. This is the angular construction of <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Thomas et al.</a>" %}

### Hidden-feature indices

The source notation is $$V_{acm}^{(\ell)}$$:

- $$\ell$$ is the spherical degree and selects the Wigner representation;
- $$a$$ is the point or atom index;
- $$c$$ is a channel index, analogous to channels in an ordinary neural network;
- $$m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell$$ is the representation-component index.

The slide calls $$m$$ the "degree of spherical harmonics," but degree is $$\ell$$; $$m$$ is the order/component within that degree. Keeping those roles separate avoids a common source of indexing errors.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s54-tfn-feature-indices.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tensor Field Network feature dictionary labeling point, channel, degree, and representation-component indices." caption="A TFN feature tensor separates location (a), channel (c), degree ℓ, and component (m). The example stores velocities and accelerations as separate type-one channels. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Thomas et al.</a>" %}

### Radial functions times spherical harmonics

For a relative displacement $$\vec r$$, the filter separates radius and direction:

$$
F_{cm}^{(\ell_f,\ell_i)}(\vec r)
=R_c^{(\ell_f,\ell_i)}(r)Y_m^{(\ell_f)}(\hat r),
\qquad
r=\lVert\vec r\rVert,
\qquad
\hat r=\frac{\vec r}{\lVert\vec r\rVert}.
$$

The radial function is learned and invariant. The spherical harmonic is fixed and has type $$\ell_f$$. Multiplying them produces a steerable filter. The extra $$\ell_i$$ index allows the radial network to depend on the input type that the filter will couple to.

### Equivariant convolution

The message-passing layer takes a Clebsch–Gordan product between the type-$$\ell_f$$ filter and a type-$$\ell_i$$ hidden feature, then aggregates over neighboring atoms:

$$
\mathcal L_{acm_o}^{(\ell_o)}
\!\left(\vec r_a,V_{acm_i}^{(\ell_i)}\right)
:=
\sum_{m_f,m_i}
C_{(\ell_f,m_f)(\ell_i,m_i)}^{(\ell_o,m_o)}
\sum_{b\in\mathcal S}
F_{cm_f}^{(\ell_f,\ell_i)}(\vec r_{ab})
V_{bcm_i}^{(\ell_i)}.
$$

The output has type $$\ell_o$$. TFN instantiates every allowed coupling satisfying

$$
|\ell_f-\ell_i|\le\ell_o\le\ell_f+\ell_i.
$$

The neighbor sum is permutation invariant over atoms; the Clebsch–Gordan contraction guarantees the rotation law.

### Nonlinearities and self-interactions

For $$\ell=0$$, an ordinary scalar activation is admissible:

$$
V_{ac}^{(0)}\longmapsto
\eta^{(0)}\!\left(V_{ac}^{(0)}+b_c^{(0)}\right).
$$

For $$\ell>0$$, TFN uses the invariant norm

$$
\lVert V\rVert_{ac}^{(\ell)}
:=
\sqrt{\sum_m\left|V_{acm}^{(\ell)}\right|^2}
$$

to generate a scalar gate:

$$
V_{acm}^{(\ell)}\longmapsto
\eta^{(\ell)}\!\left(\lVert V\rVert_{ac}^{(\ell)}+b_c^{(\ell)}\right)
V_{acm}^{(\ell)}.
$$

The gate is invariant, so it rescales all components together and preserves the type. A self-interaction layer mixes channels at a fixed point only within matching types, analogous to a $$1\times1$$ convolution.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-05/s58-tfn-architecture.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Tensor Field Network architecture with type-zero and type-one convolutions, self interactions, nonlinearities, concatenation, pooling, and classification." caption="TFN alternates typed convolutions, within-type channel mixing, and admissible nonlinearities before reducing to invariant type-zero outputs. From <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08219'>Thomas et al.</a>" %}

The resulting architecture is no longer an ordinary MPNN decorated with angles. Every hidden feature declares its rotation type, every bilinear interaction follows a legal coupling, and every nonlinearity respects that type.

## NequIP for interatomic potentials

NequIP applies a TFN-like architecture to interatomic potentials. It predicts atom-wise invariant energy contributions,

$$
E_{\mathrm{pot}}=\sum_{i\in N_{\mathrm{atoms}}}E_{i,\mathrm{atomic}},
\qquad
\vec F_i=-\nabla_iE_{\mathrm{pot}}.
$$

The sum gives an invariant total energy, and differentiating it gives conservative equivariant forces. Translation invariance follows because interactions depend on relative positions. For physical interatomic potentials, reflection is also relevant, so the architecture is designed for $$O(3)$$ rather than only $$SO(3)$$.

### Parity extends the type label

NequIP labels a feature by $$(\ell,p)$$, where $$p\in\{+1,-1\}$$ records reflection behavior. Even features satisfy

$$
V_{acm}^{(\ell,+1)}\longmapsto V_{acm}^{(\ell,+1)},
$$

while odd features satisfy

$$
V_{acm}^{(\ell,-1)}\longmapsto- V_{acm}^{(\ell,-1)}
$$

under inversion. Degree-one coordinate vectors have odd parity. The cross product of two odd polar vectors is an even axial vector. Two three-component features can therefore have the same $$SO(3)$$ rotation law and different $$O(3)$$ types.

### Filters and radial basis

NequIP again separates radial and angular dependence:

$$
S_m^{(\ell)}(\vec r_{ij})
=R(r_{ij})Y_m^{(\ell)}(\hat r_{ij}).
$$

The radial coefficient is produced by a neural network from a cutoff-aware basis,

$$
R(r_{ij})
=W_n\sigma\!\left(\cdots\sigma\!\left(W_2\sigma\!\left(W_1B(r_{ij})\right)\right)\right),
$$

$$
B_b(r_{ij})
=\frac{2}{r_c}
\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{b\pi}{r_c}r_{ij}\right)}{r_{ij}}
f_{\mathrm{env}}(r_{ij},r_c).
$$

The sine term is a Bessel-like radial basis and $$f_{\mathrm{env}}$$ is a polynomial envelope, following the smooth cutoff construction used in DimeNet. The envelope drives the interaction smoothly to zero at $$r_c$$.

### Parity-aware Clebsch–Gordan updates

With channel, degree, and parity indices exposed, the layer can be written as

$$
\mathcal L_{acm_o}^{\ell_o,p_o,\ell_f,p_f,\ell_i,p_i}
\!\left(\vec r_a,V_{acm_i}^{\ell_i,p_i}\right)
=
\sum_{m_f,m_i}
C_{\ell_i,m_i,\ell_f,m_f}^{\ell_o,m_o}
\sum_{b\in\mathcal S}
R(r_{ab})_{c,\ell_o,p_o,\ell_f,p_f,\ell_i,p_i}
Y_{m_f}^{\ell_f}(\hat r_{ab})
V_{bcm_i}^{\ell_i,p_i}.
$$

An output path is present only when both selection rules hold:

$$
|\ell_f-\ell_i|\le\ell_o\le\ell_f+\ell_i,
\qquad
p_ip_f=p_o.
$$

The first is the Clebsch–Gordan triangle rule. The second says that parities multiply. This is the precise extension from rotational equivariance to reflection-aware equivariance.

The tables complete the architectural story. Scalarization gives an invariant model with a deliberately compressed view of geometry. Vector networks preserve one nontrivial representation and can express directional response efficiently. Spherical-tensor networks retain a controlled hierarchy of angular types. Tensor Field Networks supply the general convolutional machinery, and NequIP specializes it to a reflection-aware energy model whose gradients are physical forces. The common principle is not merely "use 3D coordinates." It is to decide how every quantity should transform, then permit only computations compatible with that decision.
