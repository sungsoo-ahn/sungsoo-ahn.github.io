## Three failures of deep message passing

A message-passing neural network (MPNN) repeatedly aggregates a multiset of neighboring states and combines the result with the state at the receiving node. In the lecture's notation,

$$
h_v^{(t)}=operatorname{com}^{(t)}\!\left(
h_v^{(t-1)},
\operatorname{agg}^{(t)}\!\left(
\left\{\!\left\{(h_u^{(t-1)},(u,v)\in E)\right\}\!\right\}
\right)
\right).
$$

The shared aggregate-and-combine rule makes the model permutation equivariant, but it also ties information flow to the computational graph. On large graphs, depth exposes three different failures.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s04-three-failures.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three diagrams contrasting under-reaching, over-smoothing, and over-squashing." caption="Three different failures can appear as an MPNN becomes deeper: insufficient reach, loss of node distinctions, and compression through a bottleneck. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 4." %}

**Under-reaching** means that the model is not deep enough for relevant information to arrive. After $$L$$ layers, the state at $$v$$ can depend only on the $$L$$-hop neighborhood of $$v$$. If the shortest path from $$u$$ to $$v$$ is longer than $$L$$, then

$$
\frac{\partial h_v^{(L)}}{\partial h_u^{(0)}}=0.
$$

In principle, increasing depth fixes reach. It may then create either of the other two failures.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s05-under-reaching.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A message-passing path stopping before a distant green node." caption="A finite layer count imposes a hard graph-distance boundary on information flow. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 5." %}

**Over-smoothing** means that node representations become increasingly similar, weakening the influence of graph structure on node-level predictions. This is a property of the propagation dynamics: a repeatedly applied low-pass operator suppresses high-frequency variation. It can therefore be mitigated by changing the update rule even when the input topology stays fixed.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s06-over-smoothing.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A colorful graph whose node states become uniformly blue after propagation." caption="Repeated propagation can erase distinctions among node states. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 6." %}

**Over-squashing** means that a fixed-width state must compress too many distant signals. The number of nodes inside an $$L$$-hop neighborhood can grow exponentially, while the number and width of edges crossing a narrow cut stay fixed. This problem is induced by topology rather than by a particular message function.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s07-over-squashing.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A branching graph whose many leaves communicate through a narrow bottleneck." caption="A rapidly expanding receptive field is compressed through a narrow graph bottleneck. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 7." %}

The distinctions matter. Adding depth addresses under-reaching, but can intensify smoothing. Changing the propagation dynamics addresses smoothing, but cannot create missing routes across a topological bottleneck. Rewiring addresses the bottleneck, but may alter the semantics of the graph.

## Measuring and mitigating over-smoothing

Suppose a depth-$$N$$ network evolves the node-feature matrix through

$$
X^n=\sigma\!\left(F_\theta(X^{n-1},\mathcal G)\right),
\qquad n=1,\ldots,N.
$$

The lecture defines over-smoothing as exponential decay in a node-similarity measure $$\mu$$:

$$
\mu(X^n)\le C_1e^{-C_2n},
\qquad n=1,\ldots,N.
$$

The measure is required to satisfy two basic axioms:

$$
\mu(X)=0\iff X\text{ is constant},
\qquad
\mu(X+Y)\le \mu(X)+\mu(Y).
$$

One common graph-local choice is the Dirichlet energy. For $$v=|V|$$ nodes,

$$
\mathcal E(X^n)
=\frac1v\sum_{i\in V}\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}
\left\|X_i^n-X_j^n\right\|_2^2.
$$

It vanishes when neighboring states are identical. Another candidate is the mean average distance (MAD),

$$
\operatorname{MAD}(X^n)
=\frac1v\sum_i\sum_j
\left(
1-\frac{(X_i^n)^\top X_j^n}
{\|X_i^n\|_2\|X_j^n\|_2}
\right),
$$

which is a valid node-similarity measure when the features are normalized, $$\|X_i^n\|_2=1$$. The two diagnostics are not interchangeable. Dirichlet energy measures variation across graph edges; MAD measures global angular separation among node states.

For randomly initialized GNNs, both measures often decay approximately exponentially, but the rate depends strongly on the architecture and graph.

### Residual paths and normalization

The first mitigation family preserves earlier information through residual paths. A standard residual update is

$$
X^n=X^{n-1}+F_\theta(X^{n-1},\mathcal G).
$$

GCNII additionally injects the initial node features at every layer. In the notation used in the lecture,

$$
X^n=X^{n-1}+
\sigma\!\left(
\big((1-\alpha)LX^n+\alpha X^0\big)
\big((1-\beta)I+\beta W\big)
\right).
$$

Jumping Knowledge networks and deep adaptive GNNs follow the same broad principle: the final prediction should not depend only on the most deeply diffused representation.

The second family directly regularizes or renormalizes node features. Energetic GNNs penalize deviations of the Dirichlet energy during training. PairNorm first centers the states and then fixes their root-mean-square norm,

$$
\widehat X_i=X_i-\frac1v\sum_{j=1}^{v}X_j,
\qquad
X_i\leftarrow
\frac{s\widehat X_i}
{\sqrt{\frac1v\sum_{j=1}^{v}\|\widehat X_j\|_2^2}}.
$$

DropEdge instead adds noise to optimization by randomly masking edges. These methods act on different objects: the loss, the node states, or the graph used during training.

The third family changes the dynamics. Graph-Coupled Oscillator Networks (GraphCON) use the second-order continuous-time system

$$
X''=\sigma(F_\theta(X))-\gamma X-\alpha X'.
$$

The analogy is a system of coupled masses and springs. Diffusion irreversibly suppresses variation; an oscillator can propagate it while preserving a suitable energy.

Mitigation is therefore not automatically beneficial. The scientifically relevant question is not whether a representation remains diverse, but whether it preserves and transforms the information needed by the prediction problem.

## Over-squashing through sensitivity and curvature

Over-squashing is intrinsic to the graph topology and cannot generally be removed by replacing one shared message function with another. This motivates a larger question: should the computational graph be decoupled from the input graph? Curvature-based rewiring, graph Transformers, and dynamic edge addition all answer yes in different ways.

The first analysis measures information flow through sensitivity. Consider

$$
h_v^{(t)}=operatorname{com}^{(t)}\!\left(
h_v^{(t-1)},
\sum_u\widehat A_{vu}\,
\operatorname{mss}^{(t)}(h_v^{(t-1)},h_u^{(t-1)})
\right).
$$

Let $$d(u,v)=r$$. If the Jacobians of the combine and message functions are bounded by $$\alpha$$ and $$\beta$$ through the first $$r$$ layers, then

$$
\left\|
\frac{\partial h_v^{(r)}}{\partial h_u^{(0)}}
\right\|_\infty
\le (\alpha\beta)^r(\widehat A^r)_{vu}.
$$

The model term $$(\alpha\beta)^r$$ and the topological term $$(\widehat A^r)_{vu}$$ are separated. Even an expressive local update cannot overcome a vanishing path weight imposed by the graph.

On the binary-tree example used in the lecture,

$$
(\widehat A^r)_{vu}=\frac12\,3^{-(r-1)}.
$$

The influence of one leaf on the root decays exponentially as the number of leaves grows. This is the Tree-Neighbors Match setting: enough layers make the root reachable, yet its state must still compress an expanding collection of sources.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s20-binary-tree.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Binary tree with one highlighted leaf and a root joined through a narrow hierarchy." caption="The binary-tree example isolates topological over-squashing after reach has been satisfied. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 20." %}

The next step is to identify which edges create the bottleneck. The lecture introduces a discrete curvature map $$(u,v)\mapsto\operatorname{Ric}(u,v)$$ determined by degrees, triangles, and four-cycles around an edge. Representative values are:

| Graph family | Balanced Forman curvature |
| --- | ---: |
| Cycle $$C_{n\ge5}$$ | $$0$$ |
| Grid $$G_n$$ | $$0$$ |
| Clique $$K_n$$ | $$\dfrac{n}{n-1}$$ |
| Regular tree $$T_r$$ | $$\dfrac{4}{r+1}-2$$ |

For a simple unweighted graph, the full expression used in the source analysis is

$$
\operatorname{Ric}(i,j)
=\frac{2}{d_i}+\frac{2}{d_j}-2
+2\frac{|\#_\triangle(i,j)|}{\max(d_i,d_j)}
+\frac{|\#_\triangle(i,j)|}{\min(d_i,d_j)}
+\frac{(\gamma_{\max})^{-1}}
{\max(d_i,d_j)}
\left(|\#_\square^i|+|\#_\square^j|\right).
$$

Here $$\#_\triangle(i,j)$$ counts triangles based at the edge, while the final term accounts for nondegenerate four-cycles through the endpoints. The curvature is set to zero when $$\min(d_i,d_j)=1$$, and the four-cycle term is zero when its associated count vanishes. In particular, $$\operatorname{Ric}(i,j)>-2$$. The informal theorem in the lecture is that severe over-squashing occurs along edges of high negative curvature.

### Ricci-curvature intuition

On continuous spaces, positive, zero, and negative curvature correspond to spherical, Euclidean, and hyperbolic geometry. Nearby geodesics tend to converge, remain parallel, or diverge.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s22-curvature-manifolds.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Spherical, Euclidean, and hyperbolic surfaces representing positive, zero, and negative curvature." caption="The three prototypical curvature regimes. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 22." %}

Ricci curvature compares how local neighborhoods separate. Starting at nearby points with parallel velocity, positive curvature contracts the separation, zero curvature preserves it, and negative curvature expands it.

### Rewiring as a surgical intervention

Negative curvature gives a local diagnosis: find the bottleneck edges and change the topology around them. Adding a route across a narrow cut reduces the amount of information forced through one edge; removing an excessively high-curvature edge can avoid over-clustering.

This is a computational intervention, not automatically a scientifically valid one. In a molecular graph, road network, or relational database, a shortcut edge may improve optimization while contradicting the meaning of the original relation. Rewiring must therefore be judged both as an information-flow operation and as a modeling assumption.

## Width, commute time, and dynamic rewiring

The second over-squashing analysis asks three questions left open by the local curvature view:

1. How does hidden width affect over-squashing?
2. Can sufficient depth eliminate it?
3. How is it related to global graph topology and spectrum, beyond two-hop curvature bounds?

The setting is an isotropic MPNN,

$$
h_v^{(t)}=\sigma\!\left(
c_rW_r^{(t)}h_v^{(t-1)}
+c_aW_a^{(t)}\sum_u A_{vu}h_u^{(t-1)}
\right).
$$

Here $$\sigma$$ is pointwise, the $$W^{(t)}$$ are learned, and $$c_r,c_a\ge0$$ control the self and aggregation terms. The message-passing matrix is typically chosen from

$$
A\in\left\{D^{-1}A,\ A,\ D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}\right\},
$$

covering standard GCN-, GraphSAGE-, and GIN-like cases.

Let $$c_\sigma$$ be the Lipschitz constant of $$\sigma$$, $$w$$ the largest absolute entry across the weight matrices, and $$p$$ the hidden width. For an $$m$$-layer model,

$$
\left\|
\frac{\partial h_v^{(m)}}{\partial h_u^{(0)}}
\right\|_{L_1}
\le
\underbrace{(c_\sigma wp)^m}_{\text{model}}
\underbrace{(c_rI+c_aA)^m_{vu}}_{\text{topology}}.
$$

Width can increase the model term, but at the cost of more parameters and possible over-fitting. The graph-dependent term remains. The lecture's synthetic experiments show precisely this interaction.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s31-width-sensitivity.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Accuracy against source-target distance for several hidden widths on CrossedRing, Ring, and CliquePath graphs." caption="Width delays sensitivity collapse, but the graph family still determines how quickly long-range accuracy fails. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 31." %}

Shortest-path distance alone is not an adequate definition of “long range.” The lecture compares three graph-transfer tasks with the same source-target distance $$r$$:

$$
(c_rI+c_aA)^r_{vu}
=\left(\frac12\right)^{r-1}
\quad\text{for Ring},
$$

$$
(c_rI+c_aA)^r_{vu}
=\left(\frac23\right)^{r-1}
\quad\text{for CrossedRing},
$$

and

$$
(c_rI+c_aA)^r_{vu}
=\left(\frac12\right)^{r-1}
\frac{2}{r\sqrt{r-2}}
\quad\text{for CliquePath}.
$$

CrossedRing supplies more routes than Ring, while CliquePath introduces a severe bottleneck. GIN, GraphSAGE, GCN, and GAT all reflect the same topological ordering even though their numerical failure points differ.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s32-long-range-sensitivity.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="GIN, GraphSAGE, GCN, and GAT accuracy versus source-target distance on CliquePath, Ring, and CrossedRing." caption="Hop distance is held comparable, yet sensitivity differs because the number and quality of connecting routes differ. Original PowerPoint figure from slides 32–33." %}

A global way to measure those routes is a random walk. At node $$v$$, the walk moves to each neighbor with probability $$1/d_v$$. The commute time $$\tau(v,u)$$ is the expected number of steps required to reach $$u$$ from $$v$$ and return. It is related to effective resistance by

$$
\operatorname{Res}(v,u)=\frac{\tau(v,u)}{2|E|}.
$$

Spatial rewiring indiscriminately adds local edges. Spectral rewiring can target the routes that most reduce commute time.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s34-commute-time-rewiring.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Base, spatially rewired, and spectrally rewired graphs colored by commute time." caption="Commute time summarizes all routes between nodes and motivates topology-aware rewiring. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 34." %}

Under the normalization and ReLU setting used in the source analysis, the informal theorem is that the expected obstruction to message exchange is approximated by

$$
\frac{\tau(v,u)}{2|E|}=\operatorname{Res}(v,u).
$$

This leads to three consequences from the lecture:

- Suppressing messages from high-resistance nodes can be useful when the task is genuinely local.
- Over-squashing is harmful when the target depends on interactions between nodes at high effective resistance.
- Effective resistance is a topology-dependent obstruction that does not disappear merely by adding layers.

### Rewiring over depth

Static rewiring adds every shortcut before propagation. If all nodes within a fixed radius are connected immediately, the graph becomes dense, training becomes expensive, and the original distance-based inductive bias is weakened. Early long-range mixing also makes over-smoothing more likely.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s36-dynamic-rewiring.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Concentric distance neighborhoods around a central node with edges appearing at increasing radii." caption="Dynamic rewiring adds communication radii progressively instead of densifying the graph at the first layer. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 36." %}

Dynamic Rewiring (DRew) separates graph distance from propagation time. The classical MPNN communicates only one hop per layer; DRew opens longer edges as depth increases; $$\nu$$-DRew can additionally delay the source state used by a long-range message.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/s37-drew-delay.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three layer diagrams comparing classical MPNN, DRew, and delayed nu-DRew communication." caption="Classical propagation, distance-dependent edge addition, and distance-dependent delay. Original PowerPoint figure from slide 37." %}

Define

$$
\tau_\nu(k)=\max(0,k-\nu).
$$

The distance-$$k$$ aggregate at layer $$t$$ is

$$
a_{v,k}^{(t-1)}
=\operatorname{agg}_k^{(t)}\!\left(
\left\{h_u^{(t-1-\tau_\nu(k))}:u\in\mathcal N_k(v)\right\}
\right),
\qquad 1\le k\le t,
$$

and the node update combines every distance available at that depth,

$$
h_v^{(t)}
=\operatorname{com}^{(t)}\!\left(
h_v^{(t-1)},a_{v,1}^{(t-1)},\ldots,a_{v,t}^{(t-1)}
\right).
$$

No delay corresponds to $$\nu\to\infty$$. Both the aggregation and combine maps may carry parameters indexed by the layer $$t$$ and distance $$k$$.

The graph is filled progressively, so closer nodes still interact earlier. A larger delay can also mitigate over-smoothing: node $$v$$ receives a state from $$u$$ before that state has been repeatedly smoothed by every intervening propagation layer.

## A critical audit of GNN research

The lecture does not end with the strongest rewiring result. It turns to the research ecosystem around GNNs and gives a warning: a large paper count does not establish that common tasks, graph constructions, or empirical claims are scientifically sound.

| Critique | Immediate recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Few transformative real-world applications | Shift attention toward natural graph problems with measurable impact, such as combinatorial optimization and relational databases; improve conditional diffusion models for combinatorial tasks. |
| Graphs constructed without meaningful semantics | Explain why the relation should be represented as a graph and compare with unstructured-set baselines. |
| Weak benchmarking culture | Move beyond marginal leaderboard gains toward practical tasks, standard splits, controlled tuning, and robust evaluation frameworks. |
| No convincing graph foundation model | Move from one model per dataset toward coherent multi-dataset training, supported by large, high-quality collections with diverse graph structures. |

### Missing applications and graphs without meaning

Many GNN studies develop around niche benchmarks, including property prediction from 2D molecular graphs, without demonstrating a transformative deployment. The lecture argues for natural graph problems whose relational structure and outcomes matter outside the benchmark.

Even when a dataset is important, its graph may be artificial. A threshold, $$k$$-nearest-neighbor rule, or superpixel construction can create edges mainly to test an architectural hypothesis. The resulting benchmark measures adaptation to that construction, not necessarily a meaningful relation in the underlying data.

### Why graph foundation-model claims remain tentative

Graph learning still lacks a counterpart to the broadly transferable foundation models seen in language and vision. Graph datasets vary in node semantics, edge semantics, task scale, and even in what constitutes a graph. Domain-specific pretraining can help, but the benefit is endpoint dependent.

The lecture's final experiment pretrains on COCO-SP, MalNetTiny, and PCQM4Mv2, then fine-tunes on PascalVOC-SP, Peptides-struct, and Stargazers. The upstream results separate random initialization (RD), single-task training (ST), and multi-task training (MT).
