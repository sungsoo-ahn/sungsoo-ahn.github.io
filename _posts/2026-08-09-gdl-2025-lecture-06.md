## Three failures of deep message passing

An MPNN layer applies a permutation-invariant aggregation of neighbor states followed by a shared combine function. Stacking layers expands the nominal receptive field by one graph hop at a time.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-3.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Formal message-passing neural network update." caption="The lecture begins from the shared aggregate-and-combine MPNN formalism. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 3." %}

Large graphs expose three different failures. Under-reaching means too few layers for information to arrive. Over-smoothing means node states lose their distinctions after repeated propagation. Over-squashing means a fixed-width state must compress too many distant signals even when enough layers are present.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-4.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Under-reaching, over-smoothing, and over-squashing on graphs." caption="Reach, state collapse, and information bottlenecks are separate pathologies with similar depth-related symptoms. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 4." %}

Under-reaching is geometric. With $$L$$ message-passing layers, a node can depend only on its $$L$$-hop neighborhood. No optimizer or feature dimension can transmit a signal along a longer shortest path without changing depth or connectivity.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-5.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Message-passing receptive field failing to reach a distant node." caption="A finite layer count imposes a hard graph-distance boundary on information flow. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 5." %}

Over-smoothing is representational. Deep propagation can make node embeddings similar or even identical across connected components, although the exact outcome depends on the update dynamics and architecture.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-6.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Node colors becoming indistinguishable after repeated graph propagation." caption="Over-smoothing describes loss of node-level distinction under repeated message passing. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 6." %}

Over-squashing is topological. The number of nodes in an $$L$$-hop neighborhood can grow exponentially while each edge and hidden state retains fixed capacity. Distant information must pass through narrow cuts.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Exponential receptive field compressed into a fixed-size node representation." caption="Over-squashing compresses a rapidly growing receptive field through fixed-width graph bottlenecks. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 7." %}

## Measuring and mitigating over-smoothing

The first difficulty is definitional: one needs a measure of smoothing, a criterion for excessive smoothing, and a decision about whether the relevant object is the normalized graph Laplacian, the propagation dynamics, or the residual architecture.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Questions required to define over-smoothing." caption="Over-smoothing claims depend on a representation-distance measure and on the propagation process being studied. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 8." %}

For node embeddings $$X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$$, the Dirichlet energy

$$
E(X)=\operatorname{tr}(X^\top LX)
=\frac12\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\lVert x_u-x_v\rVert^2
$$

measures variation across edges. Exponential decay of a normalized energy across layers formalizes one notion of over-smoothing.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-9.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Dirichlet-energy definition of graph over-smoothing." caption="Dirichlet energy tracks how much neighboring node states differ. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 9." %}

Mean average distance provides another candidate by averaging pairwise differences among normalized node embeddings. It is a valid similarity measure, but unlike a graph Dirichlet energy it is not tied to graph edges or required to vanish at a particular scale.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-10.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Mean average distance measure for node embeddings." caption="Global pairwise embedding distance and edge-local Dirichlet energy measure different aspects of state collapse. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 10." %}

Experiments with randomly initialized weights show that many GNNs drive these measures downward with depth, often approximately exponentially, but at architecture-dependent rates.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Empirical decay of smoothing measures across GNN layers." caption="Different propagation rules exhibit different empirical smoothing dynamics even before task training. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 11." %}

### Residual paths and normalization

The first mitigation family preserves earlier information through residual connections. A GCNII-style update injects the initial features at every layer, while Jumping Knowledge aggregates representations from several depths and standard residual networks add the previous state.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Residual, initial-residual, and Jumping Knowledge mitigation of over-smoothing." caption="Skip pathways preserve shallower node information instead of relying only on the deepest diffused state. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 12." %}

The second family modifies the embeddings directly. PairNorm rescales node states to maintain global spread, while DropEdge randomly removes edges during training and weakens repeated mixing.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="PairNorm and DropEdge methods for reducing over-smoothing." caption="Normalization and stochastic edge removal alter the state or graph on which smoothing occurs. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 13." %}

The third family changes the propagation operator itself. Graph-Coupled Oscillator Networks introduce second-order continuous-time dynamics designed to preserve Dirichlet energy rather than monotonically diffuse it.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph-coupled oscillator network and energy-preserving dynamics." caption="Oscillatory graph dynamics replace irreversible diffusion with propagation that can conserve variation. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 14." %}

Across benchmark evaluations, several proposed remedies slow the decline of smoothing measures. The result is not a guarantee that the task improves.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Evaluation curves for several over-smoothing mitigation methods." caption="Architectures can preserve embedding diversity by the chosen metric without establishing better prediction. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 15." %}

The source emphasizes that preventing a vector from changing can trivially prevent smoothing, yet also prevent useful information propagation. PairNorm can preserve spread while degrading the expressive or optimization behavior needed by the task. A smoothing diagnostic is not itself an objective.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-16.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Risk that mitigating over-smoothing sacrifices expressive power." caption="A method can score well on a smoothing measure by suppressing the transformations the model needs. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 16." %}

## Over-squashing through sensitivity and curvature

Over-squashing is intrinsic to the graph topology and cannot generally be fixed by choosing a different shared message function. When a narrow cut separates large regions, many signals must traverse the same few edges.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-17.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph bottleneck causing over-squashing." caption="The topology can force distant evidence through a small cut regardless of the local update rule. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 17." %}

The first analysis asks how sensitive a node representation is to a distant input. This is relevant to molecular systems as well as synthetic graphs: long-range interactions can matter even when the computation graph is local.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-18.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sensitivity analysis for distant graph and molecular information." caption="Sensitivity makes information transfer measurable without assuming that hidden width alone explains it. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 18." %}

For a node representation $$h_v^{(L)}$$ and input $$x_u$$, the Jacobian norm

$$
\left\lVert\frac{\partial h_v^{(L)}}{\partial x_u}\right\rVert
$$

quantifies how strongly information at $$u$$ can affect $$v$$. Bounds relate this sensitivity to products or powers of normalized adjacency operators.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Jacobian sensitivity bound for an MPNN." caption="A node-to-node Jacobian turns distant influence into an analyzable quantity. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 19." %}

On a binary tree, the number of leaves grows exponentially with depth while the influence assigned to any one leaf shrinks. The example demonstrates over-squashing in a graph with no training ambiguity.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Binary tree sensitivity example for over-squashing." caption="A binary tree expands the number of distant sources faster than a fixed root state can preserve them. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 20." %}

Edges with highly negative discrete curvature often sit at such bottlenecks. The curvature uses local triangles and four-cycles to characterize whether neighborhoods around an edge overlap or branch away.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-21.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph edges with curvature determined by cycles and branching." caption="Highly negative curvature is a local signature of edges joining rapidly expanding graph regions. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 21." %}

### Ricci-curvature intuition

On continuous spaces, positive, zero, and negative curvature correspond roughly to spherical, Euclidean, and hyperbolic geometry. Geodesics tend to converge, remain parallel, or diverge accordingly.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-22.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Spherical, Euclidean, and hyperbolic curvature examples." caption="Continuous curvature describes how nearby geodesics converge or diverge. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 22." %}

Ricci curvature compares how small neighborhoods around nearby points separate. Coupling mass between the neighborhoods gives an optimal-transport view that transfers to discrete graphs.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Ricci curvature via transport between nearby geodesic balls." caption="Ricci curvature compares center distance with the transport distance between neighboring local distributions. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 23." %}

On graphs, neighborhood measures replace geodesic balls. Highly overlapping neighborhoods indicate positive curvature; tree-like branching produces negative curvature.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Discrete graph examples with positive, zero, and negative Ricci curvature." caption="Graph Ricci curvature distinguishes clustered edges from flat chains and expanding tree-like bottlenecks. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 24." %}

### Rewiring as a surgical intervention

If negatively curved edges indicate over-squashing bottlenecks, the graph can be rewired around them. This diagnosis is separate from molecular or domain plausibility: a computational shortcut may improve communication while changing the meaning of an edge.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-25.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph rewiring around negatively curved bottleneck edges." caption="Curvature-guided rewiring targets the graph locations predicted to squash information. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 25." %}

Adding edges across a cut creates alternative paths and changes local curvature.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="First graph-rewiring example adding bypass edges." caption="A bypass edge widens communication across a bottleneck without adding message-passing depth. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 26." %}

Different rewiring rules can add or remove edges, trading shorter information paths against distortion of the original graph.

### Rewiring over depth

Static rewiring adds every shortcut before propagation. Dynamic rewiring can instead add edges as the receptive field expands, preserving local bias early and enabling longer connections later.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Dynamic graph rewiring by message-passing layer." caption="Layer-dependent rewiring delays long-range edges until the model needs their reach. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 36." %}

In $$\nu$$-DRew, the allowed communication distance grows with depth and messages can be delayed according to graph distance.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-37.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Dynamic edge addition and message delay in nu-DRew." caption="Dynamic edge addition separates when a shortcut exists from when its message is delivered. The equations define a layer-dependent neighborhood and aggregate states from the appropriate delayed layer, with distance- and depth-dependent weights if desired.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-38.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Equations for nu-DRew dynamic message passing." caption="nu-DRew makes both the evolving edge set and delayed message source explicit in the layer update. ## A critical audit of GNN research

The lecture does not end with the strongest rewiring result. It turns to the research ecosystem around GNNs and warns that a large paper count is not evidence that common benchmarks or claims are scientifically well grounded.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-42.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Critical-thinking prompt for reading GNN research." caption="The final section asks readers to evaluate the problem and benchmark, not only the proposed layer. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 42." %}

The critique has four parts: missing real-world applications, graphs constructed without meaningful semantics, weak benchmarking culture, and the absence of broadly convincing graph foundation models.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-43.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Overview of four critiques of graph neural network research." caption="The critique separates task relevance, graph construction, evaluation culture, and scaling claims. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 43." %}

Many GNN studies use small or synthetic benchmarks with no compelling deployment. The lecture suggests redirecting effort toward natural graph problems with clear scientific impact, including combinatorial optimization and relational databases, and toward conditional diffusion models for graph-structured tasks.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-44.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Critique of missing real-world GNN applications and suggested directions." caption="A graph benchmark should correspond to a problem whose relational structure and outcome matter outside the benchmark. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 44." %}

Graphs may be constructed merely to test a hypothesis, with an arbitrary threshold or neighborhood rule supplying the edges. A benchmark then measures adaptation to that construction, not necessarily a meaningful relation in the data. The proposed response is to validate graph semantics or learn the graph rather than taking every edge set as ground truth.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-45.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Examples and critique of graphs lacking semantic meaning." caption="A graph architecture cannot rescue an input graph whose edges do not encode a defensible relation. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 45." %}

Benchmark practice is another problem. Missing standard splits, protocols, and detailed hyperparameters can make comparisons unstable, and researchers may report marginal gains without matched tuning. Shared tasks and careful evaluation frameworks matter as much as another architecture variant.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-46.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Critique of GNN benchmarking culture." caption="Uncontrolled splits and tuning can turn small benchmark differences into unreliable architecture claims. Cropped from the 2025 GDL large-graph GNN lecture, slide 46." %}

Graph foundation-model claims remain weak relative to language and vision. Even domain-specific graph models often produce modest gains, suggesting that useful scaling may require either a more coherent graph domain or much larger, higher-quality datasets.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/gdl-2025-06/slide-47.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Critique of graph foundation model claims." caption="Graph scaling lacks the shared data semantics and decisive transfer gains seen in mature foundation-model domains.
