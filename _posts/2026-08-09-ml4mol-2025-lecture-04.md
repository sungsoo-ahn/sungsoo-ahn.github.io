## Graphs as learning objects

A graph is a pair $$G=(V,E)$$. The vertex set contains $$|V|=N$$ nodes, and the edge set contains pairs $$uv\in E$$. In an attributed graph, node $$v$$ carries a feature $$x_v$$ and edge $$uv$$ may carry a feature $$e_{uv}$$. The representation-learning problem is to learn a parameterized map $$f_\theta:G\mapsto y$$ without assuming that the nodes arrive in a meaningful order.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s04-image4.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Definition of an attributed undirected graph and a graph-to-target learning problem." caption="Graph representation learning begins with a graph, its node and edge attributes, and a target. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 4." %}

The target determines the required output. A node-level task predicts one value $$y_v$$ for each vertex. An edge-level task predicts $$y_{uv}$$ for selected pairs. A graph-level task produces one value for the whole object. Molecular atom typing, bond prediction, and molecular property prediction are examples of the three levels, but the distinction applies to any graph domain.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s05-image1.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="One graph labeled with node-level, link-level, and graph-level prediction targets." caption="The same input graph supports node, link, and graph prediction, with different output symmetries. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 5." %}

A graph neural network encodes a graph through multiple nonlinear transformations that respect this unordered structure. Two broad designs dominate the lecture. A **message-passing network** connects nodes along the given edges. A **graph Transformer** allows dense interactions and must be told where graph structure enters. The distinction is not absolute—modern systems often combine the two—but it makes the central design choices visible.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s06-image2.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph encoder represented as multiple nonlinear transformations based on graph structure." caption="A GNN is an encoder whose transformations are organized by graph structure. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 6." %}

## Message passing neural networks

At layer $$\ell$$, a message-passing neural network first forms a directed message from neighbor $$u$$ to node $$v$$,

$$
m_{uv}^{(\ell)}=operatorname{MESSAGE}^{(\ell)}
\!\left(h_u^{(\ell-1)},e_{uv}\right),
$$

then aggregates the multiset of incoming messages,

$$
h_v^{(\ell)}=operatorname{AGGREGATE}^{(\ell)}
\!\left(\{m_{uv}^{(\ell)}:u\in\mathcal N(v)\},h_v^{(\ell-1)}\right).
$$

The same learned functions are reused at every node. A permutation of node labels therefore permutes the hidden states rather than changing the represented graph.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s07-image6.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Input graph followed by message computation and aggregation at its nodes." caption="One MPNN layer sends edge-local messages and pools them at their destination nodes. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 7." %}

Typical message functions are node-wise neural networks such as $$\operatorname{MLP}(h_u\Vert e_{uv})$$. Typical aggregators are sum, mean, or max. These functions are invariant to the order in which neighbors are listed, but they retain different information. A sum retains multiplicity, a mean normalizes it away, and a max preserves only coordinate-wise extrema.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s08-image7.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Equations for sum, mean, and max aggregation over graph neighborhoods." caption="Sum, mean, and max are permutation-invariant neighborhood operators with different information loss. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 8." %}

### Graph convolutional networks

The graph convolutional network is a basic message-passing model for node-attributed graphs without edge attributes. Initialize $$h_v^{(0)}=x_v$$ and repeatedly average transformed neighbor features while retaining a transformed self-feature. In the lecture notation,

$$
h_v^{(\ell+1)}=sigma\!\left(
W_{\ell}\sum_{u\in\mathcal N(v)}\frac{h_u^{(\ell)}}{|\mathcal N(v)|}
+B_{\ell}h_v^{(\ell)}
\right).
$$

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s09-image13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph convolution equation combining averaged neighbor messages with a self transformation." caption="A GCN combines normalized neighborhood information with a node's current representation. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 9." %}

Stacking node vectors as rows of $$H^{(\ell)}$$ gives the matrix form

$$
H^{(\ell+1)}=\sigma\!\left(\widetilde A H^{(\ell)}W_\ell+H^{(\ell)}B_\ell\right),
\qquad
\widetilde A=D^{-1}A.
$$

The red operation in the lecture is graph aggregation by the normalized adjacency; the blue operation is the shared self-transformation. Implementations often fold self-loops and symmetric normalization into a single sparse matrix multiplication. The important point is that graph connectivity selects which features can mix.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s10-image14.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Matrix GCN update with normalized adjacency, hidden feature matrix, and learned weights." caption="Writing GCN in matrix form separates sparse graph mixing from learned feature mixing. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 10." %}

GraphSAGE, GIN, and GatedGCN keep the same message–aggregate skeleton while changing the functions. GraphSAGE samples neighbors for inductive and scalable learning. GIN uses a sum followed by an MLP to match the discrimination of the one-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman test under suitable assumptions. GatedGCN learns edge-conditioned gates that regulate how much each message contributes.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s11-image15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="MPNN template annotated with GraphSAGE, GIN, and GatedGCN design choices." caption="Many named GNNs differ mainly in their message and aggregation operators. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 11." %}

### Graph attention

Attention replaces a fixed pooling rule with learned weights. A compatibility score $$a_{uv}$$ is computed from the states of $$u$$ and $$v$$, normalized over $$u\in\mathcal N(v)$$, and used to weight the values:

$$
\alpha_{uv}=\frac{\exp(a_{uv})}{\sum_{u'\in\mathcal N(v)}\exp(a_{u'v})},
\qquad
h_v^{(\ell)}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal N(v)}\alpha_{uv}W^Vh_u^{(\ell-1)}.
$$

If the neighborhood is changed from $$\mathcal N(v)$$ to all vertices, the same pattern becomes a Transformer layer.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s13-image17.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph-attention aggregation with softmax-normalized messages from neighboring nodes." caption="Graph attention learns a normalized importance weight for each incoming message. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 13." %}

The original GAT does not use the standard query–key dot product. It concatenates transformed endpoint features and feeds them to a learned linear score with a LeakyReLU. Multi-head attention repeats this construction in parallel and concatenates or averages the results.

## Graph discrimination and expressive power

The graph isomorphism test asks whether two graphs differ only by relabeling. Graphs $$G_1=(V_1,E_1,X_1)$$ and $$G_2=(V_2,E_2,X_2)$$ are isomorphic if there is a bijection $$\varphi:V_1\to V_2$$ that preserves adjacency and node features. A graph representation should assign the same value to isomorphic inputs.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s15-image18.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Adjacency-preserving and feature-preserving conditions for graph isomorphism." caption="Graph isomorphism is a relabeling that preserves both connectivity and attributes. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 15." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s15-image19.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two graph drawings connected by a bijection between corresponding nodes." caption="Different drawings can describe the same attributed graph when a preserving bijection exists. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 15." %}

An MPNN automatically respects an isomorphism: corresponding nodes begin with the same feature, receive the same multiset of corresponding messages, and remain matched at every layer. After invariant graph pooling, isomorphic graphs have identical graph representations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s16-image20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Commuting diagram showing isomorphic inputs receiving the same GNN graph representation." caption="Message passing is faithful to a supplied graph isomorphism. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 16." %}

The converse fails. Non-isomorphic graphs can collapse to the same representation for every MPNN. Regular graphs with the same number of vertices give an abstract family: if all nodes start identically and have the same degree, every node receives the same repeated message at every layer. Circular skip-link graphs provide one example. Decalin and bicyclopentyl illustrate that the limitation is not restricted to artificial unlabeled diagrams.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s18-image23.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two non-isomorphic regular graphs with the same number of vertices." caption="Equal-size regular graphs can remain indistinguishable to ordinary message passing. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 18." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s18-image24.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Chemical structures of decalin and bicyclopentyl presented as a molecular graph collision." caption="The lecture connects the abstract regular-graph collision to molecular graph structures. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 18." %}

Universal approximation makes the consequence precise. Let $$\mathcal F$$ be a class of permutation-invariant functions on graphs and $$\mathcal M$$ the class approximated by MPNNs. If two graphs are always assigned the same MPNN representation, then no member of $$\mathcal M$$ can approximate a target that assigns those graphs different values. Discrimination is therefore a necessary condition for universal approximation over a target function class.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s20-image26.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Function class diagram relating universal approximation to graph discrimination." caption="A model class cannot approximate a graph function that separates inputs its representations collapse. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 20." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s21-image27.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Strict inclusion of MPNN-approximable functions inside permutation-invariant graph functions." caption="MPNNs occupy a strict subset of the invariant functions when they fail to discriminate some graphs. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 21." %}

Induced-subgraph counting is one excluded target family. A function may count every occurrence of a specified motif using a subset of vertices and all edges between them. Graph pairs that an MPNN collapses can contain different motif counts, so general induced-subgraph counting is not in the ordinary MPNN function class.

### The Weisfeiler–Lehman test

The general graph-isomorphism problem is not known to have a polynomial-time
algorithm or to be NP-complete. The Weisfeiler–Lehman test supplies a practical
combinatorial heuristic. Begin by assigning every node a color, often from its
input feature. At each iteration, replace the color of a node by a hash of its
current color and the multiset of its neighbors' colors. If the color histograms
differ between two graphs at any iteration, the graphs are non-isomorphic. If
the histograms never differ, the test is inconclusive rather than a proof of
isomorphism.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s25-image31.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="First Weisfeiler–Lehman coloring step on two graphs." caption="The WL test begins from node colors and repeatedly hashes neighborhood color multisets. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 25." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s26-image32.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Second Weisfeiler–Lehman refinement step on two graphs." caption="Color refinement propagates structural differences outward one neighborhood at a time. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 26." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s27-image33.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Different color histograms produced by Weisfeiler–Lehman refinement." caption="Different stable color histograms certify that the two inputs are non-isomorphic. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 27." %}

The update resembles message passing, but not every aggregator is injective on multisets. GIN was designed to simulate the one-dimensional WL refinement: sum aggregation counts repeated colors, while an MLP can represent the hash on bounded multisets. Mean and max can lose precisely the multiplicity information needed by the test. GIN and a degree-normalized GCN can therefore have different expressive power even though both are MPNNs.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s29-image35.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Equations comparing graph isomorphism network and graph convolutional network updates." caption="GIN uses an injective sum-and-MLP design to match WL discrimination; normalized averaging need not. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 29." %}

## Graph Transformers need graph structure

A vanilla Transformer receives a set of node tokens. Dense self-attention is permutation equivariant, but it does not know which tokens were adjacent in the input graph. If the only inputs are node features, two graphs with the same feature multiset but different edges look identical.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s30-image36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graph tokens passed to a Transformer with a question mark over missing graph structure." caption="A Transformer can process an unordered node set, but adjacency is absent unless it is explicitly encoded. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 30." %}

In language, learned or sinusoidal position vectors identify word order. On graphs there is no single canonical first, second, or third node, so positional information must itself respect graph relabeling. The structural signal can enter node features, attention biases, edge tokens, or a local message-passing branch.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s31-image38.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Ordered word tokens with positional features contrasted with otherwise identical tokens." caption="Sequence Transformers distinguish token positions; graph Transformers require a graph-compatible replacement. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 31." %}

One option is a Laplacian eigenvector positional encoding. For $$L=D-A$$,

$$
Lv_k=\lambda_kv_k.
$$

Compute several eigenvectors, stack them as columns, and attach row $$i$$ to node $$i$$. The Laplacian depends on the graph, making its eigenvectors a graph analogue of sinusoidal coordinates. Their signs are ambiguous, and repeated eigenvalues permit arbitrary basis rotations inside an eigenspace, so an implementation must handle those symmetries rather than treating the coordinates as canonical.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s33-image40.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Laplacian eigenvector matrix supplying node-wise positional encodings to a Transformer." caption="Rows of selected Laplacian eigenvectors provide graph-dependent node coordinates. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 33." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s34-image41.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Degree and adjacency matrices forming a graph Laplacian." caption="The graph Laplacian is the degree matrix minus adjacency and therefore changes with connectivity. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 34." %}

Another option modifies the attention score by a pairwise structural bias,

$$
\alpha_{uv}=\operatorname{softmax}_u\!\left(b_{uv}+(W^Qh_v)^\top W^Kh_u\right).
$$

The bias $$b_{uv}$$ can encode shortest-path distance, edge type, or another pair relation. Graphormer combines shortest-path spatial encoding, edge-type encoding, and degree-based centrality encoding.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s35-image44.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Graphormer diagram with spatial, edge, and centrality encodings." caption="Shortest paths, edge types, and centrality can enter a Transformer as graph-aware biases and features. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 35." %}

GraphGPS++ illustrates the hybrid endpoint. A local MPNN and a global attention block operate in parallel, their outputs are combined with residual connections and feed-forward layers, and the design is tuned for molecular property prediction at scale. The lecture uses its benchmark curve to emphasize that architecture, optimization, and dataset scale must be evaluated together.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s36-image45.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Block diagram of a hybrid GraphGPS-style local message-passing and global-attention layer." caption="GraphGPS-style layers combine local graph propagation with global attention. ## The first GNN wave: 2013–2019

The second part of the lecture is deliberately chronological. The timeline is not a claim that every paper replaces the previous one. It shows recurring questions: how to generalize convolution, how to sample and pool, what permutation symmetry allows, what GNNs can distinguish, and how to make propagation deeper or more global.

The 2013 work *Deep Convolutional Networks on Graph-Structured Data* partitioned graphs into clusters and pooled them, while also proposing a spectral version. The spectral route became clearer in ChebNet: approximate a spectral filter with Chebyshev polynomials of the Laplacian. A polynomial of degree $$K$$ is localized to a $$K$$-hop neighborhood, connecting graph signal processing to spatial message passing.

Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 39." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s40-image53.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Chebyshev spectral filtering architecture on a graph." caption="ChebNet approximates localized spectral filters with Laplacian polynomials. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 40." %}

Node2vec took a different route. It generated node-centered random walks, biased the walk between breadth-first and depth-first behavior, and then learned representations from the resulting sequences. Kipf and Welling's GCN derived a simple first-order approximation to spectral graph convolution, helping establish the normalized propagation rule that became a standard baseline.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s41-image54.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Breadth-first and depth-first biased graph walks used by node2vec." caption="Node2vec turns a graph neighborhood into sampled sequences while balancing local and outward exploration. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 41." %}

The 2017 neural message-passing paper formulated message and update functions as a common framework and applied them to the QM9 quantum-chemistry dataset. GraphSAGE made node representation learning inductive and scalable by sampling neighborhoods. R-GCN introduced relation-specific transformations for graphs with typed edges.

Deep Sets characterized linear permutation-equivariant maps on element-wise features. For a scalar feature vector $$x\in\mathbb R^N$$, a linear map commuting with every permutation has the form

$$
Cx=w_1x+w_2\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top x.
$$

The result identifies a local term and a global pooled term. GatedGCN and GAT then supplied two related ways to condition messages on pairs: learned gates and normalized attention.

Jumping Knowledge Networks addressed depth by combining representations from several layers instead of trusting only the final diffusion state. Its analysis used random walks and influence functions to explain how local information can disappear. DiffPool learned a soft assignment $$S^{(\ell)}$$ from nodes to clusters and coarsened both features and adjacency:

$$
X^{(\ell+1)}=S^{(\ell)\top}Z^{(\ell)},
\qquad
A^{(\ell+1)}=S^{(\ell)\top}A^{(\ell)}S^{(\ell)}.
$$

The lecture also records a practical opinion: graph pooling has often been less reliable than its CNN analogue.

Invariant Graph Neural Networks generalized the symmetry question from vectors to node- and edge-valued tensors. For a degree-$$\ell$$ tensor, the desired linear map satisfies $$L(P\star X)=P\star L(X)$$. The space is characterized by equality patterns among tensor indices, and higher tensor order produces a larger equivariant basis.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s51-image81.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Permutation acting on graph nodes and on both axes of an adjacency tensor." caption="For edge-valued tensors, relabeling acts simultaneously on both node indices. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 51." %}

GIN formally connected MPNNs to the WL test and triggered a large expressivity literature. Higher-order $$k$$-WL methods color tuples in $$V^k$$ rather than individual nodes; corresponding GNNs trade substantially higher cost for stronger discrimination. Simplified Graph Convolution removed intermediate nonlinearities and collapsed propagation into $$L^K H^{(0)}W$$, arguing that many benchmarks did not require a highly nonlinear propagation stack.

## Architecture and evaluation: 2020–2025

By 2020, architecture claims were colliding with evaluation practice. *A Fair Comparison of Graph Neural Networks* showed that results on small TU datasets could be statistically fragile and argued for proper cross-validation. Principal Neighbourhood Aggregation combined multiple aggregators and degree-dependent scalers because no single statistic captures every neighborhood. GCNII used an initial residual and an identity mapping to make deep GCNs trainable.

## Critical thinking about GNN research

The final part of the lecture is not an appendix. It changes how the preceding timeline should be read. A large literature can accumulate architecture variations while leaving the underlying tasks, graph constructions, and evaluation protocols underexamined.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s79-image151.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Sequence of graph learning papers illustrating the development of the field." caption="Architectural progress must be interpreted together with the problems and benchmarks that selected it. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 79." %}

### Missing real-world applications

Much GNN development has concentrated on niche benchmark formulations such as property prediction from a fixed two-dimensional molecular graph. Those tasks can be useful, but their impact does not follow merely from having graph inputs. The lecture argues for natural graph problems with clear real-world stakes, including combinatorial optimization and relational databases, and for end-to-end measurements of whether the method changes a consequential decision.

### Graphs can lack meaning

A graph is often constructed by the researcher rather than observed directly. Thresholds, neighborhood rules, relation definitions, and featurization decide which information becomes an edge. Not everything that *can* be modeled as a graph should be. A new benchmark should explain why its graph representation is appropriate and what information is lost or invented by that construction.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s81-image152.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Several visual domains converted into graph representations with different construction choices." caption="Graph construction is a modeling decision, and its semantics can dominate the architecture applied afterward. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 81." %}

### Benchmarking culture

Many graph datasets lack standard splits, evaluation protocols, or sufficiently detailed hyperparameters. Marginal gains are reported without enough repeated trials to establish statistical significance. The remedy is not a single universal split: it is to define the generalization question, report variance, tune baselines comparably, and make the protocol reproducible.

The empirical tables in the lecture support the criticism. On OGB tasks, changing model depth, residual connections, dropout, normalization, and training settings can reverse rankings among GCN, GraphSAGE, GIN, and GAT variants. A claimed architectural improvement must therefore be separated from its optimization and capacity budget.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-04/s84-image153.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="OGB benchmark table comparing classic GNN variants under controlled settings." caption="Controlled tuning can change the apparent ranking of familiar GNN architectures. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol graph neural networks lecture, slide 84." %}

### No true graph foundation model yet

The lecture's position is that a broadly useful graph foundation model has not yet emerged. Even domain-specific pretrained graph models often show marginal downstream gains. Moving from one model per dataset to one model across datasets requires large, diverse corpora, compatible feature semantics, objectives that transfer, and evaluation across genuinely different domains—not merely more parameters on a single benchmark family.

The final empirical example distinguishes several claims. Multi-task pretraining on COCO-SP, MalNetTiny, and PCQM4Mv2 can improve some downstream tasks such as PascalVOC-SP, Peptides-struct, and Stargazers. Yet the gains depend on the architectural modification, task, and training regime. Pretraining is therefore sometimes beneficial, not an automatic guarantee of a graph foundation model.

The lecture's storyline therefore ends somewhere different from a conventional architecture survey. Message passing gives a principled permutation-equivariant local computation. WL analysis explains a real expressive boundary. Structural encodings make global attention graph-aware. The historical timeline supplies many ways to enlarge, stabilize, or scale the computation. But none of those choices can rescue an ill-defined graph, an uninformative benchmark, or an evaluation protocol that cannot support the claimed conclusion.
