## Molecules and materials

A molecule is a finite group of atoms connected by chemical bonds. A material is an ensemble of molecules, atoms, or ions whose collective arrangement produces properties that an isolated molecule does not possess. The distinction is not purely one of size. A single conjugated molecule may carry current between electrodes, while a disordered film of the same molecule exhibits transport limited by packing, defects, and intermolecular contacts.

Machine learning can only use a molecule after we choose a representation. That choice decides which distinctions are visible to a model.

### One-dimensional identifiers

SMILES encodes a molecular graph as a string. Atoms appear as symbols, bonds and branches use punctuation, and ring closures use matching indices. The representation is compact and convenient for sequence models, but it is not unique: the same graph admits many valid traversal orders. A canonicalization procedure is therefore needed whenever a task treats the string itself as the data record.

InChI takes a different approach. It builds a layered identifier containing the molecular formula, connectivity, hydrogen atoms, charge, stereochemistry, and isotopic information. Its standardization makes it useful for database matching and deduplication. SMILES is often more convenient for generation; InChI is designed as an identifier.

### Two-dimensional graphs and fingerprints

The molecular graph is the most direct representation of covalent connectivity. Atoms become nodes, bonds become edges, and attributes record element, formal charge, aromaticity, bond order, or stereochemical labels. A graph removes the arbitrary order of a string, but it still omits the conformational geometry that determines many physical interactions.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s06-image6.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A small molecular graph with atoms as labeled nodes and bonds as edges." caption="A molecular graph records atoms, bonds, and their attributes without committing to a particular string traversal. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 6." %}

A molecular fingerprint compresses local graph patterns into a fixed-length vector. Circular fingerprints repeatedly expand atom-centered neighborhoods, hash the resulting identifiers, and set the corresponding bits or counts. Increasing the radius exposes larger motifs, while the finite hash space introduces collisions: distinct neighborhoods can map to the same index.

### Three-dimensional coordinates and chirality

Atomic coordinates record a conformation as positions $$\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^3$$. They expose distances, bond angles, and torsional angles, which are needed for steric interactions and most energy models. Coordinates also introduce equivalences: translating or rotating the entire molecule does not change its internal state, and flexible molecules possess many thermally accessible conformations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s08-image11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A ball-and-stick molecule beside an internal-coordinate diagram of bonds, angles, and torsions." caption="Cartesian coordinates determine a conformation, while internal coordinates expose its bond lengths, angles, and torsions. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 8." %}

Reflection is different from rotation. A chiral molecule and its mirror image cannot be superimposed by a proper rotation. Enantiomers share most scalar physical properties in an achiral environment but can interact differently with chiral proteins, enzymes, and receptors. A representation that discards stereochemistry can therefore merge compounds with different biological effects.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s09-image12.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two mirror-image molecular structures illustrating chirality." caption="Enantiomers are mirror images that cannot be aligned by rotation. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 9." %}

### Crystalline solids, lattices, and symmetry

A crystal repeats a finite basis of atoms over a lattice. If $$\mathbf{a}_1,\mathbf{a}_2,\mathbf{a}_3$$ are lattice vectors, equivalent cells occur at

$$
\mathbf{R}_{n_1n_2n_3}=n_1\mathbf{a}_1+n_2\mathbf{a}_2+n_3\mathbf{a}_3,
\qquad n_1,n_2,n_3\in\mathbb{Z}.
$$

The lattice supplies periodic translations; the basis supplies chemical identity and positions inside one cell. Neither alone specifies the crystal.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s10-image13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Diagram showing a lattice plus a two-atom basis producing a periodic crystal structure." caption="A crystal structure combines a translation lattice with an atomic basis. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 10." %}

Molecular symmetry uses rotations, reflections, and inversion operations that leave a finite structure unchanged. Crystal symmetry adds translation and operations coupled to translation. The seven crystal systems constrain unit-cell lengths and angles. Centering those cells produces fourteen Bravais lattices. Point operations produce 32 crystallographic point groups, and combining point operations with translations, screw axes, and glide planes gives 230 space groups.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s11-image15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Examples of rotational and reflection symmetries in molecular shapes." caption="Molecular point symmetry records the rigid operations that leave a finite structure unchanged. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 11." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s14-image20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Hierarchy relating seven crystal systems, fourteen Bravais lattices, thirty-two point groups, and 230 space groups." caption="Crystal systems, Bravais lattices, point groups, and space groups encode progressively richer periodic symmetry. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 14." %}

Symmetry constrains measurable properties. A molecule with an inversion center cannot possess a permanent electric dipole because inversion would reverse the dipole while leaving the molecule unchanged. The only vector satisfying both requirements is zero. Crystal tensors are restricted in the same way: symmetry can force elastic, dielectric, piezoelectric, or optical-response components to vanish or become equal.

Real materials are not perfect repetitions. Vacancies, interstitial atoms, substitutions, dislocations, grain boundaries, and surfaces break ideal symmetry. These defects can control ionic conductivity, carrier density, mechanical strength, and catalytic activity. A pristine unit cell is therefore a useful reference, not a complete description of a sample.

## Quantum mechanics

Classical representations say where nuclei and bonds are. Quantum mechanics explains why those bonds and structures are possible.

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is

$$
\hat{H}\Psi = E\Psi,
$$

where $$\Psi$$ is the wavefunction, $$\hat{H}$$ is the Hamiltonian, and $$E$$ is an allowed energy. For a molecule, the Hamiltonian contains kinetic energies for electrons and nuclei plus electron–nucleus attraction, electron–electron repulsion, and nucleus–nucleus repulsion. Solving the eigenvalue problem yields a wavefunction and energy; other observables follow from expectation values of the corresponding operators.

The wavefunction is an amplitude rather than a directly observable probability. For one particle, $$\lvert\Psi(\mathbf{r})\rvert^2$$ gives a probability density. Its sign or complex phase still matters because amplitudes interfere before they are squared.

### Hydrogen and atomic orbitals

Hydrogen is the simplest exact three-dimensional solution. Its stationary states factor into radial functions and spherical harmonics,

$$
\Psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi).
$$

The quantum numbers label energy shells, angular momentum, and orientation. The familiar $$s$$, $$p$$, $$d$$, and $$f$$ orbitals correspond to $$\ell=0,1,2,3$$. Nodes and angular lobes are features of the amplitude; the electron density is its squared magnitude.

### Approximations and the many-electron problem

Molecules contain moving nuclei and interacting electrons. The Born–Oppenheimer approximation exploits the much larger nuclear masses: for fixed nuclear coordinates $$\mathbf{R}$$, solve the electronic problem first, then treat its energy as a potential for nuclear motion (<span id="cite-born1927"></span>[Born and Oppenheimer, 1927](#ref-born1927)). This separation underlies potential-energy surfaces and most molecular simulation.

Even with fixed nuclei, the electronic wavefunction depends on $$3N_e$$ spatial coordinates plus spin. Electron–electron repulsion couples those variables, so the exact state cannot generally be written as independent one-electron functions. Antisymmetry adds another constraint: exchanging two electrons must change the sign of the fermionic wavefunction. Slater determinants enforce this property for orbital products, but a single determinant cannot represent all electron correlation.

Atomic orbitals provide a practical basis for molecular orbitals. In a linear-combination-of-atomic-orbitals description,

$$
\phi_p(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu p}\chi_\mu(\mathbf{r}),
$$

where $$\chi_\mu$$ are basis functions centered on atoms. In-phase overlap concentrates amplitude between nuclei and produces a bonding orbital. Out-of-phase overlap creates a node and an antibonding orbital.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s28-image34.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Constructive and destructive combinations of two hydrogen 1s orbitals forming bonding and antibonding orbitals." caption="Adding atomic amplitudes produces a bonding orbital; subtracting them creates an antibonding node. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 28." %}

Density functional theory replaces the many-electron wavefunction with the electron density $$n(\mathbf{r})$$ as the central variable. The Hohenberg–Kohn result establishes that the ground-state density determines the external potential and therefore the ground-state observables (<span id="cite-hk1964"></span>[Hohenberg and Kohn, 1964](#ref-hk1964)). Kohn–Sham DFT introduces auxiliary orbitals that reproduce the density while collecting the unknown many-body contribution into an exchange-correlation functional (<span id="cite-ks1965"></span>[Kohn and Sham, 1965](#ref-ks1965)). The practical accuracy then depends on the functional, basis, pseudopotential, numerical settings, and the chemical regime.

## Electronic properties: from orbitals to bands

Electrons fill available states subject to the Pauli exclusion principle. In a molecule, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) bracket the frontier where removing or adding electronic population becomes easiest. Their energy separation is often used as a qualitative measure of chemical reactivity, optical excitation, and electronic stability, although a Kohn–Sham orbital gap is not generally identical to an experimental excitation gap.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s32-image36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Molecular orbital energy levels with occupied and unoccupied frontier orbitals." caption="The HOMO and LUMO sit on opposite sides of the molecular occupation boundary. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 32." %}

When many atoms repeat in a crystal, closely spaced molecular or atomic levels broaden into bands. Bloch states are labeled by crystal momentum $$\mathbf{k}$$, and their energies form dispersions $$E_n(\mathbf{k})$$ across the Brillouin zone. The highest occupied set becomes the valence band; the lowest unoccupied set becomes the conduction band.

The position of the Fermi level and the band gap distinguish common electronic regimes. In a metal, a band is partially filled or occupied and unoccupied bands overlap, so carriers respond without crossing a gap. In an insulator, a large gap suppresses thermal excitation. A semiconductor has a smaller gap whose carrier population can be controlled through temperature, doping, fields, or illumination.

## Intra- and intermolecular forces

Quantum mechanics supplies energies; chemistry often reasons in terms of forces. Once an electronic energy $$E(\mathbf{R})$$ has been defined for nuclear coordinates, the force on nucleus $$i$$ is

$$
\mathbf{F}_i=-\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_i}E(\mathbf{R}).
$$

Different bonding labels describe recurring electronic arrangements rather than separate fundamental forces.

### Covalent, ionic, and metallic bonding

A covalent bond forms when atoms share electronic density, usually through occupied bonding orbitals. Directional overlap explains why covalent molecules have characteristic bond angles and local geometries.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s40-image41.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Methane molecule with shared electron pairs in four covalent carbon-hydrogen bonds." caption="Covalent bonding localizes shared electronic density between atoms. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 40." %}

Ionic bonding emphasizes charge transfer and Coulomb attraction between oppositely charged ions. Metallic bonding instead involves valence electrons delocalized over many atomic centers. These descriptions can mix: real solids often combine ionic, covalent, and metallic character.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s42-image43.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Regular array of positive metal ions surrounded by delocalized electrons." caption="Metallic bonding spreads valence electrons across an extended array of ionic cores. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 42." %}

### Hydrogen bonding, permanent dipoles, and dispersion

Hydrogen bonding occurs when hydrogen attached to an electronegative atom interacts with an electron-rich acceptor. It is directional and stronger than a generic dipole–dipole contact, which makes it central to water structure, protein folding, and molecular recognition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s43-image44.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Water molecules connected by directional hydrogen bonds." caption="Hydrogen bonding joins a polarized donor bond to an electron-rich acceptor. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 43." %}

Two molecules with permanent dipoles interact through the orientation-dependent electrostatic coupling of their partial charges. Thermal rotation averages this interaction differently in gases, liquids, and constrained binding pockets.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s44-image46.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Attractive and repulsive orientations of two molecular dipoles." caption="Permanent dipoles attract or repel according to their relative orientation. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 44." %}

London dispersion does not require a permanent dipole. Instantaneous fluctuations in one electron cloud induce correlated fluctuations in another, producing an attractive interaction. Dispersion is individually weak but ubiquitous, and it grows with polarizability and contact area.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s45-image47.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two atoms with correlated instantaneous dipoles illustrating London dispersion." caption="Correlated charge fluctuations generate London dispersion even between nonpolar species. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 45." %}

The energy scales form a useful hierarchy rather than a strict partition. Intramolecular covalent bonds are typically hundreds of kilojoules per mole. Hydrogen bonds and electrostatic contacts are weaker, while individual dispersion contacts are weaker still. Large biomolecules and condensed phases contain many such contacts, so their collective contribution can rival a smaller number of stronger interactions.

## Statistical mechanics: from one molecule to many

A microscopic state specifies positions and momenta for all particles. A macroscopic sample never occupies just one such state in a reproducible way. Statistical mechanics assigns probabilities to microstates and connects their distribution to temperature, pressure, energy, and experimentally averaged observables.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s48-image48.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Cartoon of an ensemble containing systems with different microscopic energies." caption="An ensemble represents many microscopic realizations compatible with the same macroscopic conditions. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 48." %}

In the canonical ensemble, a state $$i$$ with energy $$E_i$$ has probability

$$
p_i=\frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z},
\qquad
Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j},
\qquad
\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}.
$$

The partition function $$Z$$ normalizes the distribution and generates thermodynamic quantities. Low-energy states dominate at low temperature; higher-energy states gain population as thermal energy increases. Observables are ensemble averages, $$\langle A\rangle=\sum_i p_iA_i$$, rather than properties of a single preferred conformation.

Gibbs free energy is the thermodynamic potential for systems at fixed temperature and pressure,

$$
G=H-TS,
$$

where $$H$$ is enthalpy and $$S$$ is entropy. Enthalpy accounts for energetic changes including pressure–volume work. Entropy measures how broadly probability is distributed over compatible microscopic states. A process can therefore be favored by lower enthalpy, higher entropy, or both. At fixed $$T$$ and $$P$$, a negative $$\Delta G$$ indicates thermodynamic favorability.

## The energetics and dynamics of chemical change

Chemistry concerns transformations: bonds break and form, conformations interconvert, phases change, and molecules move through environments. A potential-energy surface assigns energy to nuclear geometry. Stable structures appear as local minima. Reaction pathways connect minima through saddle regions, and a first-order saddle point corresponds to a transition state along one unstable direction.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s55-image57.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Three-dimensional potential-energy surface with valleys and a barrier between configurations." caption="A potential-energy surface turns molecular geometry into energy, with stable basins separated by barriers. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 55." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s56-image58.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Potential-energy surface labeled with reactant and product minima, transition structures, and saddle points." caption="Minima represent metastable structures, while saddle points organize the paths between them. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 56." %}

Molecular dynamics integrates Newton's equations on this surface,

$$
m_i\frac{d^2\mathbf{R}_i}{dt^2}=\mathbf{F}_i(\mathbf{R}),
$$

or a thermostatted stochastic variant. The resulting trajectory reveals vibrations, diffusion, conformational changes, and collective rearrangements. In practice, the accessible timescale is limited by the integration step and the height of free-energy barriers.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s54-image56.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated molecular-dynamics trajectory of a membrane protein, lipid bilayer, water, and ions." caption="Molecular dynamics propagates an atomistic system through time under forces derived from its energy model. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 54." %}

Thermodynamics and kinetics answer different questions. The free-energy change $$\Delta G$$ compares the equilibrium preference of reactants and products. The activation free energy $$\Delta G^{\ddagger}$$ controls how quickly the system crosses the barrier. A reaction can be thermodynamically favorable but kinetically slow when its barrier is high; a metastable material can persist for the same reason.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s57-image59.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Reaction-energy profiles comparing thermodynamic driving force and activation barrier." caption="Reaction free energy determines equilibrium preference, while the activation barrier determines rate. Reused from the 2025 ML4Mol chemistry and physics lecture, slide 57." %}

The six parts of the description fit together without collapsing into one representation. Graphs and coordinates encode structure. Quantum mechanics defines electronic states and energies. Energy gradients produce forces. Statistical mechanics converts microscopic energies into macroscopic probabilities. Dynamics determines which transformations occur on an observed timescale. Molecular machine learning enters at any of these interfaces, but its target and guarantees depend on which physical layer the model is approximating.
