Molecular machine learning sits on top of several physical descriptions of matter. A molecule can be a string, a graph, a set of coordinates, an electronic wavefunction, or a point on an energy landscape. A material adds periodicity, defects, collective behavior, and macroscopic observables. None of these descriptions is universally best. Each keeps certain information visible and makes other information difficult or impossible to recover.

This chapter is therefore an orientation rather than a replacement for a chemistry or physics course. It follows the same progression as the lecture: representations of molecules and materials, quantum mechanics, electronic properties, chemical forces, statistical mechanics, and finally chemical change. The formulas are simplified where the lecture is simplified, but the assumptions and common failure points are made explicit.

## Molecules and materials

A molecule is a finite group of atoms held together by chemical bonds. A material is a macroscopic collection of atoms, ions, or molecules whose arrangement produces collective properties. Water illustrates the distinction. One water molecule has a geometry and dipole moment; liquid water has density, viscosity, a dielectric response, and a hydrogen-bond network. Those bulk properties do not belong to an isolated molecule.

The boundary is not only a matter of size. A single conjugated molecule placed between electrodes supports a transport question at the molecular scale. A film made from the same molecules adds packing, disorder, interfaces, and defects. The chemical species may be unchanged while the object being predicted is different.

Machine learning starts only after this physical object has been encoded. The representation decides which distinctions a model can see. It also decides which symmetries the architecture should respect and which targets can be learned without reconstructing missing information.

### One-dimensional identifiers

SMILES represents a molecular graph as a string. Atom symbols specify elements; parentheses mark branches; digits close rings; punctuation records bond types and stereochemistry. The attraction is practical: a molecule becomes a sequence that can be stored, searched, tokenized, and processed by language-model machinery.

The string is not the molecule. A graph admits many valid traversal orders, so one molecular graph can produce many SMILES strings. Canonical SMILES chooses one ordering through a software-dependent canonicalization procedure. Randomized SMILES deliberately samples other traversals and can act as data augmentation. A model trained on raw strings must therefore learn both chemistry and the conventions of the serialization. The original SMILES specification makes this graph-to-string contract explicit (<span id="cite-smiles1988"></span>[Weininger, 1988](#ref-smiles1988)).

InChI serves a different purpose. It constructs a standardized, layered identifier for formula, connectivity, hydrogen atoms, charge, stereochemistry, and isotopes. That layered structure makes InChI useful for database matching and deduplication. SMILES is often convenient as a generative representation; InChI was designed as an identifier whose standardization is more important than ease of generation (<span id="cite-inchi2015"></span>[Heller et al., 2015](#ref-inchi2015)).

### Two-dimensional graphs and fingerprints

The molecular graph makes connectivity explicit. Atoms become nodes, bonds become edges, and attributes can record atomic number, formal charge, aromaticity, bond order, and stereochemical labels. Unlike a string, the graph has no chemically meaningful first node. Relabeling node indices leaves the molecule unchanged, which is why graph models are designed to be invariant or equivariant to permutations.

A bare graph still omits a conformation. It says which atoms are bonded, but not which low-energy geometry the molecule adopts, whether two nonbonded atoms approach closely, or how the environment changes that geometry. The graph is sufficient for some properties and incomplete for others.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s06-image6.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two molecular structures illustrating atoms and bonds in a molecular graph." caption="A molecular graph records atoms, bonds, and their attributes without committing to a particular string traversal. Source: <a href='https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008504'>Yirik and Steinbeck, Fig. 3</a>." %}

Fingerprints compress graph neighborhoods into a fixed-length vector. In a circular fingerprint, each atom begins with an identifier derived from local features. Repeated updates combine the identifier with those of neighboring atoms, so the radius determines how much of the local environment is represented. The resulting identifiers are hashed into bit positions or count bins. Extended-connectivity fingerprints formalized this procedure for similarity search and predictive modeling (<span id="cite-ecfp2010"></span>[Rogers and Hahn, 2010](#ref-ecfp2010)).

This compression is cheap and often strong for classical machine learning, but it is not lossless. Different neighborhoods can collide in the same finite hash space. The fingerprint also records that a motif occurs without necessarily preserving where two motifs occur relative to one another. Increasing the radius exposes larger substructures, while increasing the vector length reduces collisions; neither operation turns the fingerprint into a full three-dimensional description.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s07-image8.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Circular molecular neighborhoods at increasing radii mapped to fingerprint identifiers." caption="A circular fingerprint expands atom-centered neighborhoods and hashes their identifiers into a fixed representation. Sources: <a href='https://michellelynngill.com/assets/pdf/presentations/2020_GillMichelle_RiceDataScienceConference.pdf'>Gill</a> and <a href='https://doi.org/10.1021/ci100050t'>Rogers and Hahn</a>." %}

### Three-dimensional coordinates and chirality

Atomic coordinates represent one conformation as positions $$\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^3$$. Bond lengths, bond angles, torsional angles, and nonbonded distances can all be computed from these coordinates. This makes geometry available to an energy model, but it introduces equivalences: translating or rotating the whole molecule does not change its internal state.

Coordinates also describe only one member of a conformational ensemble. Rotation around a single bond can move a molecule among several low-energy conformers without changing its covalent graph. A coordinate-based predictor therefore needs a clear contract. Is the input an optimized geometry, an experimental structure, one sampled conformer, or a collection of conformers? The same graph paired with different coordinates can correspond to different energies and interactions.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s08-image11.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A molecule shown through three-dimensional structure and internal coordinates." caption="Cartesian coordinates determine a conformation, while internal coordinates expose bond lengths, angles, and torsions. The scaffold is from <a href='https://doi.org/10.1007/s12039-014-0700-2'>Panini et al.</a>; the rotatable-bond labels and conformer overlay were added for the lecture." %}

Reflection is not the same as rotation. A chiral molecule and its mirror image cannot be superimposed by any proper rotation. Such enantiomers share many scalar properties in an achiral environment, yet they can interact differently with chiral proteins, enzymes, and receptors.

A completely unlabeled two-dimensional graph misses this distinction. A graph with stereochemical edge and atom labels can retain it, and SMILES uses symbols such as `@` and `@@` to encode local handedness. For alanine, changing that symbol distinguishes the two stereoisomers even though their atom and bond inventories agree. The lesson is precise: connectivity alone is insufficient, not that every two-dimensional representation must discard chirality.

### Crystalline solids, lattices, and symmetry

A periodic material cannot be represented by one finite point cloud without also specifying how that point cloud repeats. Let

$$
\mathbf{L}=[\mathbf{a}_1,\mathbf{a}_2,\mathbf{a}_3]^{\mathsf T}
$$

collect three lattice vectors. If the reference cell contains fractional or Cartesian positions $$\mathbf{p}_i$$ and atom types $$a_i$$, the infinite set of periodic images is generated by

$$
\mathbf{p}_{i,\mathbf{n}}
=
\mathbf{p}_i+n_1\mathbf{a}_1+n_2\mathbf{a}_2+n_3\mathbf{a}_3,
\qquad
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^3.
$$

The lattice supplies translations; the basis supplies chemical identities and positions inside one cell. Neither alone specifies a crystal. The stored unit cell is also not unique: the same infinite crystal can be described by different origins and equivalent choices of lattice basis.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s10-image13.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Diagram showing a lattice plus a basis producing a periodic crystal structure." caption="A crystal structure combines a translation lattice with an atomic basis. An exact <a href='https://www.slideserve.com/odessa-spears/chapter-1-crystal-structure'>mirror of the diagram</a> is available, although its original author is unclear." %}

Symmetry describes transformations that leave the represented object unchanged. For a finite molecule, the relevant operations include rotations, reflections, inversion, and improper rotations about a fixed point. A point group collects all such operations for that molecule. Water and methane have different point groups because the transformations that preserve their nuclear arrangements differ.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s11-image15.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Hexagonal close-packed unit cell and packed-sphere structure." caption="A two-atom basis in a hexagonal cell produces the ABAB stacking of the hexagonal close-packed structure. Source: <a href='https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Materials+Science+and+Engineering%3A+An+Introduction%2C+10th+Edition-p-9781119405498'>Callister and Rethwisch, Fig. 3.3</a>." %}

A Bravais lattice classifies translational arrangements of points. In three dimensions, seven crystal systems constrain cell lengths and angles, and centering those cells produces fourteen Bravais lattices. A Bravais lattice is not itself the point group of a particular decorated crystal. It captures translations before the chemical basis is attached.

Crystal symmetry combines those translations with point operations. A rotation followed by a fractional translation produces a screw operation; a reflection followed by a translation produces a glide operation. Compatibility among lattice and point operations yields 230 three-dimensional space groups. The count is not a simple product of fourteen lattices and thirty-two crystallographic point groups because many combinations are incompatible or equivalent.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s14-image20.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Hierarchy relating seven crystal systems, fourteen Bravais lattices, thirty-two point groups, and 230 space groups." caption="Crystal systems, Bravais lattices, point groups, and space groups encode progressively richer periodic symmetry. Source: <a href='https://www.globalsino.com/EM/page4547.html'>GlobalSino</a>." %}

Symmetry restricts measurable properties. Consider a molecule with an inversion center. Inversion leaves the molecule unchanged but reverses a polar vector such as the electric dipole. The only permanent dipole compatible with both statements is zero. The same reasoning constrains tensor properties of crystals: symmetry can force components of dielectric, elastic, piezoelectric, or optical-response tensors to vanish or become equal.

Ideal symmetry is a reference, not a full description of a sample. Vacancies remove atoms from lattice sites; interstitials add atoms between regular sites; substitutions change chemical identity; dislocations, grain boundaries, and surfaces break periodicity on larger scales. These defects can determine carrier concentration, ionic conductivity, mechanical strength, and catalytic activity. A model trained only on perfect unit cells cannot represent those mechanisms unless defects enter its input distribution.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s16-image22.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Lattice diagrams showing vacancies, interstitials, substitutions, and a Frenkel defect." caption="Point defects locally break the symmetry of an ideal lattice and create new chemical environments. Source: <a href='https://www.substech.com/dokuwiki/doku.php?id=imperfections_of_crystal_structure'>SubsTech</a>." %}

## Quantum mechanics

Graphs and coordinates state where nuclei and bonds are placed. Quantum mechanics explains why those arrangements have particular energies and electronic properties.

### The eigenvalue problem and its outputs

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is

$$
\hat{H}\Psi=E\Psi.
$$

The Hamiltonian $$\hat{H}$$ is an operator, not merely a scalar input. It contains the mathematical instructions for kinetic and interaction energies. Under a fixed-nuclei description, atomic identities and nuclear coordinates determine the external potential in that operator. Solving the eigenvalue problem returns allowed states $$\Psi_k$$ and energies $$E_k$$:

$$
\hat{H}\Psi_k=E_k\Psi_k,
\qquad
E_0\leq E_1\leq E_2\leq\cdots.
$$

The lowest eigenvalue $$E_0$$ is the ground-state energy. Higher eigenpairs describe excited stationary states. Energy is one number for each state; the many-electron wavefunction is a high-dimensional function. For $$N_e$$ electrons, its spatial arguments alone occupy $$\mathbb{R}^{3N_e}$$, and a complete description also includes spin. This output mismatch matters for machine learning: predicting one total energy is not the same problem as representing the electronic state that generates many observables.

The wavefunction is a probability amplitude. For one particle, $$\lvert\Psi(\mathbf{r})\rvert^2$$ is a spatial probability density. The sign or complex phase of $$\Psi$$ still matters because amplitudes interfere before their magnitudes are squared. For many electrons, $$\lvert\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{N_e})\rvert^2$$ is a joint density in configuration space, not a three-dimensional picture around one nucleus.

### Hydrogen and atomic orbitals

The hydrogen atom supplies the canonical exactly solvable three-dimensional example. After separating the center-of-mass motion, the relative coordinate obeys

$$
\left(
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2
-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}
\right)
\Psi(r,\theta,\phi)
=E\Psi(r,\theta,\phi),
$$

where $$\mu$$ is the electron--proton reduced mass. Hydrogen-like one-electron ions share the same analytic structure with a modified nuclear charge. General many-electron atoms do not, because electron--electron repulsion couples their coordinates.

Hydrogen eigenfunctions separate into radial and angular factors,

$$
\Psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi)
=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi).
$$

The quantum numbers identify the shell, angular momentum, and orientation. The familiar $$s$$, $$p$$, $$d$$, and $$f$$ labels correspond to $$\ell=0,1,2,3$$. An orbital is a one-electron function, and the displayed lobes usually show an isosurface of its amplitude with color marking sign. They should not be read as classical paths followed by electrons.

### Approximations and the many-electron problem

The stationary equation already leaves out time dependence. The full quantum state obeys $$i\hbar\,\partial_t\Psi=\hat{H}\Psi$$; a time-independent eigenstate is a special solution when the Hamiltonian is time independent. The lecture focuses on that stationary setting because energies and ground-state properties are the immediate targets.

Molecules also contain moving nuclei. The Born--Oppenheimer approximation uses their much larger masses to separate electronic and nuclear motion (<span id="cite-born1927"></span>[Born and Oppenheimer, 1927](#ref-born1927)). For fixed nuclear coordinates $$\mathbf{R}$$, one solves the electronic problem first. The resulting electronic energy as a function of $$\mathbf{R}$$ becomes a potential-energy surface for nuclear motion. This is an approximation, not a declaration that electrons and nuclei never couple; it can fail near degeneracies and in strongly nonadiabatic processes.

Before that separation, a nonrelativistic molecular Hamiltonian can be organized as

$$
\hat{H}
=
\hat{T}_e+\hat{T}_N
+\hat{V}_{eN}+\hat{V}_{ee}+\hat{V}_{NN}.
$$

The terms are electronic kinetic energy, nuclear kinetic energy, electron--nucleus attraction, electron--electron repulsion, and nucleus--nucleus repulsion. For example,

$$
\hat{T}_e=-\sum_{i=1}^{N_e}\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_i^2,
\qquad
\hat{V}_{ee}=\sum_{i<j}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\lvert\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_j\rvert}.
$$

The electron--electron term prevents the wavefunction from separating into independent one-electron factors. Fermionic antisymmetry adds another constraint: exchanging two electrons changes the sign of the state. A Slater determinant enforces antisymmetry for orbital products, but a single determinant cannot describe all electron correlation. The difficulty is therefore structural, not simply that the input contains more atoms.

Atomic orbitals provide a basis for approximate molecular orbitals. In a linear combination of atomic orbitals,

$$
\phi_p(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu p}\chi_\mu(\mathbf{r}),
$$

where $$\chi_\mu$$ are basis functions centered on atoms and $$c_{\mu p}$$ are coefficients. In-phase overlap places amplitude between nuclei and forms a bonding combination. Out-of-phase overlap creates a node between nuclei and forms an antibonding combination. The orbitals belong to the molecular system; they are not immutable atomic objects pasted together.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s28-image34.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Constructive and destructive combinations of atomic orbitals forming bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals." caption="Adding atomic amplitudes produces bonding density between nuclei; subtracting them creates an antibonding node. Source: <a href='https://chem.libretexts.org/Courses/Pacific_Union_College/Quantum_Chemistry/09%3A_Chemical_Bonding_in_Diatomic_Molecules/9.05%3A_Bonding_and_Antibonding_Orbitals'>Chemistry LibreTexts</a>." %}

Density functional theory changes the central variable from the full many-electron wavefunction to the electron density $$n(\mathbf{r})$$. The Hohenberg--Kohn result establishes that the ground-state density determines the external potential and therefore ground-state observables (<span id="cite-hk1964"></span>[Hohenberg and Kohn, 1964](#ref-hk1964)). Kohn--Sham DFT introduces auxiliary orbitals that reproduce the density while collecting the unknown many-body contribution into an exchange--correlation functional (<span id="cite-ks1965"></span>[Kohn and Sham, 1965](#ref-ks1965)).

This reduction makes real molecules and materials tractable, but not cheap or exact. Conventional Kohn--Sham implementations often scale roughly cubically with system size because of orbital operations; particular algorithms, basis sets, and sparsity assumptions change that scaling. Steeper costs such as sixth-order behavior are more characteristic of some correlated wavefunction methods than of DFT as a universal rule. Accuracy also depends on the functional, basis, pseudopotential, numerical settings, and chemical regime.

## Electronic properties: from orbitals to bands

An electron in an atom does not orbit the nucleus like a planet. Atomic and molecular orbitals are probability amplitudes with characteristic energies. Filling those levels connects the quantum state to qualitative chemical behavior.

### Frontier molecular orbitals

The highest occupied molecular orbital, or HOMO, is the occupied orbital with the largest orbital energy in a chosen mean-field description. Removing an electron often involves frontier occupied states, so HOMO character is associated qualitatively with electron donation. “Highest occupied” is an occupation label, not a claim that this orbital contains the molecule's total energy.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s32-image36.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="P2N3 minus molecular orbitals with their occupations and energies." caption="The occupied and unoccupied orbitals of P2N3− lie on opposite sides of the frontier-orbital boundary. Source: <a href='https://doi.org/10.1039/C5SC04667J'>Hou et al.</a>" %}

The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital, or LUMO, is the unoccupied orbital with the smallest orbital energy. Adding or exciting an electron often involves this frontier empty state, so its shape indicates where accepting character may be concentrated. HOMO and LUMO pictures are useful chemical summaries, but their meaning depends on the electronic-structure approximation.

The HOMO--LUMO difference is

$$
\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HL}}
=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{LUMO}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HOMO}}.
$$

A smaller frontier-orbital gap often correlates with easier low-energy excitation and greater chemical softness; a larger gap often accompanies lower polarizability or reactivity. These are trends, not universal laws. A Kohn--Sham orbital gap is generally not identical to an experimental optical gap, a fundamental charge gap, or an activation barrier. The word “gap” must retain the measurement or theory that defines it.

### Bands in periodic solids

When many atoms repeat in a solid, each atomic-like level produces many combinations. As the number of unit cells grows, the allowed levels become densely spaced and form energy bands. Periodicity labels electronic states by crystal momentum $$\mathbf{k}$$, and a band structure plots the dispersions $$E_n(\mathbf{k})$$ across the Brillouin zone.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s35-image38.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Atomic levels splitting into increasingly dense levels and then an energy band." caption="As the number of coupled atomic centers grows, discrete levels split into densely spaced bands. Source: <a href='https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry_Textbook_Maps/Advanced_Theoretical_Chemistry_%28Simons%29/02%3A_Model_Problems_That_Form_Important_Starting_Points/2.02%3A_Bands_of_Orbitals_in_Solids'>Simons</a>." %}

The valence band is the highest occupied band manifold in the simple semiconductor picture. The conduction band is the next higher, usually mostly empty manifold into which carriers can be excited. The band gap is an interval of energy with no allowed bulk states for the ideal periodic system.

Metals, insulators, and semiconductors differ in band occupation as well as gap size. A metal may have a partially filled band or overlapping valence and conduction bands; saying only “no band gap” is an introductory shorthand. An insulator has a sufficiently large gap that ordinary thermal excitation produces few mobile carriers. A semiconductor has a smaller gap whose carrier population can be changed through temperature, doping, illumination, or fields. Defects can add states inside the ideal gap, reconnecting this electronic picture to the earlier discussion of imperfect crystals.

## Intra- and intermolecular forces

Quantum mechanics defines the energy. Chemistry often reasons through recurring bonding patterns and forces derived from that energy. If $$E(\mathbf{R})$$ is a potential energy for nuclear coordinates, then

$$
\mathbf{F}_i=-\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_i}E(\mathbf{R}).
$$

The categories below are practical descriptions of electronic organization, not independent fundamental interactions added on top of quantum mechanics.

### Covalent, ionic, and metallic bonding

Covalent bonding shares electronic density between atoms. The overlap is directional, so covalent molecules possess characteristic bond angles and local geometries. Bonding molecular orbitals stabilize density between nuclei, linking this chemical picture to the linear-combination construction above.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s40-image41.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Methane with shared electron pairs in four carbon-hydrogen bonds." caption="Covalent bonding places shared electronic density between atoms and creates directional molecular structure. Source: <a href='https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Methane_shells.svg'>Wikimedia Commons</a>." %}

Ionic bonding emphasizes charge transfer and Coulomb attraction between oppositely charged ions. Sodium chloride and magnesium oxide are standard examples, though their charge distributions are not perfectly point-like. Metallic bonding emphasizes valence electrons delocalized across many atomic centers. The resulting mobile electronic states help explain electrical and thermal conduction.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s42-image43.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Regular array of positive metal ions surrounded by delocalized electrons." caption="The metallic-bonding picture spreads valence electrons across an extended array of ionic cores. Source: <a href='https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Metallic_Bonding_Example.svg'>Wikimedia Commons</a>." %}

These labels are limiting descriptions. Real compounds can mix covalent, ionic, and metallic character, and the balance can change with crystal structure, oxidation state, or environment.

### Hydrogen bonds, permanent dipoles, and dispersion

An intermolecular interaction acts between molecular units, although the same physics can also appear between distant groups within a large molecule. A hydrogen bond forms when hydrogen covalently attached to an electronegative donor interacts with an electron-rich acceptor. The interaction is directional, stronger than a generic orientation-averaged dipolar contact, and central to water structure, DNA base pairing, protein folding, and molecular recognition.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s43-image44.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Water molecules connected by directional hydrogen bonds." caption="A hydrogen bond joins a polarized donor bond to an electron-rich acceptor along a preferred geometry. Source: <a href='https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/10-1-intermolecular-forces'>OpenStax Chemistry 2e, Fig. 10.10</a>." %}

A permanent electric dipole measures separation between positive and negative charge. Two polar molecules therefore interact in an orientation-dependent way: one alignment is attractive, another is repulsive. Thermal rotation changes the average interaction, so a fixed pair geometry and a bulk liquid do not share one universal dipole--dipole energy.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s44-image46.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Attractive and repulsive orientations of two molecular dipoles." caption="Permanent dipoles attract or repel according to their relative orientation. Source: <a href='https://www.chemistrylearner.com/chemical-bonds/dipole-dipole-forces'>Chemistry Learner</a>." %}

London dispersion requires no permanent dipole. Instantaneous fluctuations in one electron cloud induce correlated fluctuations in another, producing attraction. Dispersion is present in all molecules and grows with polarizability and contact area. “van der Waals” is used inconsistently: it may refer broadly to several noncovalent interactions or more narrowly to dispersion plus short-range repulsion, so the intended convention should be stated.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s45-image47.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Two atoms with correlated instantaneous dipoles illustrating London dispersion." caption="Correlated charge fluctuations generate London dispersion even between nonpolar species. Source: <a href='https://www.chemistrylearner.com/chemical-bonds/van-der-waals-forces'>Chemistry Learner</a>." %}

The lecture's approximate energy hierarchy is useful for scale:

| Interaction | Typical energy per interaction |
|---|---:|
| Covalent bond | 200--800 kJ/mol |
| Ionic bond | 100--400 kJ/mol |
| Hydrogen bond | 10--40 kJ/mol |
| Dipole--dipole interaction | 5--20 kJ/mol |
| London dispersion contact | 0.1--10 kJ/mol |

These ranges overlap and depend strongly on medium, geometry, charge, and the definition of one “interaction.” Intramolecular bonds are usually stronger individually, while a biomolecule or condensed phase contains many noncovalent contacts. Their collective contribution can therefore compete with a smaller number of strong bonds.

## Statistical mechanics: from one molecule to many

A microscopic state specifies positions and momenta, or an appropriate quantum state, for all particles. A macrostate specifies coarse conditions such as particle number, volume, pressure, and temperature. Many microstates are compatible with the same macrostate. Statistical mechanics assigns probabilities across them and connects microscopic energies to measured averages.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s48-image48.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Macrostate and microstate sketches illustrating an ensemble of microscopic realizations." caption="A macrostate fixes coarse conditions, while an ensemble represents the many compatible microscopic realizations. Source: <a href='https://studylib.net/doc/27659869/all-thermo-notes'>Devriendt, Fig. 2.2</a>." %}

### The Boltzmann distribution

For a canonical ensemble at fixed particle number, volume, and temperature, a state $$q$$ with energy $$E(q)$$ has density

$$
p(q)=\frac{e^{-\beta E(q)}}{Z},
\qquad
Z=\int e^{-\beta E(q)}\,dq,
\qquad
\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}.
$$

The partition function $$Z$$ normalizes the distribution. For two states,

$$
\frac{p(q_2)}{p(q_1)}
=
e^{-\beta[E(q_2)-E(q_1)]}.
$$

An energy difference therefore matters relative to the thermal scale $$k_{B}T$$. At low temperature, high-energy states are exponentially suppressed. Raising the temperature flattens the distribution and makes them more accessible. An observable is an ensemble average,

$$
\langle A\rangle=\int p(q)A(q)\,dq,
$$

not necessarily the value at the minimum-energy structure.

### Free energy, enthalpy, and entropy

The canonical partition function generates the Helmholtz free energy,

$$
F=-k_{B}T\log Z.
$$

This point corrects a common shorthand in introductory slides: $$-k_{B}T\log Z$$ is Helmholtz free energy for the canonical ensemble, not automatically Gibbs free energy. At fixed temperature and pressure, the relevant thermodynamic potential is Gibbs free energy,

$$
G=H-TS,
$$

and it is generated from the isothermal--isobaric partition function. At constant $$T$$ and $$P$$, spontaneous change decreases $$G$$ until equilibrium is reached.

Enthalpy is

$$
H=U+PV,
$$

where $$U=\langle E\rangle$$ is the ensemble-averaged internal energy in the simple classical picture. Entropy is

$$
S=-k_{B}\int p(q)\log p(q)\,dq.
$$

The minus sign is essential: spreading probability over more compatible states raises entropy. The combination $$G=H-TS$$ shows why a process can be favored by a lower enthalpy, a higher entropy, or both. Entropy is not merely “disorder,” and enthalpy is not entropy with a different symbol; they are distinct quantities joined by the free-energy balance.

## The energetics and dynamics of chemical change

Chemistry is about changes of state as well as static structures. A conformational change alters geometry without changing covalent connectivity, such as rotation around a single bond or folding of a protein. A chemical reaction changes connectivity as old bonds break and new bonds form. Melting and other phase changes alter collective organization without necessarily changing molecular identity. These transformations occupy different regions and paths on an energy landscape.

### Molecular dynamics connects forces to ensembles

Molecular dynamics propagates coordinates through time using forces from an energy model. In deterministic Newtonian dynamics,

$$
m_i\frac{d^2\mathbf{R}_i}{dt^2}
=
-\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_i}U(\mathbf{R}).
$$

A Langevin thermostat adds friction and random forcing. For a common convention,

$$
d\mathbf{R}_t=\mathbf{V}_t\,dt,
$$

$$
d\mathbf{V}_t
=
-\frac{\nabla U(\mathbf{R}_t)}{m}\,dt
-\gamma\mathbf{V}_t\,dt
+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma k_{B}T}{m}}\,d\mathbf{W}_t.
$$

The first term is acceleration from the potential, the second removes momentum through friction, and the third supplies thermal noise. Their fluctuation--dissipation relation is what makes the stationary distribution consistent with temperature $$T$$. Simply adding arbitrary noise would not guarantee Boltzmann sampling.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s54-image56.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated molecular-dynamics trajectory of a membrane protein, lipid bilayer, water, and ions." caption="Molecular dynamics propagates a membrane protein, lipid bilayer, water, and ions through time under forces derived from an energy model." %}

Trajectories provide data for protein folding, ligand binding, diffusion, and the temperature-dependent behavior of materials. Equilibrium sampling and dynamical prediction are related but not identical goals. A thermostat may produce the correct stationary distribution while altering real-time kinetics. The integration step resolves fast vibrations, while rare transitions may occur on timescales far beyond an affordable trajectory.

### Potential-energy surfaces

A potential-energy surface maps nuclear geometry to energy,

$$
U:\mathbf{R}\mapsto U(\mathbf{R}).
$$

Although it is often drawn as a two-dimensional landscape, a molecule with many atoms has a high-dimensional surface. Low regions correspond to favorable geometries. A local minimum is stable against sufficiently small displacements; it need not be the global minimum. Diamond is a familiar metastable example: a barrier allows it to persist even when another carbon phase is thermodynamically preferred under the same conditions.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s55-image57.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Potential-energy surface for a water geometry plotted against bond angle and bond length." caption="A low-dimensional slice of the water potential-energy surface maps bond length and angle to energy. Source: <a href='https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Potential_Energy_Surface_for_Water.png'>Wikimedia Commons</a>." %}

Reaction pathways connect minima. Along a minimum-energy path, the transition state is the maximum-energy point along the path, but geometrically it is a first-order saddle: energy decreases in the reaction direction on either side and increases in the remaining local directions. Minima represent reactants, products, or metastable intermediates; saddle points organize the routes between them.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s56-image58.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Potential-energy surface labeled with reactant and product minima, saddle points, and transition structures." caption="Minima, first- and second-order saddles, and a valley--ridge inflection point organize the local topology of an energy surface. Source: <a href='https://doi.org/10.1002/wcms.34'>Schlegel, Fig. 1</a>." %}

### Thermodynamics and kinetics answer different questions

For a process at constant temperature and pressure,

$$
\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S
$$

compares the equilibrium preference of products and reactants. A negative $$\Delta G$$ makes the forward direction thermodynamically favorable under the stated conditions. It does not say that the process will be fast.

The activation free energy $$\Delta G^{\ddagger}$$ controls the rate of barrier crossing. A reaction can be thermodynamically favorable but kinetically slow when the barrier is high. A catalyst changes the pathway and lowers the activation barrier without changing the equilibrium free-energy difference between the same initial and final states.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-02/s57-image59.webp" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Reaction-energy profiles comparing uncatalyzed and catalyzed activation barriers." caption="The reaction free-energy difference sets equilibrium preference, while the activation barrier sets the rate; a catalyst lowers the latter. Source: <a href='https://openlibrary.org/books/OL39891834M/Essential_Biochemistry_for_Medicine'>Fry, Fig. 9.2</a>." %}

The layers now reconnect. Strings, graphs, coordinates, and periodic cells encode the object. Quantum mechanics defines electronic states and energies. Energy gradients produce forces. Statistical mechanics converts microscopic energies into probability distributions and macroscopic observables. Dynamics determines how the system moves through those distributions and whether it crosses relevant barriers on an observed timescale. A molecular machine-learning model can approximate any one of these maps, but its input, target, symmetries, and guarantees depend on which physical layer it replaces.
