## Molecular dynamics

Molecular dynamics is a computer simulation method for following the motion of atoms and molecules over time. A potential-energy function $$U(R)$$ supplies the force, while an integrator turns that force into a trajectory.

For positions $$R_t$$, velocities $$V_t$$, and masses $$m$$, a Langevin description is

$$
dR_t=V_t\,dt,
\qquad
dV_t=
-\frac{\nabla U(R_t)}{m}\,dt
-\gamma V_t\,dt
+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma k_{\mathrm B}T}{m}}\,dW_t.
$$

The first term is Newtonian acceleration. The second is friction. The Brownian increment $$dW_t$$ injects thermal noise, with its amplitude tied to friction and temperature by the fluctuation–dissipation relation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s02-membrane-protein-md.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated molecular system evolving inside a solvated membrane environment." caption="Molecular dynamics turns a molecular model into a time-dependent atomistic trajectory." %}

The equations alone are not yet a simulation. A practical algorithm alternates position prediction, boundary handling, force evaluation, velocity correction, and calculation of observables. Every step uses a finite timestep, so the integrator and timestep are part of the model actually executed.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s02-md-loop.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flowchart of a molecular dynamics integrator from initialization through force evaluation and observable calculation." caption="A molecular-dynamics loop repeatedly advances coordinates, recomputes forces, corrects velocities, and records observables." %}

For deterministic Newtonian dynamics, a standard realization of this loop is velocity Verlet. If $$a_n=-M^{-1}\nabla U(R_n)$$, then

$$
R_{n+1}=R_n+\Delta t,V_n+\frac{\Delta t^2}{2}a_n,
$$

followed by a fresh force evaluation $$a_{n+1}=-M^{-1}\nabla U(R_{n+1})$$ and the velocity correction

$$
V_{n+1}=V_n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(a_n+a_{n+1}).
$$

The second force evaluation is not bookkeeping. It is what couples the newly predicted geometry back into the momentum update. Langevin integrators split this deterministic motion from damping and random kicks. In either case, the timestep must resolve the fastest retained motion. If a bond vibration has period $$10\,\mathrm{fs}$$, using a step of the same order does not merely make the trajectory coarse; it can make the discrete dynamics unstable. Constraints on fast X--H bonds are often introduced precisely so that a somewhat larger step remains numerically safe.

The noise term also has a precise scale. Over a finite interval $$\Delta t$$, the Wiener increment is distributed as $$\Delta W\sim\mathcal N(0,\Delta t I)$$. The random velocity kick therefore scales as $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$, whereas the deterministic force and friction increments scale as $$\Delta t$$. Friction without the matched noise would cool the system toward rest. Noise without friction would heat it indefinitely. Their pairing is what makes the canonical temperature stationary under the idealized Langevin model.

### Thermodynamic ensembles

Molecular-dynamics methods are often classified by which thermodynamic quantities are controlled. The notation uses $$N$$ for particle number, $$V$$ for volume, $$E$$ for total energy, $$T$$ for temperature, and $$P$$ for pressure.

- **NVE**, the microcanonical ensemble, keeps particle number, volume, and energy fixed. The system is isolated from an external thermostat or barostat.
- **NVT**, the canonical ensemble, keeps particle number, volume, and temperature fixed. A thermostat exchanges energy with the system to maintain the target temperature.
- **NPT**, the isothermal–isobaric ensemble, keeps particle number, pressure, and temperature fixed. A thermostat and barostat allow energy and volume to fluctuate.

| Ensemble | Held fixed | Allowed exchange | Typical role in the lecture workflow |
|---|---|---|---|
| NVE | $$N,V,E$$ | none | test energy conservation; production dynamics without external controls |
| NVT | $$N,V,T$$ | heat through a thermostat | temperature ramp and canonical production |
| NPT | $$N,P,T$$ | heat and volume through thermostat and barostat | pressure equilibration and constant-pressure production |

The choice determines the equilibrium distribution being sampled. It also determines which dynamic quantities should be interpreted cautiously: a thermostat that is appropriate for canonical averages can perturb time correlations, and a barostat introduces its own volume dynamics.

This distinction separates a control variable from an instantaneous value. NVT does not mean that the kinetic temperature is numerically constant at every step. It means that the stationary distribution is conditioned on the prescribed temperature, while energy fluctuates through the thermostat. Likewise, NPT allows the simulation cell to fluctuate around the pressure-controlled equilibrium. An NVE energy trace is therefore a useful integration diagnostic: systematic drift can expose an excessive timestep, inconsistent force, or loose constraint tolerance. It is not evidence by itself that the force field is chemically accurate.

### The simulation workflow

Initialization is the key practical issue. A typical workflow begins with preprocessing: specify the Hamiltonian or force field, choose an integrator and discretization, construct boundary conditions, and select an initial state. The coordinates are then energy-minimized, often with L-BFGS or FIRE, to remove severe overlaps or strained geometries.

The system is heated through a temperature ramp, commonly under NVT control, and equilibrated at the desired pressure under NPT. Only after these transients are removed does the production run begin under the ensemble appropriate to the observable. Postprocessing saves the trajectory and computes thermodynamic and dynamical properties.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s04-md-workflow.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated workflow from molecular system setup through minimization, heating, equilibration, production, and postprocessing." caption="The MD workflow separates system construction, minimization, heating, equilibration, production, and analysis." %}

A long trajectory cannot repair a poorly prepared system. A wrong protonation state, density, box size, composition, or force field remains wrong after equilibration; a temperature ramp that is too abrupt can destabilize the structure before the intended ensemble is reached.

Equilibration and production answer different questions. During equilibration, the distribution is still forgetting the constructed initial condition, so discarding an initial segment is part of the estimator rather than wasted computation. During production, frames should be sampled often enough to resolve the observable but not treated as independent simply because they are stored separately. For an observable $$A_t$$, the effective sample size is controlled by its autocorrelation time,

$$
N_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx \frac{N}{1+2\sum_{k\ge 1}\rho_A(k)}.
$$

Ten thousand strongly correlated frames may contain far less information than ten thousand independent samples. This is why the workflow ends with analysis rather than with the trajectory file itself.

### Force fields from classical models, electronic structure, and machine learning

There are several ways to compute the force field. Classical force fields are efficient but depend on restricted analytic forms and fitted parameters. *Ab initio* electronic-structure methods are more accurate and transferable in principle, but far slower. Machine-learning force fields seek the middle ground: approximate high-level energies and forces with a model cheap enough for simulation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s05-ml-potential-landscape.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Landscape of atomistic systems connecting accurate ab initio calculations, machine learning, and efficient force fields." caption="Machine-learning potentials aim to combine electronic-structure accuracy with force-field efficiency across molecules, materials, and biomolecules." %}

“Fast and accurate” is relative to the configuration distribution on which the potential is used. An ML potential can be orders of magnitude faster than DFT while still being slower than a simple classical force field. Its reliability also depends on whether the production trajectory stays inside the chemical and thermodynamic domain represented by its training data.

An energy-based model makes the force field

$$
F_i(R)=-\nabla_{R_i}E_\theta(R).
$$

This construction guarantees that the learned force is conservative for a fixed model and that a translation-invariant energy yields zero net force. But it does not guarantee the correct energy surface. A small average force error can become a large simulation error if it changes the curvature near a minimum, lowers a barrier, or creates an unphysical low-energy region that the trajectory discovers only after many steps. The simulator is an adversarial user of its potential: it repeatedly visits whatever geometry the learned energy makes accessible.

The relevant validation is therefore layered. Static energy and force errors test local interpolation. Short rollouts test integration stability and temperature control. Long rollouts test whether structural distributions, transition statistics, and material observables remain plausible. These tests are not interchangeable, which is why accelerating a force evaluation does not remove the timescale problem that motivates the second half of the lecture.

## What molecular dynamics can measure

A trajectory is useful because it converts a force model into structural, thermodynamic, and kinetic observables. The lecture uses conformation analysis, free energies, and diffusion coefficients as three examples.

### Conformation analysis

Molecules—especially biomolecules—adopt many three-dimensional conformations. Molecular dynamics explores these shapes and the transitions among them. The raw coordinates are high-dimensional, so analysis often projects the trajectory onto dihedral angles or other collective variables.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s06-torsion-energy.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="A molecular torsion and energy profiles as a function of a dihedral angle." caption="Dihedral angles provide compact coordinates for rotations around molecular bonds." %}

A Ramachandran plot records two backbone dihedral angles and shows which regions of conformational space are occupied.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s06-dihedral-landscape.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Ramachandran-style free-energy map over two molecular dihedral angles." caption="A two-dimensional dihedral map summarizes recurrent conformations and barriers visited by the trajectory." %}

Other collective variables can be hand-designed geometric quantities or learned low-dimensional representations.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s06-neural-collective-variable.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Neural dimensionality reduction of molecular conformations into a free-energy landscape." caption="Learned collective variables compress atomistic configurations into a low-dimensional landscape for analysis." %}

The projection is not neutral. Two distinct structures may overlap in a poor collective variable, and a barrier can disappear when hidden coordinates are marginalized. The plot describes the chosen representation of the trajectory, not the full configuration space.

For a trajectory projected to coordinates $$z=\xi(R)$$, the apparent free-energy surface is

$$
F(z)=-k_{\mathrm B}T\log p(z)+C.
$$

This is a probability statement, not simply the potential energy evaluated at one representative structure. A wide family of moderately energetic configurations can have lower free energy than a single low-energy conformation because entropy contributes through the marginalized probability mass. If a histogram bin has probability $$0.20$$ and another has probability $$0.02$$ at room temperature, their projected free-energy difference is $$k_{\mathrm B}T\log 10\approx1.36\,\mathrm{kcal\,mol^{-1}}$$. The arithmetic is simple; obtaining converged probabilities is the difficult part.

A collective variable should therefore be judged by what it retains. Dihedral angles are natural for local bond rotations. Distances or contacts may separate folded from unfolded structures. A learned coordinate may discover a slower combination, but its visual smoothness is not proof that it is kinetically sufficient. If structures with different future behavior collapse to the same $$z$$, a one-dimensional landscape can look clean while hiding memory in discarded coordinates.

### Folding and binding free energies

For a region $$A$$ of configuration space with potential energy $$U(x)$$, define the restricted configurational partition function

$$
Z_A=\int_A \exp\!\left[-\frac{U(x)}{k_{\mathrm B}T}\right]dx.
$$

The free-energy difference between regions $$A$$ and $$B$$ is

$$
\Delta G_{A\to B}
=-k_{\mathrm B}T\log Z_B
+k_{\mathrm B}T\log Z_A.
$$

Folding and binding calculations use this relation to compare folded and unfolded states or bound and unbound states.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s07-binding-free-energy-cycle.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Thermodynamic cycle for computing protein-ligand binding free energy." caption="Binding free energies can be organized through a thermodynamic cycle connecting solvated and complexed states." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s07-folding-landscape.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Rugged protein-folding energy landscape descending from unfolded states to a native state." caption="The folding landscape contains many intermediate conformations and barriers between unfolded and native basins." %}

The logarithm makes missing probability mass consequential. A trajectory that never crosses between basins cannot determine their equilibrium weights merely by running longer inside one basin.

Suppose a simulation observes folded and unfolded basins with equilibrium probabilities $$p_F=0.9$$ and $$p_U=0.1$$. The folding free energy is

$$
\Delta G_{U\to F}=-k_{\mathrm B}T\log\frac{p_F}{p_U}
=-k_{\mathrm B}T\log 9.
$$

At $$300\,\mathrm K$$, where $$k_{\mathrm B}T\approx0.596\,\mathrm{kcal\,mol^{-1}}$$, this is about $$-1.31\,\mathrm{kcal\,mol^{-1}}$$. If the observed 9:1 ratio came from a trajectory trapped in the folded basin rather than from repeated equilibrium exchange, the same calculation would be numerically precise and scientifically wrong. Basin populations need mixing, independent replicas, or an enhanced-sampling estimator with valid reweighting.

Binding adds another layer. The free-energy difference concerns normalized bound and unbound ensembles, including solvent and standard-state conventions. A favorable binding free energy does not specify the association or dissociation rate. Two ligands can share the same equilibrium constant while having very different residence times because the barriers along their binding pathways differ. Free energy measures relative probability mass; kinetics measures probability flow between regions.

### Diffusion coefficients

Diffusion measures how rapidly particles spread through random motion. Molecular dynamics estimates it from the mean-squared displacement. For a selected set $$S$$ of particles,

$$
\operatorname{MSD}_S(\tau)
=\frac{1}{|S|}
\sum_{i\in S}
\left\|x_{t+\tau,i}-x_{t,i}\right\|^2,
$$

usually averaged over many choices of the time origin $$t$$. In $$d$$ dimensions, a diffusive long-time regime satisfies

$$
\operatorname{MSD}(\tau)\approx 2dD\tau,
$$

so the slope determines the diffusion coefficient $$D$$.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s08-ion-diffusion.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Atomic diffusion pathways in a solid-state material." caption="Atomistic trajectories reveal how mobile species move through a host material." %}

The slope must be fitted in the correct regime. At very short times motion can be ballistic; finite cells and insufficiently long trajectories can distort the apparent long-time behavior.

The time-origin average matters. With frames $$x_0,\ldots,x_N$$ and lag of $$k$$ frames, one estimates

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{MSD}}(k)
=\frac{1}{N-k+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-k}
\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}
\lVert x_{n+k,i}-x_{n,i}\rVert^2.
$$

For three-dimensional isotropic diffusion, the long-time slope is $$6D$$, not $$D$$. If the fitted slope is $$0.12\,\mathrm{\mathring A^2/ps}$$, then $$D=0.02\,\mathrm{\mathring A^2/ps}$$. Periodic boundary conditions require unwrapped coordinates: using wrapped positions can turn a genuine cell-crossing displacement into an artificial jump in the opposite direction. Drift of the entire simulation cell or center of mass should also be removed when it is not part of the transport process.

Diffusion illustrates why a trajectory generator cannot be validated only frame by frame. A model could reproduce the correct one-frame distribution and still move too quickly, too slowly, or by the wrong hopping mechanism. The slope, directional anisotropy, waiting-time distribution, and temperature dependence probe dynamical information that a static structural score does not contain.

## Why machine-learning potentials still need acceleration

Machine-learning potentials dramatically reduce the cost of a force evaluation, but rare events can still occur on timescales far beyond an atomistic timestep. Bond rotations, protein folding, ligand binding, nucleation, and ion hops may require many millions or billions of steps.

The lecture separates two acceleration axes. **Spatial coarse-graining** reduces the number of modeled degrees of freedom. **Temporal coarse-graining** advances the system over a longer effective interval or generates a distribution over distant future states.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s09-spatial-coarse-graining.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Pipeline for learning a spatially coarse-grained molecular force field." caption="Spatial coarse-graining maps atomistic configurations to fewer variables, learns coarse interactions, and decodes coarse trajectories." %}

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s09-temporal-coarse-graining.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Temporal coarse-graining from an initial molecular state to a distribution at a later lag." caption="Temporal coarse-graining replaces many small integration steps with a longer-lag transition model. [Timewarp](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/a598c367280f9054434fdcc227ce4d38-Abstract-Conference.html) uses such long proposals inside an equilibrium-correct MCMC chain." %}

These approaches solve different bottlenecks. Fewer particles make every force evaluation cheaper. A longer transition step attacks the number of sequential evaluations. Neither guarantees that the accelerated trajectory retains the observables of the original atomistic dynamics.

The scale mismatch is severe even after replacing electronic structure with a neural potential. A $$1\,\mathrm{fs}$$ integrator needs one million sequential steps to reach a nanosecond and one billion to reach a microsecond. A thousand-fold cheaper force evaluation does not change this count. Moreover, a rare event with mean waiting time $$1\,\mathrm{ms}$$ is unlikely to appear in a single microsecond trajectory. The bottleneck is not only floating-point throughput; it is the serial waiting time imposed by metastability.

Spatial and temporal reduction also preserve different probability objects. Let $$z=M(R)$$ be a coarse mapping. A spatial model approximates the marginal equilibrium distribution

$$
p(z)=\int p(R)\,\delta\!\left(z-M(R)\right)dR
$$

and, if dynamics matter, an effective evolution on $$z$$ that generally contains friction and memory. A temporal model instead approximates a transition kernel $$K_\tau(R'\mid R)$$ at lag $$\tau$$ or a joint distribution over several frames. Matching $$p(z)$$ does not imply the correct kinetics on $$z$$. Matching an endpoint kernel at one lag does not imply correct paths at shorter lags. Every acceleration claim should name which object it preserves.

## Classical acceleration

### Coarse-grained molecular dynamics

Classical coarse-grained MD groups several atoms into a bead, defines a force field on the beads, and simulates the resulting reduced system.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s10-coarse-grained-chain.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Atomistic molecular chain grouped into a smaller number of coarse-grained beads." caption="Coarse-graining replaces groups of atoms with effective interaction sites." %}

Removing fast internal degrees of freedom smooths the energy landscape and can permit a much longer timestep—of order picoseconds in the lecture's comparison. The reduced particle count also lowers the cost of each force evaluation.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s10-landscape-smoothing.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Rugged all-atom energy landscape compared with a smoother coarse-grained landscape." caption="Integrating out atomistic detail can smooth a landscape and make longer numerical steps possible." %}

The speed comes from information loss. Coarse models may fail to represent hydrogen bonds, ligand binding, or other phenomena that depend on omitted atoms. Their effective interactions can be state-dependent, reducing transferability. They can also distort dynamical observables because a smoother coordinate does not inherit the original friction and memory automatically.

Formally, an equilibrium coarse potential is a potential of mean force,

$$
U_{\mathrm{CG}}(z)
=-k_{\mathrm B}T\log
\int \exp[-\beta U(R)]\,
\delta\!\left(z-M(R)\right)dR+C.
$$

It depends on the mapping, thermodynamic state, and atomistic model. The integral explains both the smoother landscape and the transfer problem: the effective interaction already averages over all eliminated coordinates. Changing temperature, composition, or chemical environment changes that average. A pairwise bead potential fitted in one state need not remain valid in another.

The same loss of information makes backmapping one-to-many. If a bead records only the center of mass of several atoms, many bond rotations and side-chain arrangements produce the same coarse coordinate. A deterministic decoder selects one representative and underestimates conditional uncertainty. A generative backmapper instead targets $$p(R\mid z)$$, subject to bond geometry, chirality, steric exclusion, and consistency with the coarse state. It can reconstruct atomistic detail for analysis, but it cannot recover dynamical history that was never retained.

### Enhanced sampling

Enhanced-sampling methods modify how the system explores configuration space.

**Temperature annealing** temporarily raises the temperature so that barriers are crossed more often. The system must later be cooled and interpreted under the intended target conditions.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s11-temperature-annealing.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Probability distributions broadening as temperature increases during annealing." caption="Temperature annealing broadens the sampled distribution and increases barrier-crossing probability." %}

**Replica exchange** runs several replicas at different temperatures and proposes swaps between them. Hot replicas cross barriers readily; accepted swaps can bring those configurations into colder replicas.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s11-replica-exchange.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Replica-exchange trajectories moving among a ladder of temperatures." caption="Replica exchange couples simulations at several temperatures through accepted state swaps." %}

**Metadynamics** adds a history-dependent bias along selected collective variables. Depositing bias where the system has already visited fills free-energy wells and encourages exploration elsewhere.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s11-metadynamics.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated metadynamics bias filling a free-energy basin along a collective variable." caption="Metadynamics progressively fills visited basins with bias potential along a chosen collective variable." %}

Each method changes the path distribution. Equilibrium observables require the corresponding acceptance rule or reweighting relation, and the quality of a collective-variable method depends on whether its coordinates resolve the slow event.

For replica exchange, the swap is not accepted merely because it increases exploration. Swapping configurations $$x_i,x_j$$ between inverse temperatures $$\beta_i,\beta_j$$ uses

$$
\alpha=\min\left\{1,
\exp\left[(\beta_i-\beta_j)(U(x_i)-U(x_j))\right]
\right\}.
$$

This Metropolis correction preserves the joint product of canonical distributions. Temperature spacing controls the overlap of adjacent energy distributions: replicas that are too far apart almost never swap, while an unnecessarily dense ladder spends computation on redundant temperatures.

Metadynamics makes the correction problem more explicit. With a collective variable $$s(R)$$ and accumulated bias $$V_t(s)$$, the simulator evolves on $$U(R)+V_t(s(R))$$ rather than on $$U(R)$$. The bias accelerates escape, but the raw visitation histogram is not the unbiased equilibrium histogram. Reweighting introduces factors involving $$\exp[\beta V_t]$$, and its variance grows when important configurations receive extreme weights. A beautiful flattened landscape is useful only if the chosen coordinate resolves the event and the estimator accounts for the changed measure.

Annealing, replica exchange, and metadynamics therefore occupy different positions. Annealing uses a schedule and is often an optimization or initialization device. Replica exchange preserves equilibrium at each temperature through explicit swap acceptance. Metadynamics deliberately builds a time-dependent bias and later reconstructs free energies under assumptions about bias deposition and convergence. Calling all three “faster MD” hides the distinct statistical contracts.

## Neural acceleration in space and time

### Learned coarse-grained force fields

Neural coarse-grained MD retains the spatial reduction but learns the mapping or effective force field from atomistic data. The aim is to preserve more of the chemistry than a fixed hand-designed bead model while benefiting from fewer degrees of freedom.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s12-neural-coarse-graining.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Neural pipelines for transferable coarse-graining and generative backmapping of proteins." caption="Neural coarse-graining learns reduced interactions and, in some methods, a distribution over compatible atomistic backmaps. See [chemically transferable generative backmapping](https://openreview.net/forum?id=7DnvWyVkUo)." %}

A coarse state generally corresponds to many atomistic configurations. Backmapping is therefore a conditional generative problem, not a deterministic inverse. A model can be chemically transferable only to the extent that its training data constrain both the reduced dynamics and the unresolved atomistic distribution.

Learning the coarse force field and learning the backmapper are separate tasks. Force matching fits a reduced force so that coarse trajectories reproduce an atomistic mean force under the mapping. Backmapping fills in the conditional atomistic distribution after a coarse state has been generated. A good decoder cannot repair a coarse trajectory that visits the wrong states, and a good coarse equilibrium model does not ensure that decoded atomistic frames form a dynamically consistent path.

This division is visible in the neural pipeline. The encoder or mapping defines which atoms become beads. A reduced model evolves those beads. A generative decoder then samples unresolved coordinates. Chemical transfer requires the model to handle bead environments and residue types not memorized from one protein. Physical validity additionally requires local bond geometry and nonlocal steric consistency. Reconstruction error alone favors the conditional mean, which can be a structure that no atomistic configuration actually adopts.

### Learning a long-lag transition

Ordinary integration predicts an infinitesimal drift and repeatedly applies it at femtosecond resolution. Temporal coarse-graining instead learns

$$
p(x_{t+\tau}\mid x_t)
$$

for a much larger lag $$\tau$$. The lecture contrasts femtosecond-scale simulation with direct prediction over nanosecond-scale intervals.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s13-timewarp.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Comparison of ordinary molecular dynamics with a time-warped long-lag sampler." caption="A long-lag proposal can replace many sequential MD updates while an acceptance step preserves the equilibrium target, as in [Timewarp](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/a598c367280f9054434fdcc227ce4d38-Abstract-Conference.html)." %}

The output must be a distribution because a distant state is not determined uniquely by the current coordinates. Distinct microscopic paths can leave the same starting basin, and thermal noise makes their endpoints random.

[Timewarp](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/a598c367280f9054434fdcc227ce4d38-Abstract-Conference.html) makes an important distinction: its learned normalizing flow proposes a large move, but a Metropolis--Hastings step decides whether the chain accepts it. If the current state is $$x$$, a proposal $$y\sim q_\theta(y\mid x)$$ targets the Boltzmann density $$\pi(x)\propto e^{-\beta U(x)}$$ through

$$
\alpha(x,y)=\min\left\{1,
\frac{\pi(y)q_\theta(x\mid y)}
{\pi(x)q_\theta(y\mid x)}
\right\}.
$$

The proposal can be imperfect without biasing the stationary distribution, provided the ratio is evaluated correctly and the chain mixes. Poor proposals still fail operationally through low acceptance or weak movement. This is not the same as claiming that the generated jump follows the physical-time transition kernel. Timewarp is primarily an equilibrium sampling accelerator; the acceptance rule protects equilibrium statistics, not the literal kinetics of a $$10^5$$--$$10^6\,\mathrm{fs}$$ molecular path.

Long-lag learning also changes the generalization question. An infinitesimal force model can be reused at many step counts, whereas a kernel learned at lag $$\tau$$ is calibrated to a particular temporal resolution. Repeated application asks whether the learned operator remains consistent under composition: does two model steps resemble the physical kernel at $$2\tau$$? A model can match single-step endpoints but drift under repeated rollout if this semigroup relation fails.

### MDGen: trajectory generation as video generation

MDGen trains a video-style generative model on trajectories sampled at a coarser timescale. Non-autoregressive diffusion can generate several frames jointly, fill in missing intervals, and extrapolate trajectories.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s14-mdgen-evaluation.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="MDGen generated molecular paths, state populations, and kinetic comparisons." caption="[MDGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17808) compares generated paths through structural states, free-energy surfaces, and trajectory-level statistics." %}

Calling this “video generation” clarifies both the power and the boundary. Joint generation can avoid compounding one-step errors, but a visually plausible sequence is not automatically a physical-time process. State populations, transition statistics, and lag consistency must be evaluated separately.

The conditioning pattern defines the task. Conditioning on the first frame asks for forward simulation. Conditioning on both endpoints asks for a transition bridge. Hiding intermediate frames asks for temporal interpolation or upsampling. Hiding part of the molecular system asks for inpainting. These are distributions with different information sets, even when one network parameterizes all of them.

That flexibility is the appeal of [MDGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17808), but it also makes evaluation task-specific. Forward trajectories should reproduce metastable populations, transition counts, and time correlations. Bridges should be diverse conditional paths rather than copies of a preferred route. Upsampling should agree with the high-frequency motion that was omitted. Inpainting should respect interactions between the observed and generated atoms. A single coordinate RMSD cannot certify all four.

There is also a clean marginal-versus-path counterexample. A generator could sample every frame independently from the exact equilibrium distribution. Every one-frame structural histogram would be perfect, yet velocities, transition paths, autocorrelations, and diffusivity would be meaningless. Conversely, a smooth trajectory can have realistic local motion while spending the wrong fraction of time in each basin. Molecular video quality is not a surrogate for either thermodynamics or kinetics.

### Accelerated atomic transport

The same long-lag idea can estimate lithium diffusivity in solid-state electrolyte candidates. A flow-matching model generates atomistic transport over multiple timesteps, with predictor and corrector components, and is tested for transfer across composition, thermal condition, and supercell size.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s15-liflow.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Flow-matching predictor-corrector method for long-lag lithium transport and transfer tests." caption="[LiFlow](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-025-01125-4) advances an atomic displacement distribution with a propagator and corrects local geometries with a second model." %}

The scientific target remains diffusivity, not merely endpoint reconstruction. The generated displacements must reproduce the long-time mean-squared-displacement slope under the conditions where the coefficient is reported.

[LiFlow](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-025-01125-4) formulates the long step as conditional generation of atomic displacements. Its propagator proposes displacement over the coarse interval, while a corrector repairs locally implausible geometry. An adaptive Maxwell--Boltzmann prior conditions the displacement scale on chemical and thermal context. This structure reflects the application: lithium ions may travel far enough to determine transport, while the host lattice must remain locally physical.

Transfer across composition, temperature, and supercell size is not one claim. Temperature changes both the displacement distribution and the activated hopping rate. Composition changes the energy landscape. A larger cell introduces longer-wavelength structure and more simultaneous mobile ions. Reporting performance on each axis separately reveals whether the model learned a transport mechanism or merely interpolated a fixed benchmark geometry.

If a generated trajectory yields $$\operatorname{MSD}(t)$$, the predicted diffusivity follows from the same long-time slope as ordinary MD. That common estimator is valuable: it compares the accelerated and reference processes on a physical observable. Agreement at one short lag, however, does not guarantee the asymptotic slope, so the evaluation interval must reach the diffusive regime rather than stop in the initial vibrational or caged regime.

### Neural biased molecular dynamics

The final approach keeps an MD simulator but learns an additional drift that guides the trajectory toward a desired rare event. If $$b_\theta(R)$$ is a learned bias potential, the acceleration uses

$$
dV_t=
-\frac{\nabla\!\left[U(R_t)+b_\theta(R_t)\right]}{m}\,dt
-\gamma V_t\,dt
+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma k_{\mathrm B}T}{m}}\,dW_t.
$$

The neural bias reshapes the landscape so that transitions between states $$A$$ and $$B$$ occur more readily.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="assets/img/blog/lectures/ml4mol-2025-08/s16-transition-path-molecule.gif" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Animated molecular transition path between two conformations." caption="[TPS-DPS](https://openreview.net/forum?id=WQV9kB1qSU) trains a diffusion path sampler to generate transition paths between specified metastable states without a hand-designed collective variable." %}

Here the generated object is a path conditioned on endpoints, not an unconstrained equilibrium trajectory. For a discretized path $$\tau=(R_0,\ldots,R_T)$$, the unbiased path density contains the initial density and every simulator transition,

$$
p(\tau)=p_0(R_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}p(R_{t+1}\mid R_t).
$$

Adding a control force replaces this with a biased density $$q_\theta(\tau)$$ that visits the rare endpoint more often. The scientific problem is then to learn useful control while retaining the ability to relate generated paths to the intended transition-path distribution. Treating the bias as if it were part of the original molecular potential would erase that distinction.

[TPS-DPS](https://openreview.net/forum?id=WQV9kB1qSU) amortizes this path sampling with a diffusion path sampler and improves training through control variates, replay, and off-policy exploration. Its motivation matches the lecture's final comparison: avoid committing the entire method to a hand-designed collective variable while still targeting transitions between specified metastable states. The learned scale-based equivariant bias respects geometric transformation laws, but symmetry alone does not make a path statistically correct. Endpoint satisfaction, path diversity, transition-state structure, and reweighted path likelihood remain separate checks.

The unbiased and biased animations make the tradeoff visible. The unbiased process has the correct natural waiting behavior but may not cross during an affordable run. The biased process crosses often enough to study mechanisms, but its raw event frequency is artificial. Acceleration is useful precisely because it changes what is frequently observed; inference is valid only when that change is included in the estimator.

This closes the lecture's progression. Classical coarse-graining removes spatial detail; enhanced sampling changes exploration; neural coarse-graining learns a reduced spatial model; temporal generative models skip over many small steps; neural biased MD preserves the simulator while changing its drift. All accelerate rare events, but they preserve different objects. Some target equilibrium averages, some target coarse structural dynamics, some target long-lag transition distributions, and some target reactive paths. The right method follows from which of those objects the scientific question actually needs.
